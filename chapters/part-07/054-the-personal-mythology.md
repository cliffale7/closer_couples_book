## The hook

A timeline is a list of what happened. A mythology is a plot. It wants a title, a theme, a few characters, something that pushed back, a turn you did not order, and a chapter still open on the desk. The antagonist can be a year. It can be an illness, a habit, a season of bad sleep. It does not have to be a villain with a name you can boo.

You will tell this in under 6 minutes. Your partner will hand the theme back in one sentence and ask which part is metaphor. They will not close the unfinished chapter, and they will not treat the story as a symptom.

## Why it might matter

People already tell their lives as stories, in families and in the versions they use to get through dinner. The told version tends to flatten into events in order. First this city, then that job, then you. Order is useful. It is also how a life starts to sound like a form. Giving the life a plot asks ruder, kinder questions. What was the story doing? What kept opposing it? Where did it turn? What is still unfinished, on purpose, in the teller's hands?

The mechanism is a one-page structure, not a required hero's journey and not a set of imported stages. A plain novel outline does that job as well as a legend does. Mythic language is optional. If you would rather say "a gatekeeper" than invent a beast, say gatekeeper. The motif for this chapter happens to be a beast in the margin. The beast is decoration. Your outline does not have to include one.

The partner's moves are small because interpretation is where this exercise goes wrong. Retell the theme in one sentence. Ask which part is metaphor. Accept the answer. A story can hold a hard year without becoming a diagnosis, and retelling it is not treatment. This chapter claims no therapeutic effect. It is a way of hearing the plot a person is currently using, including the part they refuse to resolve for your comfort.

## Before you begin

You need a page and, ideally, a timer. Oral, written, or a few drawn symbols with captions all count. If you are tired, the short form is title, twist, and unfinished chapter. Grief can be in the story. An antagonist can be a season. You do not have to make the season mean something redemptive before the timer ends.

Living people appear only in words you would say to their face, or as abstracted roles. "A gatekeeper." "A teacher who was kind once." "Someone I loved and will not characterize tonight." Casting a living person as a caricatured villain is out. If the only way you can tell the story is by burning someone, choose a different antagonist, a circumstance, an illness, a habit, a year.

You may pass, stop the timer, or leave the unfinished chapter as a title with no synopsis. The teller controls which chapter stays open. The partner does not interpret the myth clinically and does not supply the ending.

Agree that tragic is not a required genre. Comic, flat, odd, and half-finished are legal. You are also allowed to revise the role if you hear yourself locked in a part you cannot stand. "That came out more doomed than I mean. I am changing the theme before we continue." Good. The story is yours to retitle in the room.

## The experience

Fill a one-page outline. Keep each line short enough that the telling can survive contact with a clock.

Title. It can be plain. "Three Apartments and a Reputation." It can be plainer. "The Year I Became Easy to Schedule."

Theme. One sentence about what the story seems to be doing. Devotion to a craft. Flight from a reputation. Learning to stay in a room. You may not know yet. "Theme unclear, possibly stubbornness" is a real theme line.

Three characters. Ages of the self are allowed, and they are often the best cast. The 19-year-old who said yes too fast. The self who became the person everyone could count on. The person speaking, who does not yet have a final name. Other people enter as roles you would say aloud.

One antagonist. Circumstance, illness, year, habit, or a person held to the rule above. "A stretch of insomnia that edited my personality." "My habit of explaining a feeling until it dies." Both are antagonists. Neither needs a moral.

One twist. A turn the earlier chapters did not predict. A job that ended sideways. A friendship that carried more of the plot than the romance did. A success that removed a story you were enjoying. The twist can be quiet.

One unfinished chapter. A situation still in motion, or a question the story is not ready to answer. You will say it, and your partner will leave it unfinished. If they feel the itch to resolve it, that itch is theirs to sit on.

Set a timer for 6 minutes or less. Tell the outline as a story, not as a commentary on the outline. Present tense or past, legend voice or kitchen voice. Stop when the timer goes off, even if the ending is mid-sentence. Mid-sentence is a kind of honesty.

The listener then does exactly two things.

Retells the theme in one sentence. "What I heard is a story about someone trying to keep a craft alive through a year that scrambled their sleep." The teller may correct it. The correction wins.

Asks, "Which part is metaphor?" The teller answers. The insomnia may be literal and the lost pencil may be metaphor. The gatekeeper may be a real office and also a picture of caution. Take the answer as the map. Do not translate it into a symptom, a type, or a prediction.

Switch. If the first telling ran hot, the second teller may use the short form instead. That is not a lesser mythology. It is a fit mythology.

Close by reading the unfinished chapter titles once more, in a list, with no discussion of how they ought to come out. The list is the closing ritual. It keeps the partner's hope from sealing the book.

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** Title, twist, and unfinished chapter, told in a few minutes each. The listener still reflects a theme, even a provisional one, and still asks what is metaphor. This is the right version on a depleted day, and it is a valid telling.

**40 minutes.** The full one-page outline, a telling under 6 minutes each, the theme sentence, the metaphor question, and the closing list of unfinished titles. This is the standard plot.

**75 minutes.** Write the outline as a tiny novel sketch, then tell it without reading every line, so the spoken version can breathe. After the metaphor question, you may revise the title once. You may not revise the other person's unfinished chapter. Extra time can also hold a second, shorter telling in a different genre, the same facts as a comedy, if the teller wants to see what the facts do in a lighter coat. Only if they want it.

## Going deeper

Ask one more question, if invited: "Who is telling this, the age in the story or the person in the chair?" Sometimes the theme belongs to an earlier self, and the person in the chair has a different one trying to get a word in. If both themes are true, write both. Do not force a single moral.

You may mark, in the margin, one scene you would cut if the story were a novel and you were a ruthless editor. Cutting is not betrayal. It shows you which episodes are doing thematic work and which are only chronological. Chronology has its own chapter.

A later session can take the unfinished chapter and retitle it without advancing it. Titles are cheap and reversible. Endings, in this exercise, are not required.

## Variations and replay

By letter, send the outline and ask for the two moves back: one sentence of theme, one question about metaphor. No critical essay.

Drawn symbols with captions are a full version. A square for an apartment, a dark bar for the insomniac year, a question mark for the open chapter. Explain the marks in words so the listener is not left to psychoanalyze a doodle.

Cultural variety is already built in, because no quest stages are required. Tell it as a family anecdote, a fable, a case note, a comic strip in six panels. If mythic language feels like a costume, take it off.

If speech is hard, read the outline aloud as notes and let that count as the telling. If the timer is stressful, use a rough sense of 6 minutes and stop when the unfinished chapter has been named. The timer serves the shortness. It is not a test.

006, The Museum of Me, tours objects, people, songs, and events as exhibits. 002, The Life You’ve Never Told Me, is one episode in detail. 077, The Relationship Timeline, maps the two of you in time. This chapter is a plotted story of a single life. Replay it when the twist looks different, or when an unfinished chapter has changed its title. Keep the old outline. Then versus now is the point of saving it.

## Examples

Fictional. This mythology belongs to Vale, with Rowan listening. It is an illustration of tone, not a template you have to match, and not a reading of anyone's life.

Title: "Low Maintenance."

Theme: being easy to keep around did the work of a personality for a long time, and the person telling the story has not decided which rooms still deserve that ease.

Characters: the 25-year-old who got praised for answering every message the same day. The friend who shows up with soup and does not require a speech. The person at the table, fond of the reputation and tired of it.

Antagonist: a habit of explaining a want until the want leaves, and a dull year of bad sleep. No relative is cast as a monster.

Twist: a job that was supposed to last a season lasted years, because the people in charge loved someone who did not make a fuss. The ease that felt like kindness became the plot.

Unfinished chapter: "Which rooms, if any, still deserve the easy version." Vale stops. The chapter stays open.

Rowan retells: "I heard a story about a reputation for being low-friction, useful enough to trap the person inside it, with the question of where to keep that ease still open." Vale corrects one word. "Not trap. Delay. I am not ready for trap." Rowan takes the correction.

Rowan asks which part is metaphor. Vale says the bad sleep was literal, "low maintenance" is a phrase people actually used, and the soup-bringer is a real neighbor described kindly. Rowan does not turn the bad sleep into a symbol. Rowan does not write the ending in which Vale becomes difficult on purpose. The unfinished chapter remains a question.

Rowan's own telling, after, uses only a title, a twist, and an open chapter, because the first story took the air. That choice fits the rules. A mythology does not have to be epic to be one.

## Afterward

What did the plot make visible that a timeline would have left in order?
Did the theme sentence survive contact with the teller, and where did it need a correction?
Which part was metaphor, and did the listener leave the literal parts literal?
What did it take to keep the unfinished chapter unfinished?

You can love a person's story and still not be its editor. The open chapter is not a problem you failed to solve.

## Our Story

Date the session and write the titles down, even if you write nothing else. Personal pages can hold the details you would not put in a story told at a dinner. They are optional to share. The shared page is a good place for the corrected theme sentence, the version you both agree was heard.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- My title, my theme, and the chapter I left unfinished
- Which part was metaphor, in my own words
partner_b:
- The antagonist in my story, named as a circumstance, a season, a habit, or a role I would say aloud
- The sentence my partner used for my theme, and the correction I made if it missed
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```

Reimagined from the ordinary custom of telling a life as a story. The chapter asks for a plot: theme, characters, antagonist, twist, and an unfinished chapter. A plain outline is enough.
