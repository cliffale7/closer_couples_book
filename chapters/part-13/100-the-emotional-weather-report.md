## The hook

"How was your day?" is a kind question that often wants a verdict. Fine. Long. Don't ask. A forecast is ruder and more accurate. You name the sky you are actually under, including the ridiculous sky, and the other person is allowed to look at it without becoming the meteorological service.

The weatherperson may add one optional line: what could help tomorrow. That line stays in their hands. They can arrange the help themselves. They can make an explicit request. They do not hand the other person the climate and call it love.

## Why it might matter

Days get summarized into a word, and the word invites a manager. Fine gets a nod. Bad gets a fix. A forecast asks for a description: condition, direction, region. Description gives the listener something to stand next to. It does not appoint them.

The mechanism is the split between weather and labor. "Tomorrow would go better if I stepped outside once. I can do that myself" is a complete kindness to the future, and it leaves the partner unemployed by the metaphor. "Company in the kitchen would help, and I will ask if I want it" keeps the request voluntary and specific. A partner can be company. A partner cannot be the sky.

This ritual was formulated for this project. Mood metaphors are common in classrooms and in other people's kitchens. They are not a treatment, and naming a feeling is not claimed to change a brain or a forecast. Wit is optional. A plain sentence is the same ritual.

## Before you begin

You can do this at the sink, on a walk, or in two lines of text. No one has to forecast every day. "No forecast tonight" is a complete answer. Then talk the way you already talk.

Pick one region so the sky stays local: work, body, the relationship, or the private interior. A forecast of every system on earth is a speech. One region is a report.

Three agreements:

The listener may ask where it is heaviest, or whether it is passing. Advice waits until it is requested.
Tomorrow's help is spoken by the weatherperson. It is not assigned to the partner by default.
A forecast that is really a coded complaint deserves a plain sentence instead. You can still name the relationship as the region. Say your weather. If you want a change from the other person, call it a request, out loud, and let them answer as a person.

If the report is despair, panic, or danger, treat it as a real signal. Step out of the metaphor. Stay with them, and bring in outside help when safety is in question. A poem is the wrong tool for an emergency.

Skip thunderclap theater if loud weather play is too much. The ritual fits fatigue because it is short.

## The experience

The weatherperson gives three beats, and a fourth only if they want it.

Region. One word or one phrase.
Condition, and the direction of change. Clearing, steady, incoming, stuck, a break and then more.
Optional: what could help tomorrow, and who will carry it. Use one of these shapes. "A help I can arrange is ___." "A help I am actually requesting is ___." "I am requesting nothing."

Then stop. The listener does not hand over an umbrella in the form of a plan.

Two sample forecasts, written for this chapter. Use them as patterns, not as scripts you have to earn.

Playful: "Region, work. Late fog, a small break of sun after lunch, chance of existential drizzle tonight. Tomorrow would go better if I stepped outside once. I can do that myself."

Serious: "Region, body. Steady rain since the appointment. I don't want ideas. Company in the kitchen would help, and I will ask if I want it."

A request, when there is one, is specific enough to accept or decline. "I am asking you to sit in the kitchen while I heat soup. You don't have to talk." That is different from "You should fix my rain." The first can be declined, and the decline is not a weather failure. The second was never a fair job.

Listener questions that stay inside the ritual:

Where is it heaviest right now?
Is it passing, or is it the kind that sits?
Do you want a question, or is the report finished?

Questions that sneak a fix, such as "Have you tried," wait for a yes.

If you dislike metaphor, use the same beats in plain language, and treat that path as equal:

Right now I am ___.
It may last ___.
A help I can arrange is ___.
A help I am actually requesting is ___ / none.

Both of you may report. The two forecasts do not have to merge. Two weather systems can share a coast. A combined outlook is optional and usually unnecessary on a weeknight.

If you keep a log, the columns are date, region, forecast, help I will arrange for myself, and anything I am explicitly asking. The asking column is often blank. The log is a memory, not a compliance chart. Missing days are none of the log's business.

## Three ways to play

**5 minutes.** One person, one region, condition and direction. The tomorrow line only if it is already obvious. The listener asks at most one question, or none. "No forecast" counts as played.

**10 minutes.** Both people report their own sky. Each may add the help line, including "nothing requested." The listener reflects the condition in a few words if that helps, and does not problem-solve. This is the version that replaces "How was your day?" on an ordinary evening.

**15 minutes.** Both forecasts, plus the log line written down, plus one optional combined outlook only if you both want it. Use the extra minutes when the weather is real and you want the request, if any, to be unmistakable. Still one region each. A second region is another day, or a sentence you add because it will not stay quiet, not a tour of the whole climate.

## Going deeper

Once a week, if you like the ritual, read the log backward and notice only this: which help you arranged for yourself, and which explicit request was actually answered, declined, or never made. Skip the scoring. A kind forecast is one you can reread without wincing at your own drama or at a job you quietly gave away.

You can name a micro-climate inside a good day. Sun with a pocket of hail is a legal report. Performing cheer is optional and usually detectable. If the true condition is mixed, say mixed.

A further question, only when the weatherperson invites it: "Is any of this mine to know overnight, or can it stay yours?" Some reports are for company. Some are for the record of having said them. The difference is theirs to draw.

## Variations and replay

By text, two lines: region and condition, then the help line or "no request." The reply can be "Heard" plus, if wanted, the listener's own forecast. Advice by message is still advice.

If speech is tiring, point at a picture of a sky, then add one sentence of the plain-language path. If metaphor is tiring, stay with the four blanks and skip the rain entirely. The motif is a small square of sky with an empty line under tomorrow. You do not have to draw it.

Replay whenever "How was your day?" has gone numb, or whenever you notice you have been fixing each other out of habit. You can also use it in the morning, as a departure forecast, with the same rule: tomorrow's help is not a task dropped on the way out the door.

This report stays brief, and solving is off the job description. 088, The Thing I’m Still Figuring Out, is for an unresolved question you are willing to share. 091, What We Want More Of, looks at appetite over time. A weather report is a snapshot. It can mention a want. It does not negotiate the whole menu.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan, at the counter: "Region, work. Late fog, a break of sun after lunch, chance of existential drizzle tonight. Tomorrow would go better if I stepped outside once. I can do that myself. I am not asking you to be the outdoors."

Vale asks, "Is the fog passing?" Rowan says, "Mostly. The drizzle is a meeting I keep rewriting." Vale nods and does not offer a script for the meeting.

Vale's own report does not match the metaphor and does not have to. "Region, body. Steady rain since the appointment. Right now I am wrung out. It may last through tomorrow morning. A help I can arrange is going to bed when the dishes are done, even if the dishes are mediocre. A help I am actually requesting is none. Company in the kitchen would help, and I will ask if I want it. Tonight the request line is blank."

Rowan says, "Heard." They do not write a combined outlook. Two systems, one coast. In the log, Rowan's asking column is blank. Vale's is blank. The blank is the point.

On another night Vale does make a request. "I am asking you to sit in the room while I answer that message. You don't have to draft it." Rowan can say yes or not tonight. The rain stays Vale's.

## Afterward

This debrief can be 1 minute, or it can be silence.

Did the report describe weather, or did it assign a job?
Was tomorrow's help arranged by the person who owns it, requested outright, or left blank on purpose?
Did the listener try to clear the sky?
If you are keeping a log, is the asking column honest, including the blanks?
Do you want this ritual tomorrow, or is "no forecast" the true forecast?

You do not owe each other better weather. You can owe a clear request when you actually have one, and you can survive the evening without one.

## Our Story

Date the session if you write it down. Note the version and whether you finished or waved it off. Mood is optional. A blank day in a log is allowed.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together, and only if you want a combined page at all.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- Today's inner weather: the front, the pressure, and, if you want it, what might help tomorrow.
- The help you will arrange for yourself, and anything you are explicitly asking. Often the second line is none.
partner_b:
- Your forecast, drawn from your own sky. The two forecasts do not have to merge.
- A help you can arrange, and a help you are actually requesting, or none.
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- Write a combined outlook only if you want one. Two weather systems can share a coast.
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
- What the next day actually brought, and whether the forecast was kind.
```

## Notes

No research citation is claimed. Mood metaphors and emotional check-ins are common. They are not a validated protocol for couples, and this chapter does not claim that naming a feeling changes the brain or the weather. Formulated for this project. The partner is company only by request. A forecast of despair, panic, or danger is a real signal, not a figure of speech. The log is not a compliance chart.
