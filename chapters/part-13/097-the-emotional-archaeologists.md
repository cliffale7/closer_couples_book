## The hook

Start from a strong feeling whose first cause may change. Annoyance at the dishes. Pride at the news. Jealousy at a glance. The caption glued to it may be right. It may also be the sentence that was fastest, the one that kept the moment from having to be looked at twice.

This hour is a dig with two roles and one feeling. The explorer chooses the reaction and says, in a single sentence, what they assume caused it. The archaeologist asks a short sequence of questions, reflects the words they actually heard, and withholds the clever theory. Partway through, the first cause may stop fitting. The explorer is the person who gets to notice that. The archaeologist does not diagnose a better one and hand it over.

## Why it might matter

The same heat, the same flush, the same slump, and the household already has a story ready. Official stories save time. They also hide the layer that was skipped because it was awkward, tender, or simply slower. The explorer is allowed to leave with a different cause than the one they walked in with.

The mechanism is narrow. Hearing your own account reflected, without an upgrade, lets you test whether the cause still holds. Being explained feels like help and often functions as a lid. Being accurately repeated feels different. You get to say "close," or "no, the heat was earlier than that," or "the cause I walked in with is the wrong object."

A need may show up under the feeling: company, fairness, a witness, a limit, a little glory that wants to be seen. Treat that need as a hypothesis. The explorer may keep it, revise it, or set it down. It is not a work order for the partner, and it is not a find the archaeologist gets to name.

This shape was formulated for this project. Nothing here claims a discovery, a treatment, or a guarantee that one sitting will reach a buried truth. Sometimes the first cause survives the dig. That is a finished result.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can hear each other. Phones facedown. Paper or a notes app, so the opening sentence can be compared with the closing one. A timer helps the dig stay a dig.

Choose who explores first. One reaction only: joy, jealousy, embarrassment, pride, sadness, or a close cousin. Recent enough to remember, small enough to hold tonight. If the feeling that wants the floor is panic, or if the hour is a crisis or anything involving abuse, leave the roles. Respond as a partner. This frame has no business managing danger.

Jealousy and anger are allowed as self-observation. They are a poor courtroom. If what you actually need is a direct complaint about something the other person did, say that in ordinary language, or schedule it. Do not bury a case inside a dig and hope the questions will prosecute it.

Three agreements, said once:

You may pass, pause, or trade the reaction for a milder one.
A stop word ends the dig immediately. Pick a word that will not appear by accident. "Shelf" works. So does "red pen."
The explorer speaks the last sentence, which may keep, revise, or abandon the original cause. The archaeologist does not announce the revision.

No lying down, dimmed lights, or touch is required. Long pauses count as answers in progress.

## The experience

Take 3 minutes. The explorer writes one sentence: the reaction, and the cause they are walking in with. Read it aloud. The archaeologist copies it, or lets the explorer keep the paper in view. That sentence is the ground level. You will want it later, when memory tries to pretend you began somewhere wiser.

Agree the stop word again, out loud.

The archaeologist asks one question at a time, in roughly this order, and may skip a layer the explorer does not want:

1. What happened just before?
2. What did your body do?
3. What meaning arrived first?
4. What else might be true?
5. What might the feeling be protecting, or wanting?

After each answer, reflect words, not a theory. Use the explorer's nouns. Then ask, "Is that close?"

A clean reflection sounds like this: "Hot face, plates that were already stacked, and a meaning that you were too much at the table. Is that close?"

If the explorer says no, repeat their correction and stop improving it. If they say "partly," ask which half. If they go quiet, wait. Offer the stop word rather than a better question.

Leave these moves unused. They end the dig even when they sound kind:

A diagnostic label.
"So you're really just..."
A summary cleverer than the sentence the explorer just said.
A dig into a feeling they only mentioned as an example.
A turn that starts gathering evidence about the partner.

When the explorer notices the cause moving, let them be the one to say so. You will hear it in ordinary language. "That isn't the dishes." "The pride is real, and the audience I wanted was smaller than the one I named." The archaeologist's next line is still a reflection, plus "Is that close?" The archaeologist does not unveil the true feeling.

The last minute belongs to the explorer. They look at the opening sentence and say one of three things, in their own words:

Keep. The cause I walked in with still holds.
Revise. Here is the cause I would write now. The first one was a guess, and I am marking it as a guess.
Abandon. I do not have a replacement, and I am done looking tonight.

If a need showed up, they may add it as a hypothesis: "Under this I think I wanted a witness. I might be wrong." The archaeologist thanks them and does not assign the need to anyone. Close there. A second feeling is a different day, unless you are both genuinely fresh and the first dig already ended with a thank-you.

15 minutes and one layer is a complete short path. Stopping at "what my body did" can be the whole session.

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** One reaction. Write the opening cause. Ask only the first question that has any life in it, reflect it, and ask "Is that close?" The explorer says keep, revise, or abandon. One layer is a finished dig.

**40 minutes.** The full sequence, still one reaction. Pauses included. The closing sentence gets a full minute, and the opening sentence stays visible so a revision is a revision, not a foggy memory of having always known.

**50 minutes.** The explorer writes the five answers first, slowly, in silence. Then they read, and the archaeologist reflects only what was read, with "Is that close?" after each layer they choose to keep. Use this when speech is costly, when you are in different rooms, or when a voiced theory would be too tempting. The extra time is for pace. It is not an invitation to excavate a second site.

## Going deeper

On a later day, bring the hypothesis back beside a fresh example of a similar reaction. Ask only whether it still fits. A hypothesis that fails is useful. Write the failure down. Do not promote it into a personality.

You can add one question, only if the explorer invites it: "Where does this show up on an ordinary Tuesday, in a smaller size?" That keeps the dig from living only in dramatic weather. The archaeologist may notice a pattern. They offer it as a sighting, one sentence, and the explorer decides whether it belongs in the closing line.

Leave the partner's motive out of the deeper pass. If the explorer wants to know why the other person did something, that is a different conversation, with both people allowed to answer for themselves.

## Variations and replay

By letter, the explorer writes the five layers and the closing sentence. The reply is a reflection and "Is that close?" sent after the letter is read. No theory in the margin.

If writing is tiring, say the opening cause and let the archaeologist jot the exact words. If speech is tiring, stay on the page and trade the paper. The motif for this chapter is a cut bank of earth, one stratum labeled in the explorer's hand. A private sketch is optional.

Replay when a familiar reaction returns and the old caption feels automatic. Date both sentences, the first cause and the last, so a later dig has something to meet. The same heat, a year on, is often a different object wearing the same name.

010, The Fear Beneath the Fear, stays with successive layers under a fear. This dig can start from joy, pride, jealousy, embarrassment, or sadness, and the assumed cause is allowed to move while you watch. 087, The Question Behind the Question, starts from an ordinary answer and follows it. 020, The Listening Experiment, gives one person a longer uninterrupted turn and a reflection at the end. Different tools. Leave them different.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan chooses embarrassment from a dinner last week and writes: "I got quiet because the story about my old boss ran too long, and I was ashamed of talking so much."

Vale asks what happened just before. Rowan says the question was ordinary. Vale had asked how the reorganization was going. Rowan started talking, then heard their own voice speed up. The body layer: face hot, hands stacking plates that were already stacked. The first meaning: "I am too much at this table."

Vale reflects: "Hot face, plates that were already stacked, and a meaning that you were too much. Is that close?"

Rowan says, "Close. The too-much sentence is old. What else might be true is that I wanted you to want the next part, and I got scared you were being polite."

Vale does not say that this is really about work, or about a parent, or about anxiety. Vale asks what the feeling might be wanting. Rowan says, "A sign you actually wanted the rest. I didn't check. I supplied the shame instead."

Rowan's last sentence: "The cause I walked in with was half right about the shame and wrong about the source. I was embarrassed that I wanted interest and didn't ask. I'm keeping that revision, and I'm marking the first cause as a guess. I am not asking you to prove interest on a schedule."

Vale says, "Thank you for the dig," and stops.

Vale's own turn, on another night, is pride after a small public thanks at work. The opening cause is "I was proud because someone noticed." Midway, Vale hears themselves say that the pride got sharp when they imagined Rowan hearing a shorter version and thinking it was nothing. Vale revises: the pride was real, and under it was a wish to be believed at home without performing the whole story. Vale names that as a hypothesis. Rowan, now the archaeologist, repeats the hypothesis and does not accept a job.

## Afterward

Sit for a minute before you tidy the dig into a lesson.

What was the opening cause, in the sentence you actually wrote?
Did it keep, move, or get set down, and who was the person who said so?
Where did the archaeologist want to offer a better theory?
If a need appeared, did it stay a hypothesis, or did it turn into an assignment?
Do you want this reaction left alone for a while?

You do not owe each other a cleaner emotion. You can keep the two sentences, the first and the last, and you can keep them accurate.

## Our Story

Date the session. Note the version, the place, and whether you finished or paused. Initials and mood are optional. Write what you are willing to reread.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together. If you were the archaeologist, your page is still about your own reaction when you take a turn. It does not excavate the other person.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- A strong reaction you investigated, layer by layer, and the need under it if you found one. Mark a guess as a guess.
- Stop before you invent a motive. If the first cause moved, say what it moved to, in your words.
partner_b:
- Your reaction, your layers, your pace. This page does not excavate the other person.
- What you kept, revised, or left alone about your own first cause.
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- The need you think was underneath, written as a hypothesis you are allowed to revise
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
- Did the hypothesis hold the next time a similar reaction appeared?
```

## Notes

Formulated for this project: one chosen reaction, layer-by-layer questions, and a rule that the explorer, not the archaeologist, announces any change in the assumed cause. That is a provenance note. It is not a claim of worldwide novelty, and it is not a validated method. See L1998. In diary studies of daily interactions, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator, and in Study 2 emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. See R1988 for the model behind that finding: disclosure, response, and the feeling of being understood. Both support a listener who reflects. Neither tested this dig, and neither shows that a buried need can be reliably excavated in one sitting. The need stays a hypothesis the explorer may decline. If the hour meets abuse, panic, or a crisis, leave the roles and respond as a partner.
