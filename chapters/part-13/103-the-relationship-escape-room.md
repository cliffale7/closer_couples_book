## The hook

Three evenings got misfiled, and the irritating part is that each of you saw a different slice of the night. One of you knows when the toast happens. The other knows which evening is the walk. Neither packet is the whole ledger. You sit in one agreed place, with the real door unlocked, and you rebuild the page by saying your clues out loud.

The title is a puzzle evening at a table. You are restoring a ledger, not trying to get out of a locked place. The win is a page you write together. There is no rank and no forfeit. Opening the door, skipping a lock, or stopping for the night is part of the game.

## Why it might matter

Most couple games hand both people the same card and ask who remembers faster. This one splits the information on purpose. The mechanism is the join. A placement becomes legal only when your clue and their clue can share a sentence. Neither person has the complete information. The clues require joining each other's knowledge. Solving it silently and announcing the grid misses the only interesting part.

Parlor mysteries, logic grids, and two-handed clue games are folk forms. This starter was written for this project and solved on paper. It has not been live-playtested with a couple. It is not a commercial room, a kit, or a treatment. No outcome is claimed. The pleasure on offer is a restored page and a few private locks you may leave closed.

## Before you begin

Choose one place and stay there. A table, a couch, a call with two private documents. The door of the actual room stays open. You may stand up, leave, or stop. Nobody is locked in. If you are playing by call, "the door stays open" means either person can end the call without finishing the grid.

Choose North and South by preference. Puzzle pride is a bad way to assign the seats. Gender is irrelevant to the packets.

Materials: two envelopes labeled North and South; the shared reference card printed or copied where both of you can see it; Packet North only in North's envelope; Packet South only in South's envelope; the blank grid on the table; a pencil; scrap paper; one copy of `journal/templates/escape-clue-sheet.md` per puzzle if you want the record. Phones facedown if you both agree. Color is not a clue. No darkness, no physical locks, no crawling, no audio shocks, and no moving through the house. Large print is welcome. If one person is the only reader, they read a single requested clue and stop. They do not dump the packet across the table.

Personal slips are optional. Blank slips are allowed. No sexual disclosure is required. A memory disagreement is a moment to mark the cell "uncertain," not a cross-examination. A partial page is a finished night.

## The experience

### What you both see before either packet is opened

Do this in order. Sit down. Leave both envelopes closed. Read the shared reference card aloud. Set it face up between you, in large enough print that each of you can see it without borrowing the other's paper. It stays face up for the whole game. It is not sealed inside a packet.

**Shared reference card. Both players read this aloud and leave it face up. Do not put it inside either envelope.**

**Hours are only 6, 8, and 10.** Evenings are Cedar, Harbor, and Lantern. Foods are oranges, soup, and bread. First moves are a question, a toast, and a walk. Each item is used once.

"Two hours" uses this clock and no other. The ledger has no hour 4 and no hour 12. Both players can see that closed set before North reads Packet North and before South reads Packet South. The packets do not contain this sentence. If you are printing, this card is its own sheet, outside the envelopes.

Then read the prologue aloud:

### What each person receives

Both of you receive, before any envelope is opened: the shared reference card, including the sentence "Hours are only 6, 8, and 10," the prologue, and the blank grid.

Each of you also receives two slips.

Place-slip. One shared place, a short phrase. Keep it in your own envelope. Do not show it.

Reason-slip. On the front, a food, song, or object you associate with your partner. On the back, why, in a sentence or two. Fold the back shut. Put this slip into your partner's envelope, not your own. You will be guessing a slip you did not write.

Blank slips are legal. If you use them, you can still finish the night. The sentence that closes a fully passed pair of locks lives in the covered facilitator section, because it names a restored row. You do not need that sentence to start.

North receives: the envelope labeled North; Packet North and no lines from Packet South; North's own place-slip; the reason-slip South wrote, back folded shut, which North does not open until the Cedar lock.

South receives: the envelope labeled South; Packet South and no lines from Packet North; South's own place-slip; the reason-slip North wrote, back folded shut, which South does not open until the Cedar lock.

### Packet North

Give this only to North, after the shared reference card is face up.

You know the toast's hour and Cedar's relation to the soup. You do not know which evening is the walk, or where the bread sits. The hours, evenings, foods, and first moves are on the shared card. Use that card. It is not reprinted here as hidden information.

- N1. The toast happens at 8.
- N2. Cedar begins two hours before the soup.
- N3. Harbor is not the walk.
- N4. Bread is not served at the earliest hour.

### Packet South

Give this only to South, after the shared reference card is face up.

You know which evening is the walk, where the bread sits, and that the question comes two hours before Harbor. You do not know the toast's hour. The hours, evenings, foods, and first moves are on the shared card. Use that card. It is not reprinted here as hidden information.

- S1. Lantern is the walk.
- S2. Bread is served at 10.
- S3. The question is asked two hours before Harbor.
- S4. Soup is not served at 10.

### How to build the grid

Read your own packet silently once. Then build the grid one placement at a time. Every placement is spoken with the clue number that supports it. "I think Harbor is at 8" is incomplete. "N1 puts the toast at 8" is a legal offer. Your partner answers with their own clue number, with "my packet doesn't have that," or with "my packet conflicts." A conflict means a misread. There is one grid. If a clue seems to fight the shared card, read the shared card again before you invent a second solution.

You may read a clue aloud. You may not hand your packet across during play. One person does not solve in silence and then announce the page. If you are stuck for several minutes on one cell, ask for a single clue to be reread. If you are still stuck, you may uncover one numbered step in the facilitator section, then cover it again. Uncovering the whole key retires the logic case. You may still play the locks.

When both of you agree the grid matches both packets, read it back once, citing the clue numbers in the last column. Then, and only then, open the locks. If you would rather stop at the grid, skip to the bypass described under the locks and end. A grid with the private locks unopened is a finished short game.

### The three locks

**Cedar lock.** The player holding the partner's reason-slip reads the front aloud and guesses why that food, song, or object was chosen. Then they may read the back. A wrong guess still opens the lock. Mocking the reason closes the night. Stop, thank each other, and leave the rest folded.

**Harbor lock.** Each player tries to name the other's place-slip. Up to three guesses. The holder answers only "yes" or "another try." After three misses the holder may read the slip or pass. A pass opens this bypass, which does not require the facilitator section: name any shared place both of you can picture. That named place stands in for the slip.

**Lantern lock.** Write one sentence that uses one true detail from either personal lock and the Cedar row of the grid you restored. If both personal locks were passed, use the bypass sentence in the facilitator section. Uncover only the line labeled Bypass, after the grid is done. If you would rather not open the key at all, write your own closing line: you restored the grid, and the private locks stayed closed. Your sentence is a complete ending. The printed bypass is there so a fully passed night still has an exact restoration if you want to check the Cedar row.

### Clue sheets

Use `journal/templates/escape-clue-sheet.md` for any puzzle you want in the journal, including this starter and any custom puzzle later. One sheet per puzzle. Baselines at least 0.32 inch apart.

Title, date, the place you agreed on, the attempt, and which puzzle of the night. Mark what it was built from: shared history, knowledge of each other, a skill.

Write the clue in your own words. Eight lines are there so you can include the stall.

Write how you solved it together, including the help and the moment it opened. If you left it unsolved, write that.

"What I brought" and "What you brought" have equal lines. The point is how the two skills met.

Mark whether you paused or opened the door, and the house rule you are keeping.

The sheet is a cooperative record. It does not send either of you out of the place you agreed on. Skipping a puzzle is part of the game.

### How to reset

Erase the grid. Refold the reason-slip backs. Return place-slips to their writers. Reason-slips are journaled or destroyed at the writer's choice. Once the facilitator key has been read in full, the logic case is retired for this pair. A later night reuses the lock procedure with new slips. Do not rename the evenings, hours, foods, or moves unless someone writes a new puzzle and test-solves a new key first. Shuffle nothing. This starter has no deck.

### How to customize

A second night can be all locks and no new logic: new place-slips, new reason-slips, same three-lock procedure, no renamed grid.

To write a new logic puzzle, give each person four clues, keep a closed set both players can see before the clues are read, use each item once, and test-solve it on paper before the date. Confirm there is one solution. Write the key on a separate sheet and keep it face down. If your solve produces two grids, the puzzle is not ready. The hours, or whatever closed set you invent, have to sit on a shared card, not only inside a sealed packet. Otherwise the first deduction is not available to both of you.

A personal puzzle uses the clue sheet and stays at the table. One person holds a memory. The other holds a skill, or a second memory. The puzzle is finished when you have joined them into one sentence, or into one small paper object you make where you are sitting. You do not leave the agreed place. The door stays open. Stopping is a result.

### Facilitator section: solution key

Cover this section before you play. If you are printing, start it on a new sheet and leave that sheet face down. Players use the shared reference card and the two packets. Open this key only if you are stuck and both agree to look at one numbered step, or after the grid is done and you want to check. Reading the whole key retires the logic case for this pair.

The first step uses Packet South's S3 together with the shared card both players could already see: hours are only 6, 8, and 10.

1. S3 allows question-at-6 with Harbor-at-8, or question-at-8 with Harbor-at-10. Those are the only pairs, because the shared card gives no other hours.
2. N1 puts the toast at 8, so the question cannot be at 8. The question is at 6. Harbor is at 8.
3. Harbor's move is the toast.
4. The remaining hour, 10, is the walk. S1 says Lantern is the walk, so Lantern is at 10.
5. Cedar is the remaining evening, at 6, and holds the question.
6. S2 puts bread at 10, so Lantern serves bread.
7. N2: Cedar at 6 means the soup is at 8. Harbor serves soup.
8. Oranges remain with Cedar.
9. N3, N4, and S4 confirm the grid. If one of them conflicts, a clue was misread. There is no second solution.

| Evening | Hour | Food | First move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cedar | 6 | oranges | a question |
| Harbor | 8 | soup | a toast |
| Lantern | 10 | bread | a walk |

**Bypass.** A complete solution when the private locks stay closed: "We restored Cedar, at 6, oranges, and a question, and we left the private locks closed."

Practice pair, fictional, for checking the locks. You do not have to use these slips. North is Rowan, place-slip "ferry," reason-slip front "ginger," back "Vale bought ginger chews for my sore throat and pretended they were ordinary." South is Vale, place-slip "library," reason-slip front "pretzel," back "Rowan broke a pretzel into the soup the night the stove failed." The logic key does not change. Rowan holds Vale's pretzel slip, because reason-slips go into the partner's envelope. Vale holds Rowan's ginger slip. Place-slips stay with the people who wrote them.

End of the facilitator section. Cover it again if you only needed one step.

## Three ways to play

**45 minutes.** Shared card, both packets, the grid, and the bypass if you want the private locks left closed. Cite clue numbers. Stop when the page is restored or when either of you is done. A partial grid, with the door opened, is a finished short game.

**75 minutes.** The full starter: grid, then Cedar, Harbor, and Lantern, including the right to pass a lock. Write one clue sheet for the ledger. Reset the slips the way the reset section says.

**120 minutes.** The starter, then one custom puzzle on a fresh clue sheet, built from a memory and a skill, test-solved if it is a logic grid, with its own covered key. Same place. Same open door. If the starter already filled the evening, save the custom puzzle. More puzzle is optional.

## Going deeper

After a clean grid, ask which clue you each could not have used alone. Write that pair of numbers on the clue sheet under "Who knew what." That is the join, and it is worth more than the speed.

You may add one house clue next time that only one of you understands, a private nickname for a place or a song, and see whether the other person can meet it without a packet. If it turns into a test of devotion, retire it. The ledger is play.

If you write a second logic case, keep the shared closed set on the table before the envelopes open. A beautiful clue that depends on a fact only one packet contains, and that the other player cannot see, will stall for the wrong reason.

## Variations and replay

Low energy: South reads S3, you both look at the shared card, and you fill only the hour column, then stop. Hours are only 6, 8, and 10, and that may be enough puzzle for a tired night.

Long-distance: two private documents and one call for the joint grid. The shared reference card is pasted at the top of both documents, above the packet, so both people see the hours before they read a clue. Still no handing the packet over as a single dump. Read one requested clue and stop.

Accessibility: one numbered clue at a time, large print, no color coding, seats you already use. The grid can be spoken and written by one pencil both of you can see.

Once this key has been read, retire the case. Replay the locks with new slips as often as you like. New logic wants a new test-solved key.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you. Read this after you play, or after you have decided the key can be seen. The deduction lives in the facilitator section. This scene is about the tone of the locks.

They have the shared card face up. Vale says, "S3, the question is two hours before Harbor. The card says hours are only 6, 8, and 10, so I can offer two pairs and I cannot pick yet." Rowan says, "N1, the toast happens at 8. Hold that next to your pairs." They keep going one clue at a time. Neither of them slides a packet across.

At the Cedar lock, Rowan holds Vale's slip, reads "pretzel," and guesses, "You think I eat them at my desk." The back says Rowan broke a pretzel into the soup the night the stove failed. The guess is wrong. The lock still opens. Rowan does not joke about the soup. Vale, holding "ginger," guesses a tea Rowan dislikes. The back is ginger chews for a sore throat, passed off as ordinary. Vale says, "I had the object and missed the kindness." That sentence is the lock working.

Harbor: Rowan guesses the library on the second try. Vale misses the ferry three times and passes. They name the kitchen table, a place both can picture, and the pass stands. Lantern is one sentence with the pretzel and the Cedar row. They write it on the clue sheet, erase the grid, and refold the backs.

## Afterward

Which placement moved only because both packets were in the room?
Where did you want to take the packet and finish it yourself?
Did a wrong personal guess still feel like an opening, or did it ask to become a score?
What did you leave closed on purpose?
If you opened the real door, or ended the call, what did that protect?

The restored page is play. It is not a verdict on who knows the relationship better.

## Our Story

Date the session, the place you agreed on, and whether you finished, paused, or opened the door. The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. One clue sheet per puzzle. Printed prompts want at least eight handwriting lines, baselines at least 0.32 inch apart.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- A puzzle you brought from shared history or from a skill of yours, and what you saw while the two of you worked it.
- The clue number you had to say aloud before the grid would move.
partner_b:
- A puzzle that needed what you know, and the moment cooperation mattered more than speed.
- Whether a private lock stayed closed, and the ending you used.
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- The story of the room you agreed on: where you stalled, how you helped, what you solved, and what you left with a smile. Nobody was locked in.
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
- What kind of puzzle you want to build next, and what you will leave out of it.
```

Original formulation for this project: a cooperative tabletop mystery in which each person holds different information, plus three optional personal locks. The grid and the key were derived for this book and checked on paper. That solve is editorial. It is not a live playtest, not a claim of worldwide novelty, and not evidence that puzzle-solving changes a relationship.

No empirical citation is claimed. Folk logic grids are a tradition. Commercial room names and their puzzles are not used here. The starter is original.
