## The hook

The wallet is the activity: why this person would return it, keep it, or walk it to a desk. The answer moves through a value, a fear or a hope, and some practical wall. This experiment asks you to borrow that movement.

A scenario is read. Each of you writes the choice you believe the other would make, and three beats of why. Then the person whose mind it is explains the choice they would actually make, and the reasons. A matched yes with a wrong reason scores below a missed yes with a grasped reason. The yes itself is worth zero. Differences are information. They are not a failed evening.

## Why it might matter

Stored trivia and borrowed reasoning are different skills. This hour scores the reasons, if you score at all, and the yes itself is worth zero. Being accurately imagined in your reasons feels different from being correctly labeled yes or no. A lucky label can still be a stranger wearing your answer.

The partner is the authority on their own mind. A score, if you use one, glorifies the reasons you could name. It does not measure love, and it does not measure empathic accuracy. No study is claimed for this game. If the points start to sting, drop them and keep the conversation.

[043](043), The Newlywed Game, Without the Wedding, asks you to match facts and preferences you have stored. [085](085), What Would You Do If…?, asks what you yourself would do. This chapter stays on the borrowed reason.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can both write before either of you speaks. A call works if the writing stays private until the reveal. Phones facedown unless one of them is the paper. Either person may reject a scenario before any reasoning is written. After three rejects, write a replacement together in four lines: who asks, what is at stake, what it costs your partner, and what it costs someone else. Leave sex, fidelity, and humiliation of a body or a culture out of the deck. A guessed betrayal or a guessed sexual refusal is a poor joke. Do not deal one.

Replace any scenario that assumes a job, a sibling, a car, money, or a household you do not have. A night class can become a friend's project. A car can become a bike or a tool. A parent can become an older relative who still visits. The funny ones have to stay funny for the person inside them.

Agreements, said once:

You are answering as your partner, with the facts on the card. You may add a constraint you know is true ("you don't drive at night") and you name it as one of the three beats.
The person being imagined may correct the reason. They may not be outvoted about their own values.
A warm miss costs nothing. You ask, "What value did I miss?" and you write the answer down.
You do not bring a miss into tomorrow as a grievance.
Passing a scenario is a complete turn.

## The experience

Play three scenarios in a night: one realistic, one funny, one morally gray. Cap the night there so the debrief stays kind. Draw by number, from a hat or from a die you both trust, and veto before writing. If the clock dies after two, save the gray one for another night. A finished night can be two. The bank will wait. If you are tired at the start, play the funny one and stop.

**The turn.**

1. Read the scenario aloud. Confirm you both accept it.
2. For 2 minutes, in silence, each of you writes the other's answer. Do this before anyone speaks, or the second writer will cheat by accident.
3. On the slip: the choice you think they would make. Then three beats: a value, a fear or a hope, and a practical constraint. If one beat is "none that I can name," write none.
4. Reveal one borrower at a time. Read the slip. Then the person whose mind it is gives their actual choice and their actual reasoning. They say which beat was a real value, which was a real constraint, and which belonged to a stranger.
5. If you are scoring, mark the slip. Then switch borrowers on the same scenario.
6. Each person writes one line: the value they missed, in the other's words if those words were offered. That line is the point of the turn.

**Scoring, optional and light.** The choice itself scores zero, whether you matched it or missed it. Naming a real value of theirs, a value they recognize as theirs in this situation, scores 2. A constraint you understood scores 1. A wrong value cannot be rescued by a lucky yes. Adjacent counts as the 2 only if they say "that's mine." If they hesitate, it is a miss. Ask what value you missed, write it down, and subtract nothing. There is no prize for the higher total. If the total makes either of you smaller, throw it away before you leave the table.

### Realistic

**R1. Overtime, and a friend in crisis.** Your partner is asked to stay late on the one night a friend has asked them to come over because the friend is in a real crisis, not a vague bad mood. The work is genuine. The friend is someone they love. Leaving work has a cost. Staying has a cost. What do they do in the next hour, and why?

**R2. Housekeeping, in front of them.** A parent, or an older relative who still has opinions, criticizes your partner's housekeeping in front of them and in front of you. The criticism is about a messy room, not about a moral failing, and it lands anyway. What do they do in the next 10 minutes?

**R3. The car for a week.** A neighbor asks to borrow the car for a week. The neighbor has been decent. The car is needed for ordinary life. There is no villain in the ask. If you have no car, use a bike, a camera, or the one good pot. What do they do?

**R4. The wallet.** They find a wallet with cash and an ID, on a sidewalk or a shop floor, with no owner in sight. What do they do before the day ends?

**R5. The restaurant already won.** The group chat has agreed on a restaurant your partner dislikes. Everyone else has said yes. Objecting now makes them the problem. Going means an evening of food they do not want. What do they do?

**R6. The sudden toast.** They are asked, with two hours' notice, to give a toast for someone they like and do not know how to praise in public. Refusing is allowed and a little ugly. Accepting means standing up. What do they do?

**R7. The airport.** A friend asks for a ride from the airport at an hour that wrecks their sleep before a hard day. The friend could take a cab. The friend asked them. What do they do?

**R8. The gift from the avoided person.** A package arrives with their name on it, clearly a gift from someone they have been gently avoiding. You are the one who sees it first. What do they want done with it, and what do they do when they find out it is in the house?

### Funny

**F1. The solemn dessert.** A solemn host serves, with visible pride, a dessert your partner hates. The table is watching the first bite. What do they do?

**F2. The free tattoo.** They are offered a free tattoo of your face, today, by an artist who is genuinely good. It is a joke that could become permanent if they nod. What do they do?

**F3. The hated song.** Their most disliked song comes on, and everyone else starts dancing as if the song were a civic duty. What do they do with their body and their face?

**F4. The incorrect expert.** A stranger explains your partner's own job to them, incorrectly, with great confidence, for 9 minutes. Escape is possible and slightly rude. What do they do?

**F5. The vegetable prize.** They win a contest. The prize is a year of the vegetable they cook badly and pretend to enjoy when other people serve it. Public refusal is available. So is a cupboard full of the vegetable. What do they do?

**F6. The microphone.** At a party they are handed a microphone and the room expects a character they did not agree to play. The character is silly, not cruel. What do they do?

**F7. The houseplant threat.** Their phone autocorrects a tender text into something that sounds like a threat against a houseplant. The recipient has already replied, "Are you okay?" What do they send next?

**F8. The accidental sculpture.** In a museum they have been leaning, for several minutes, on what they thought was a bench. A guard arrives to explain that it is a sculpture. What do they do in the first 30 seconds?

### Morally gray

**G1. The small secret.** A sibling, or a close friend if there is no sibling, asks them to keep a non-dangerous secret from you. The person is taking a night class, failing it, and does not want advice or an announcement. The class harms no one. Your partner knows you would rather not be left out of something true. What do they do?

**G2. The refund.** A refund is available only if they give a reason that is not quite true. The product disappointed them for a true reason the form does not list. The false reason is mild. The amount matters to the week and would not ruin a person they know. What do they do?

**G3. The skipped event.** A friend wants them to skip your event to help someone else. The someone else is in a real bind. Your event is one you have been looking forward to, and you would feel the absence. What do they do?

**G4. The shortchange.** They see a stranger shortchanged by a large amount. The stranger has not noticed. The line behind them is long, and speaking up will make a scene. What do they do?

**G5. The taken credit.** In a meeting, a coworker presents an idea that was your partner's, and the room praises the coworker. Correcting the record embarrasses everyone. Letting it go costs them the credit. What do they do before the meeting ends?

**G6. The hallway fifty.** They find a fifty that clearly fell from a neighbor's pocket. The neighbor is proud, short on money, and would hate to be seen as careless. Returning it in public and sliding it under the door are different acts. What do they do?

**G7. The grieving untruth.** A relative on the phone asks them to agree with a claim they think is false. The relative is grieving, and the claim is how the relative is making sense of the week. The claim is untrue and not dangerous. What do they say on that call?

**G8. The early table.** At a community sale they can have early access because they know the organizer. The advantage is small, legal, and slightly sneaky. Using it means a nicer thing in the house and a worse story if anyone asks how they got it. What do they do?

Close the night by reading the missed-value lines aloud, or by putting them in the journal and saying nothing clever. Thank the person who corrected you. Stop at three scenarios even if the bank is tempting. The gray one does not need a verdict. You were practicing the borrow, not sentencing anyone.

## Three ways to play

**20 minutes.** One scenario, both of you writing. Skip the score. Read the missed value and stop. The funny list is the kindest single draw. One scenario is a finished session.

**40 minutes.** One realistic, one funny, one gray, in that order, unless you are too tired for gray and you say so. Both people write every time. Optional scoring. Timer on, so the third scenario still happens as a conversation and not as a rush. If you pass 40 minutes after two, save the gray card.

**70 minutes.** The same three, with a slower reveal. After each actual answer, the borrower may try one second borrow: rewrite the three beats now that they have heard the correction, and read the new slip. The second borrow is practice. It does not erase the first score, and it does not have to be scored at all. Still cap the night at three scenarios.

## Going deeper

On a later night, take one scenario you both found slippery and ask only the constraint question: "What practical fact about your life did I leave out?" Constraints are often where love of accuracy actually lives. The value can be shared and the wall can still be private.

You may invent one scenario from your real week with the names removed, if both of you agree it is cold enough to play. A live fight is not a card. If either of you feels the heat, the card goes back in the drawer and you talk as yourselves, outside the game, or you do not talk about it tonight.

A deeper gray card, only if you both want it, can be one you write about loyalty to a friend and loyalty to each other where nobody is in danger and nobody is being lied to about sex. Keep G1's scale. Small, non-dangerous, and still uncomfortable.

## Variations and replay

Low energy: one funny scenario, spoken instead of written, with the three beats still named. The writing rule bends. The silence-before-reveal rule stays, so jot a word on a scrap even if the rest is talk.

Long-distance: private notes, then a call for the reveal. The bank travels.

Accessibility: read the card aloud, allow extra time, skip any scenario that assumes a body, a job, or a family shape you do not have. Large print. No humiliation of a culture or a body, including in the funny list. F8 is about a bench that was art. If museums are a sore subject, draw again.

Replay until the bank thins, then write four new cards using the four-line replacement rule. Retire any card that produced a flinch you do not want to repeat. House rule worth keeping: the choice column stays unscored.

[082](082), The Moral Gray Area, is a conversation about dilemmas with no obvious right answer. The gray list here is fuel for a borrow, and then a correction. Leave the chapters different.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

They draw R4, the wallet. Rowan, writing as Vale, chooses "turn it in the same day," fears that Vale is managing a reputation and dreads being seen as a thief, and names the ID as a constraint because a lost-and-found exists. Vale's actual choice is also to turn it in the same day. The value is different. Vale is thinking about a person already having a bad day, and about a transit card a stranger once mailed back. Vale grants the ID as a real constraint and refuses the reputation fear. Rowan matched the yes. The yes scores 0. The wrong value scores 0. The constraint scores 1. Rowan writes the missed value in Vale's words: "The owner is already having a bad day."

They draw F1, the dessert. Vale, writing as Rowan, chooses "eat it and praise it," and names the value as Rowan would rather swallow a bad sweet than embarrass a host who tried. Rowan's actual choice is two bites, praise for the effort, and a stop. The host-kindness is real. The missing beat is a limit: Rowan does not force down food that makes them ill. Vale missed the choice and grasped the value. The value scores 2. The choice scores 0. Vale's 2 sits above Rowan's lucky yes. That is the experiment working.

Nobody wins the relationship. They skip G3 because the week already has a tender calendar, draw G1 instead, and stop when Vale says the night-class secret would be kept for a while, with a plain sentence to Rowan that a small secret is being held and that it is not about the two of them. Rowan had guessed "tells me everything the same night." Rowan writes that missed value down and does not argue the guess into a rule.

## Afterward

Where did you borrow a real value, even if the choice was off?
Where did a correct choice hide a stranger's reason?
Which constraint surprised you because it was practical and still revealing?
Did the points start to sting? If they did, did you drop them?
Is there a scenario you refuse to play, and can you say so without justifying it?

You do not owe each other a matching mind. You can owe an accurate correction, and you can receive one without turning it into a grade.

## Our Story

Date the session, the scenario numbers, and whether you scored. The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. Printed prompts want at least eight handwriting lines, baselines at least 0.32 inch apart.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- The scenario, the choice you thought the other person would make, and the motive you were sure they had.
- The value you missed, in their words if they offered them.
partner_b:
- The choice you assigned them, and the clue you used — a habit, a value, or a past decision — so your page records the guess, not the grade.
- A reason of yours that your partner borrowed accurately, and a clue that belonged to a stranger.
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- Set the real reasons beside the borrowed ones. Keep the comparison about why. There is no prize for guessing.
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
- Where you borrow each other's reasons more accurately now, and where you still invent a stranger.
```
