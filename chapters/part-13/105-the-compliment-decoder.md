## The hook

"You're amazing" floats. It may be true, and it has no address. A specific compliment usually does. Someone made room, remembered a name, put a dish down, refused to turn a story into a project. The person who receives the line will often supply a scene, the one they have been crediting. The person who wrote the line may have meant a different hour entirely.

You each write a handful of observations that could only have come from living with this person. The receiver guesses the episode. Then the writer reveals the real one. A match is a pleasure. A mismatch is the chapter working. Attention is not on trial.

## Why it might matter

Stock praise is pleasant and unlocatable. Kind, beautiful, amazing: the words can be sincere and still leave the receiver decorating a blank. When the praise points at a behavior, a memory is already attached in the giver's mind. The receiver's guess shows which memory they have been living with. Those two memories are often both respectful, and they are often not the same day.

The mechanism is the delay. If you reveal the source first, the guess never happens, and you learn only that the praise was acceptable. If you guess first, you learn what the other person thinks you see. The reveal then tells the truth about the source. Being accurately imagined feels different from being praised. This hour separates those two pleasures on purpose.

Feeling appreciative and feeling appreciated have been tied to responsiveness and to the ordinary work of keeping a relationship. That finding is not a test of this sheet. A wrong guess does not mean the appreciation failed. It means the episode in the giver's pocket was not the episode on display.

This decoder was formulated for this project. It is not a claim of worldwide novelty, and it is not a quiz about categories of love.

## Before you begin

Each person writes five observations about the other. The short path uses three. Go past looks, and past stock adjectives such as kind, beautiful, or amazing. Each line should point at a pattern or a behavior: a way of entering a room, a habit with other people's stories, a competence that shows up on ordinary Tuesdays.

On a private line the writer does not show yet, note the actual episode. One scene, with a rough date. "Last Tuesday, the dish" is enough. "Always" is too foggy to decode.

Skip sexual or bodily compliments unless you have both opted into that kind of candor for this sitting. Do not compliment someone for tolerating harm. A line that is really advice, "You're so good when you finally relax," is not a compliment. Retire it and write what they actually did.

If a line depends on a tone of voice or a facial expression, put the meaning in the action so it survives being read aloud, or read by someone who did not see the face.

You may pass on guessing a line that feels loaded, and still hear the reveal, or pass on the reveal too. Passing is an ordinary option. A finished decode is not consent to anything further.

## The experience

Make a decoder sheet with four columns:

Compliment.
Recipient's guessed episode.
Actual episode.
Same / different.

Write the five compliments where the other person can see them. Keep the actual-episode column covered, folded, or in a note you do not scroll to.

One giver reads a compliment, or lets the receiver read it. The receiver guesses the lived story in a sentence or two. They are guessing the source the giver had in mind, not auditioning for a prize. Then the giver reveals the memory, which may be another day entirely.

Mark same or different. Different is a successful twist. Say so, in the room, the first time it happens, so nobody apologizes for failing a test you are not giving.

The receiver may say the guessed episode also mattered. It can. It does not replace the writer's source. Both can be true. The sheet's last column is about the source the giver meant, not about which story was worthier.

One round is enough. Five lines, or three. After the reveals, each person names one compliment whose real episode they want kept beside the praise. The misses do not need a memorial. Journal the episodes the receiver wants to keep.

A sample row, written for this chapter:

Compliment: "You make room for a story without parking in it."
Guess: "The night I talked about my sister."
Actual: "Last Tuesday you put the dish down and did not offer a fix."
Same / different: different.

Both scenes are respectful. The reveal simply tells the truth about which one prompted the line.

If you are tempted to use the reveal as proof that your partner does not pay attention, stop. That move breaks the game into a lecture. The interesting fact is the gap between the credited scene and the intended one. You can be curious about the gap. You do not score it.

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** Three compliments each. Guess, reveal, mark same or different. Name one line worth keeping. Three is a finished decoder. Use this when five would turn appreciation into homework.

**20 minutes.** Five each, written briefly, then decoded in a steady rhythm. About a minute for the guess and the reveal. This is the version that lets a pattern appear: maybe you both credit the dramatic nights and the giver has been watching the small repairs.

**25 minutes.** The round of five, plus a few minutes on one mismatch you both found interesting. Tell the guessed story in two sentences, then the actual story in two sentences, and stop. The extra time is for savoring the gap. It is not a review of how well anyone knows their partner.

## Going deeper

On a later day, take one kept episode and notice whether you can see the behavior again in the wild, without announcing that you are collecting evidence. If you tell them you saw it, do it as a new compliment with the source attached immediately. The decoder is for the hidden episode. Daily life can have the source in the same sentence.

You can sort a line, privately, into pattern or single scene. "You make room for a story" might be a pattern the dish night merely proved. Say that if it is true. The guessed episode still gets named before the proof is revealed.

Leave love-language inventories in their own drawer. People seem to benefit from a range of affectionate behavior. This sheet does not sort you into a type, and matching categories is not the game.

## Variations and replay

By call, exchange sealed lines, guess on the call, then reveal. By text, the guess has to arrive before the source. If you reveal early, that row is void and you can laugh and pick another.

Spoken or written both work. No appearance ranking. A compliment the other person cannot access through the sense you used should be rewritten until the action is the meaning.

The motif is a card faceup and a second card still turned over. The face-down card is the source.

Replay when praise has gone generic, or when you notice you have been thanking each other for the wrong scene for weeks. Date the sheet. The same behavior may have a new source next year, and the old source can remain true.

030, The Compliment You Can’t Dodge, practices receiving praise without dodging it. 078, The Unspoken Thank-You, delivers one thanks that never quite got said. 076, The Moment I Saw You Differently, recalls a time your picture of the other person expanded. Here the work is narrower: decode the specific memory the giver was holding, and let that memory be a surprise.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan writes, among five lines: "You make room for a story without parking in it." Vale guesses, "The night I talked about my sister. You didn't steer." Rowan reveals, "That night mattered. The source I mean is last Tuesday. You put the dish down and did not offer a fix while I talked about the meeting. I had been waiting for the fix. It didn't come. The story got to end." Vale says the sister night still counts in their own memory. They mark the row different. Nobody keeps score.

Vale's line: "You remember the names of people I mention once." Rowan guesses, "Your coworker Nia. I used it the next day and you looked startled, in a good way." Vale says, "Nia is real, and she is not the source. The source is the clinic clerk. I said the name once in the elevator. You used it on the way out. I had already forgotten I had said it." Rowan had built a whole warmth around Nia. The clerk is a smaller, truer room. They mark it different, and Vale asks that the clerk stay beside the compliment. The Nia guess is allowed to have been kind and still wrong about the source.

A third line matches. Rowan had written, "You return the library books before the tone in my head gets ugly." Vale guesses the rainy Thursday with the two novels. That was the day. They mark it same, enjoy it, and do not treat the match as a higher grade.

## Afterward

1 minute is enough.

Which reveal surprised you, and which scene had you been crediting instead?
Did the guessed episode still matter once the real source was told?
Where did you want to defend your attention, and did you let the gap stand?
Which single episode do you want kept beside the praise?
Did any line sneak in as advice, or as a comment on a body you had not agreed to discuss?

You do not owe each other a perfect memory. You can keep one true source, and let it be the one the giver meant.

## Our Story

Date the session. Note the version, the place, and whether you finished or paused. Initials and mood are optional.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. Write the episodes you want kept, not a catalog of the misses.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- A deeper compliment you offered, the lived story behind it, and whether the other person guessed the source.
- One compliment whose real episode you want remembered beside the praise.
partner_b:
- A compliment you received, the story you guessed, and the real source once it was told.
- Whether the guessed story still matters to you, beside the source you heard.
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- The compliment whose story you most want kept beside the praise
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
- Do you hear the story inside a compliment any sooner now?
```
