## The hook

089, The Relationship We Would Invent, writes agreements for this relationship. This chapter stress-tests a paper country. You establish it on paper. Ten principles, each one short enough that a stranger could follow it. Then you shuffle events that do not care how eloquent you were. A storm. A guest. A joke that stopped being shared. For each card, you amend, you defend, or you leave the argument open inside the fiction.

The strain tally belongs to the constitution. It is not a grade of the founders. The signature, if you both want one, is play. It is not a legal contract, and it is not a set of house rules unless you explicitly end the game first and say so.

## Why it might matter

Ideals that never meet an event stay slogans. "Be kind" cannot be strained by a card, because a card cannot find an edge to press. A sentence a stranger could follow can be strained: breakfast before any plan, the lamp by the door, the last piece of bread. The mechanism is the collision. You discover whether a principle was a value or a mood by asking what it costs to keep it and what it costs to change it.

During play you stay inside the fiction. A rhyme with your actual life can be noticed afterward. A real crisis is discussed by setting the cards down, if you both agree. It is not smuggled in as a draw. Constitution games and salon utopias are widespread. They are not a validated protocol. No outcome is claimed for this deck.

[089](089), The Relationship We Would Invent, writes agreements for this relationship, from scratch, with no civilization and no event deck. This chapter stress-tests a paper country and stays inside the fiction until both of you say the game is over.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can both see one worksheet. A call works if the worksheet is shared and one person draws and reads. Large print is welcome. No map on the floor. Principles about religion, children, money, sex, and mobility stay optional. Do not assume marriage, monogamy, or a shared purse. A citizen with limited stamina is already a full citizen. Card 8 must not become a moral about who is useful.

Either person may discard a card that lands on a live grief or a real harm, and draw the next. If a card opens an actual fear, pause the game. The fiction is the safety. Cards 7 and 10 are easy to misuse as a replay of a real, sore vote. If that is happening, discard the card.

Say these once:

A line enters the ten only if both of you can live with it.
Up to two protected dissents may be recorded. One founder wants the sentence on the page. The other signs the margin "I do not share this." A dissent is not sulking, and it is not erased to force harmony. A dissent is also not a veto of the other person's safety.
Strain scores the constitution. At 4 or more, you retire one principle or add one freedom that begins "Citizens may always…"
There is no winner.

## The experience

You are aiming at ten founding principles. The short game uses six principles and two events, for stamina. The standard game is ten principles and three events. The long game draws a fourth event and adds holidays.

**Phase 1, about 15 minutes, silent.** Each person drafts six candidates: laws, holidays, freedoms, or consequences. One sentence each. A stranger should be able to follow it. Grain worth stealing, and then replacing with your own: "Breakfast is eaten before any plan is spoken." "A borrowed book comes back with the page that mattered written on a scrap." "The lamp by the door stays lit until both citizens are home." Abstract kindness dies on contact with the deck. Write something an event could touch.

**Phase 2, merge to ten.** Read the drafts. A line stays only if both can live with it. Combine near-duplicates. When you have ten, number them. For each, mark whose words started it. Record up to two dissents in the margin. If you cannot reach ten without a sentence one of you cannot live with, stop at the number you can both sign, and let the empty rows show. Empty rows are more honest than fake harmony. The standard aim is still ten.

**Phase 3, the events.** Shuffle the twelve cards. Draw three, or four if the hour is easy. Do not reuse a card in the same sitting. For each card, about 8 minutes.

Name the principle under strain. If none of them can feel the card, the principle was too abstract. Say so, and either rewrite it in one concrete sentence or let the card raise strain by 2 as an open card.

Answer the card's three questions. Which principle strains? What does it cost to keep it? What does it cost to change it?

Then choose one move.

Amend: rewrite the principle. Both must agree to the new sentence.
Defend: keep the sentence, and say the human cost aloud.
Exception: one sentence that narrows the principle. Both agree. Mark strain +1.
If you cannot choose, the card stays open and strain rises by 2. An open card is an unfinished argument inside the fiction.

At strain 4 or more, retire one principle or add one freedom that begins "Citizens may always…" There is no relationship score hiding in the tally.

**Close.** Read the ten lines, or the fewer you truly adopted. Each person adds one holiday or one ordinary ritual. Sign only if both want to. The signature is play.

**The charter sheet.** After the game, or during it if you like the lines, use `journal/templates/civilization-charter.md`. Baselines at least 0.32 inch apart. Both founders get the same number of lines and the same size of signature.

Name of the civilization, optional and allowed to be ridiculous. Date and place. Initials.

Article from Founder A, eight lines. Article from Founder B, eight lines. Articles you adopt together. One random event that tested the charter. What you adapted. What you refused to adapt. Two signature lines of equal length.

A later event uses another copy of the amendment half. Do not erase the original articles. The ten-row worksheet is the working constitution. The charter sheet is the record of the collision.

### The event deck, ready to copy

Cut twelve cards, about 3 by 5 inches, or twelve slips of equal size. Large print is welcome. On the face, the number and the event. The three questions can sit under the event or on the back. Read all of it before you choose a move. Shuffle. Draw three, or four if time is easy. Set a discarded card aside for the rest of the sitting. The deck may be copied for play. It is not a commercial expansion.

## Three ways to play

**45 minutes.** Six principles and two events. Skip the seal if you want. This is the short path, for stamina or a short evening. Six is a finished constitution when ten would be padding.

**70 minutes.** Silent drafts, merge to ten, draw three events, record the moves, read the ten lines, add one holiday or ritual each. Sign only if you both want to.

**90 minutes.** Ten principles and four events, plus a slower dissent if you need it. Use a second charter sheet for the fourth event so the original articles stay unerased. Strain still triggers the same choice at 4.

## Going deeper

After the last card, pick the principle that survived untouched and ask whether any card in the remaining deck could reach it. If none could, it is still a slogan. Rewrite it or retire it with affection.

You may each propose one freedom that begins "Citizens may always…" even if strain never hit 4. A freedom you both like can enter as an eleventh line, marked extra, so the ten stay intact. Or it can replace a principle you have stopped believing. Replacement is an amend, and both agree.

A yearly glance can live beside [094](094), The Annual Reintroduction. The glance is not the same ritual. [052](052), The Belief Beneath the Belief, examines a real conviction. This deck examines a fictional law.

## Variations and replay

Low energy: six principles, one event, spoken more than written. The three questions still get answers. The move is still one of the four.

Long-distance: shared worksheet, one person draws and reads the full card, including the three questions.

If a principle about religion, children, money, sex, or mobility feels like the live argument rather than the fiction, leave it off the page. The country can be founded on breakfast, lamps, and bread.

Replay by rewriting before a new draw, or by shuffling the unused half of the deck back in next time. A card that landed on a real harm stays out until you both invite it. House rule worth keeping: strain is a design counter.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you. Their country is called the Soft-Door Republic, which they admit is a bit much. These ten are theirs, not a template you must adopt. They do not answer the deck. Cards they draw have nowhere to land, and the page has to show the miss.

1. Breakfast is eaten before any plan is spoken.
2. The blue mug changes hands on the first of the month, and the change is announced, not performed as a bit.
3. A walk after supper is the default. Rain moves it to the hallway and does not cancel the company.
4. Letters from outside are read aloud when both citizens are in the room.
5. The lamp by the door stays on until both are home.
6. Dishes wait until morning. The person who cooked does not wash them that night.
7. Morning music is one song, chosen by the person who woke first.
8. A borrowed book returns with a scrap naming the page that mattered.
9. A quarrel stops at the bedroom door. If it resumes, it resumes at the table.
10. Citizens may always take the last piece of bread without a speech.

They draw card 4, the joke. None of the ten can feel it. Principle 2 mentions a bit and does not cover a laugh that leaves someone out. Rowan wants to defend the charter as it stands: "We did not found a joke law, and I will not invent one to look prepared." Vale wants a sentence on the page. They cannot agree to an amend. Vale writes in the dissent margin, beside a blank they refuse to fake: "I do not share the silence. A joke that leaves one of us out should be retired." The card stays open. Strain rises by 2. The cost of defending the silence, said aloud: the left-out person has no law to point at. The cost of Vale's sentence, which does not enter the ten: a beloved bit might die, and Rowan is not ready to sign that.

They draw card 8, the storm. Principle 3 moves a walk indoors. It says nothing about being ordered to work. They amend principle 3, and both agree to the new sentence: "A walk after supper is the default, rain moves it indoors, and weather creates no duty. Help is asked. The answer can be no." The cost of the amend, said aloud: the road may stay blocked while both of them stay inside. The cost of leaving principle 3 untouched, which they do not pick: a storm becomes a reason to order a citizen out.

They draw card 1, the stores. Principle 10 is about bread inside the house. It does not say what happens when people outside ask for a share. Rowan moves to amend. Vale defends principle 10 as a household custom about the last slice, not a rule for outsiders, and says the cost of defending it: the card remains an open mouth. They do not sand the miss into a principle they do not believe. The card stays open. Strain rises by 2. At 4 they add one freedom, both agreeing: "Citizens may always say no to a request for the stores, and the no needs no lecture." The freedom is extra. The ten stay, including the amended walk.

They do not draw the guest's ritual, the year away, the silent day, the private story, the extra vote, the private calendar, the promise, the ranking, or the newcomer. Those cards are still in the deck, unanswered. They add no holiday that secretly solves a card they did not draw. They sign because they both want to. The signature does not govern Tuesday.

## Afterward

Which principle was concrete enough for a card to find it?
Where did you defend, and what did the defense cost inside the fiction?
Where did a dissent almost get erased for the look of the page?
Did a card try to become a real argument? Did you set it down?
What holiday or ordinary ritual, if any, do you actually feel like keeping?

You founded a paper country. You can leave it on the paper.

## Our Story

Date the founding, the name if it has one, and how many events you drew. The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The charter sheet holds the articles. These prompts hold the night. Printed lines are at least eight per prompt, baselines at least 0.32 inch apart. A later event gets a new amendment half, not an erasure.
