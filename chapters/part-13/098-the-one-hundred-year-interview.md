## The hook

Sit down as if the long life has already happened. You are both about 100. If that number feels cruel, call it a long life from now. The decades ahead are fiction, and the fiction is allowed to disagree with itself. One of you remembers a night class. The other remembers that the night class never existed and that the real story of those years was trains, a bad suitcase, and a joke that took a decade to land.

Then the costume comes partway off. Each of you names up to three invented memories you would genuinely like to make real. Fewer than three is a complete answer. The interview is the play. The three memories are the appetite hiding inside the play.

## Why it might matter

Couples often talk about the future as a single plan that has to survive both people's vetoes in the same 10 minutes. That is a useful way to book a trip. It is a cramped way to find out what either of you still wants. A century of obvious fiction gives the wants somewhere to sprawl. Contradictions can sit on the same porch without a vote.

The mechanism is the costume, then the cut back to now. You build those decades out of scraps already in reach: a town, a skill, a kind of Tuesday, a mistake you are willing to claim in advance. The theatrical frame should stay obviously fictional. You are not remembering. You are inventing in the past tense, which is a pleasure, and then you are choosing which inventions still tug once the scarf is off.

This was formulated for this project. Interviews with a future self are an old coaching habit. They are not this chapter. Nothing here extends a life, repairs a regret, or requires the two of you to share one canonical century.

## Before you begin

Sit as if on a porch, even if the porch is a kitchen. A scarf is optional and slightly ridiculous, which helps. Nothing is also correct. Speak a little more slowly if you want the theater. Skip the creaky-voice impression. Old age is not the joke, and illness or death at a partner's expense is out of bounds.

Set the year as 100 years from the older partner's birth, or skip the arithmetic and say, "We are both about 100." If disability, illness, or grief makes the number unkind, use "a long life from now" and keep every other rule.

Phones facedown. Paper for the future-memory page. A timer, so one person does not narrate both lives.

Three agreements:

You may pass, pause, or leave the interview without naming three memories.
During the interview, neither of you corrects the other's fiction. Afterward you may say which story you loved. You may not prosecute it.
An invented memory that spends the other person's body, fertility, faith, or home needs a plain conversation before it lands on the "make real" page. Wanting it in costume is allowed. Listing it as a plan is a different act.

The future is a poor place to indict the present. If a centenarian story is mostly a complaint about the person sitting here, set the decade down and choose a kinder fiction, or tell the complaint in your own age, outside the game.

## The experience

One of you interviews. The other answers as the centenarian. About 8 minutes, then you switch. Use these questions, or any three of them. They were written for this chapter:

What adventure still makes you laugh?
What mistake taught you a skill you were glad to have?
What ordinary Tuesday would you repeat?
What did you stop pretending?
What did we get wrong about each other, and later understand?

Answer in scenes. A town, a tool, a meal, a sentence someone said. If you catch yourself delivering a moral, return to the Tuesday. Contradictory versions of the same decade stay in play. One centenarian may have learned to sail. The other may have no memory of a boat and a vivid memory of a roof garden that mostly died. Both centuries are affectionate. Neither is the official one.

The interviewer may ask one follow-up that asks for a picture: "What did the Tuesday smell like?" or "Who was in the room when you stopped pretending?" A follow-up that asks the centenarian to justify the present relationship is a verdict. This porch has no verdicts.

When both interviews are done, take the scarf off, or simply sit up. Each person names up to three invented memories they would genuinely like to make real someday. Say them as wants, from the age you are now. A memory you enjoyed inventing and do not want to attempt is a good story. Leave it on the porch.

For each want that survives, one block on the future-memory page:

The scene, in two sentences, still a little cinematic if it must be.
A step available without heroics, from the life you actually have. One afternoon, one invitation, one class that already exists, one tool already in the house.
A piece that remains fantasy unless the other person wants it. Name that piece so it does not sneak into the step.

The page is practical and optional. It is not a promise sheet. A step can be declined later. A fantasy piece can stay loved and undone. If you cannot find a step without heroics, the memory stays fiction, and that is an honest outcome.

Read the pages to each other only as far as you want them heard. One of you may have three. The other may have one. You do not average them into a joint destiny.

## Three ways to play

**30 minutes.** Each person answers two interview questions, about 5 minutes each. Then each names one invented memory and, if they want, one step. Fewer than three is complete. Use this when you are tired or when the number 100 needs to stay light.

**50 minutes.** Both interviews, with room for one contradiction to be enjoyed rather than sanded down. Then the three-memory naming, spoken. Write the page only for the wants that still have a pulse after they are said out loud.

**60 minutes.** The standard interview, plus a quiet 10 minutes to fill the future-memory page before you read any of it. The extra time is for separating the scene, the step, and the piece that must stay fantasy. Still no shared canonical century. Still no correction of the other person's decades.

## Going deeper

On a later day, pick one step that was small enough to attempt and tell the truth about whether you did it. A step that shrank, or that turned out to belong only to the costume, goes back on the porch without a speech about follow-through.

You can replay a single decade you both described, side by side, and read the two versions aloud. The pleasure is the mismatch. Ask, "What did your version protect?" only if the speaker wants that question. Some mismatches are just style. A person who tells adventures and a person who tells Tuesdays can love each other without merging genres.

If a want keeps returning across replays, it may be ready for a real conversation about time and money. Have that conversation in the present tense, with the costume off. The centenarian does not get to sign for both of you.

## Variations and replay

By call, one person reads the questions and both answer. The future-memory page can be a shared note you fill after you hang up. By letter, write one decade and one want. The reply names a detail that felt like the writer, and does not edit their century.

If the age frame is wrong for your bodies or your grief, drop it and keep the questions, the contradictions, and the three wants. The control that matters is the return to the age you are.

No makeup, no props required. The motif is two chairs and a long shadow, with three small marks for the memories you might try. A doodle in the margin is optional.

Replay on a birthday, or when a plan has gone stale and you want appetite before logistics. Date the page. A want that was a boat at 40 may be a lake afternoon at 50, and both entries deserve to exist.

090, The Future Memory, builds one happy scene in the past tense and then asks how you might make it. Here the span is decades, contradiction is welcome, and the twist is up to three chosen inventions. 095, A Promise I Actually Want to Keep, is for commitments you mean to hold. This page can feed that chapter later. It does not do the promising. 049, The Life Not Taken, looks backward at a real fork. This interview looks forward through obvious fiction.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

They say they are both about 100. Rowan claims that at 72 they opened a tiny night class in reading maps, and that Vale fell asleep in the front row on purpose, as a review. Vale's version of the same decade has no class. It has trains, a suitcase that broke in a station, and a roof garden that failed with commitment. Vale says the suitcase fight became their best story. Rowan does not correct the missing classroom. Vale does not sink the boat Rowan adds, 5 minutes later, for a summer they also did not live.

Scarves off. Rowan names three invented memories worth trying to make real. A week in a coastal town with no agenda. A clumsy class taught once, to people who already like them. A kitchen table that feeds neighbors once a season. The steps are smaller than the costume: one long weekend this year, phones in a drawer in the mornings; one evening offered to a friend who asked, not a school; two people invited, not six. The cottage they do not own stays fantasy. So does the loyal front row.

Vale names two, and stops. Learning the names of the birds on their actual street, which can start on the next walk. And a birthday where nobody performs, which can start by telling Rowan that a restaurant speech would miss. A third picture, fixing a boat together, stays on the fantasy side because it spends a weekend and a chunk of money Vale has not asked for. Vale says so. Rowan does not recruit the boat back onto the page.

## Afterward

Before you turn a good story into a calendar, check the cut.

Which invented decade did you want to protect from correction, and what was the pleasure in it?
Which of your three, or fewer, still feels like a want once you are the age you are?
Which step is available without heroics, and which piece must stay fiction unless you both want it?
Did either century smuggle a complaint about the present? If yes, say the complaint in plain language, or drop it.
Are you willing to let the other person's incompatible future stand?

You do not owe each other a shared old age. You can keep one step, and you can keep it small.

## Our Story

Date the session. Note the version, the place, and whether you finished or paused. Initials and mood are optional.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together. Fewer than three aspirations is a finished page.

## Notes

No research citation is claimed for this activity. Future-self letters and interviews circulate in coaching. They were not the source of this chapter, and they are not its protocol. The decades you describe are fiction on purpose. The frame should stay obviously fictional. Formulated for this project. That is a provenance note, not a claim of worldwide novelty, and not a claim that imagining a century makes it happen, lengthens a life, or repairs regret. Two incompatible centuries can both be affectionate. The future-memory page is optional. It is not 095's promise sheet, and a piece the other person does not want remains fantasy.
