## The hook

Two labels, on every card: what happened, and what I wish you'd understood.

Some sentences never got a wall. They are not your whole biography. They are pocket-sized: a compliment that stopped at logistics, a fear that came out as a joke, a hug you wanted and did not request, a thanks you swallowed because the moment was already moving.

You build a very small museum of things never said. A museum with one label is a different museum. 006, The Museum of Me, is four galleries: an object, a person, a song, and an event. This one is the pair, and it is allowed to have closed rooms.

## Why it might matter

People often tell the event and hope the meaning will be inferred. Inference is how affection usually works, and it is also how the point gets lost. "Thanks for taking the car" can be true while the pride, the shyness, or the fear stays in the pocket. The second label is the sentence the event was carrying.

The mechanism is the pair. What happened keeps you honest about the world: a Thursday, a parking lot, a joke at dinner. What I wish you'd understood keeps you honest about the inside: the meaning you wanted attached, and did not attach. The visitor does not have to guess the second label. You write it. Being told the point is different from being praised for noticing it alone.

A label can finally say something tender or difficult. That sits near the ordinary hope of being received. It does not guarantee the reception. The visitor still has to take the card in, and they may carry one label without promising to become a new person.

This museum was formulated for this project. It is not a claim of worldwide novelty, and it is not art therapy with a result attached.

## Before you begin

Sit at a table with cards, or a shared document if you are apart. Large print is welcome. No craft skill. You are making labels, not dioramas.

Each person will build two to four exhibits, or one, if that is the true size of the night. Titles you may steal and revoice:

The Compliment I Never Gave
The Fear I Disguised as a Joke
The Moment I Needed a Hug but Didn't Ask
The Thanks I Swallowed
The Worry I Turned into a Chore

Warn before an exhibit about sex, family, or shame. The visitor may skip a room. "Not on display" finishes a label. A closed gallery is part of a real museum.

Three agreements:

Read as a curator, not as a prosecutor. The visitor asks one question and does not defend themselves until the curator invites it.
A need can be named. It does not become an order, and it does not become consent to touch.
Skip the ambush. An ambush is a relationship-ending fact, a sexual grievance, or a humiliation the visitor has no context to hold. If a card would be an ambush, use a gentler door: write it as "there have been evenings when," postpone it to a conversation you both schedule, or show the title only and say the gallery is closed.

You may pass, pause, or leave a case empty. Symmetry is not required. One full museum and one closed wing is a legitimate evening.

Nothing in the gallery immunizes cruelty. If a card is a blade, the visitor may set it down and say so.

## The experience

Take 15 minutes of quiet writing. Each exhibit uses this template. Copy it. The wording of the two labels stays exact, because the pair is the chapter.

Title.
Date, or a rough time. "A Thursday in March" is enough.
What happened. Concrete sentences. Who was there, what was done, what was said instead.
What I wish you'd understood. One or two sentences. The meaning, the fear, the thanks, the want.

Sample cards, invented for the template. They are not a script.

Title: The Compliment I Never Gave.
What happened: You rerouted an afternoon and I thanked you for the logistics.
What I wish you'd understood: The thanks was real and incomplete. I was proud of you, and too shy to make a speech in a parking lot.

Title: The Fear I Disguised as a Joke.
What happened: I made a joke about the lease when someone asked if we were settling in.
What I wish you'd understood: I was not mocking the apartment. I was afraid that wanting to stay would sound like a trap.

When the writing time ends, one curator opens one exhibit. They read both labels. They may add a sentence of context. They stop before a speech.

The visitor asks one question. Fair questions stay with the card. "Was the shyness about the speech, or about me?" "Do you want this carried, or was saying it the whole ask?" A question that begins the defense, "I only did that because," waits until the curator says there is room.

The curator answers as far as they want, then says one of these:

Carry this label. You don't have to change your personality. You just know.
This one was for the saying. You can set it down.
Not on display, actually. I read the title and that is enough.

Then the next exhibit, until both curators have shown what they meant to show. After the tour, each visitor names one label they can carry, in a sentence, without promising a renovation. If the honest answer is none tonight, say none.

Keep the cards the visitor has agreed to hold. Return or destroy the rest, including the closed galleries. The partner does not archive a room they were asked to leave shut.

## Three ways to play

**25 minutes.** One exhibit each. Read both labels. One question. One sentence about what can be carried. A single case is a finished museum. Use this when a larger gallery would become a dossier.

**70 minutes.** Two to four exhibits each, written first, then toured with the one-question rule. Leave time at the end to name what is carried and to return or destroy the rest. This is the version that lets a small unsaid thing stay small, instead of swelling into the story of the relationship.

**90 minutes.** The standard tour, plus a slower pass for anyone who wants to revise a second label after hearing themselves read it. Revision is allowed. A second label that came out sharper than you meant can be rewritten before it is carried. The extra time is for accuracy and for closed rooms, not for opening every door because the evening is long.

## Going deeper

On a later day, look only at the carried labels. Ask whether the understanding has shown up in any ordinary hour, in a size smaller than a speech. If it has not, the label can stay a label. Understanding is not a behavioral contract.

You can add a wing for funny regrets, if the first museum was solemn and you want the building to have a comic room. Same two labels. A joke you wish had landed, a story you wish you had told before it aged out. Keep the prosecutor out of the comic wing too.

If a postponed card still matters, schedule the conversation in ordinary language, with a start time and a right to stop. The museum is a poor place to spring it as a surprise sequel.

## Variations and replay

By call, exchange the cards as a document and tour one at a time. Audio labels work. So does a shared note with the template pasted at the top.

If writing is hard, dictate both labels and let the visitor read them back. If a direct card would ambush, use the hypothetical, the postponement, or the closed title. Those are designs, not failures of nerve.

The motif is two small plaques under a case that may be empty. Empty is part of the picture.

Replay when a sentence has lived in the pocket long enough to have a date. Date the new cards. An old unsaid thing that has since been said can be marked "moved to the hallway" and taken off display.

006, The Museum of Me, is a tour of objects, people, songs, and events that represent a life. This room exhibits only what was left unsaid, and every open case carries the two labels. 013, Confession Without Consequence, offers one chosen embarrassment, regret, insecurity, or harmless private truth for curious listening. The listening is receptive. It is not immunity for harm. Here you may show several small things, or one, or a closed wing. 078, The Unspoken Thank-You, completes one thanks that never landed. A thanks can be an exhibit here. The work is the pair of labels, not the thank-you alone.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan's first card. Title: The Compliment I Never Gave. Date: a Thursday in March. What happened: "You rerouted your afternoon to sit with my mother while I took the car in. I said thanks for the logistics. I did not say that the way you talked to her made the room kinder." What I wish you'd understood: "The thanks was real and also incomplete. I was proud of you, and a little shy about making it a speech in a parking lot."

Vale asks one question. "Was the shyness about her hearing, or about me?" Rowan says, "About making it a speech. You can carry the proud part. You don't have to redo the afternoon."

Vale's card. Title: The Fear I Disguised as a Joke. What happened: "When your brother asked if we were settling in, I made a joke about the lease. I laughed so the question would move on." What I wish you'd understood: "I wasn't mocking the apartment. I was scared that wanting to stay would sound like a trap."

Rowan asks, "Do you want a conversation about staying, or was the card the conversation?" Vale says, "The card is the conversation tonight. Carry the fear. Leave the lease alone."

Rowan has a third title, The Worry I Turned into a Chore, and says, "Not on display." Vale does not guess the chore. At the end, Vale can carry the incomplete thanks. Rowan can carry the fear under the lease joke. The closed card goes in the recycling. Neither of them promises a new personality.

## Afterward

Sit with the cards that remain before you narrate the evening into a breakthrough.

Which second label was the one you most wanted heard?
Which exhibit was for the saying, and which one did you actually ask to be carried?
Where did you want to defend yourself, and did you wait?
What is going back to the writer, or into the bin?
Is anything left that needs a scheduled conversation outside the gallery?

You do not owe each other the whole pocket. A museum with one honest case and one closed room has done the job.

## Our Story

Date the session. Note the version, the place, and whether you finished or paused. Initials and mood are optional.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. "Not on display" is a complete label. Keep, in the shared pages, only what the visitor agreed to hold. Return or destroy the rest.

## Notes

Formulated for this project: exhibit labels for what went unsaid, each with two lines, "What happened" and "What I wish you'd understood." That is a provenance note, not a claim of worldwide novelty. See L1998. In diary studies, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. An exhibit that finally states the emotional point sits next to that finding. L1998 did not test museum labels, and a label is not guaranteed to land as responsiveness. The visitor still has to receive it. This is not 006's tour of a life through objects, people, songs, and events. A closed gallery is part of the design. Needs can be named without becoming orders.
