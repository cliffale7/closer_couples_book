## The hook

Some conversations have a warm room and a drafty one. In the warm room you answer in whole sentences. You can be funny without scanning. In the drafty one your replies get shorter, your shoulders take a vote, and you start tracking the door. The rooms are made of actions, situations, and words. A phone turned facedown. A joke at a sore spot. A question asked in front of friends. A tone that means hurry up and disclose.

This chapter draws those rooms as a personal map, in both directions. One region is "I open." The other is "I guard." You and your partner may draw different houses. If you do, the difference is a finding to keep. Averaging it into one shared comfort zone would erase the person who needed the map.

No clause on this page obligates you to be unguarded. Guardedness may remain. Love and safety can share a house. This chapter does not treat them as the same room.

## Why it might matter

Most couples already know, in a half-private way, which tones make honesty easier. The knowledge stays half-private because saying it can sound like a complaint about the other person's character. A map changes the unit. You are describing cues that land on you, including ones that have nothing to do with malice. Fluorescent light. A rushed "just tell me." Being asked something tender while a friend is in the kitchen. Your partner can learn the cue without being cast as an unsafe person. The map is a record of conditions. There is no character verdict at the bottom.

Openness, when it happens, is still a choice made in the moment. You can have the phone in the other room, the good chair, the right hour, and still decide the story waits. The condition is a kindness. The decision stays yours. Requiring disclosure because the map was completed is one of the ways this chapter fails. Another is a map that only lists your partner's faults and never names a cue you yourself create, or a situation that is simply loud, bright, or public.

This page claims no study. It is not a trauma protocol, and it borrows no physiology. If the real concern is violence or coercion, stop. This worksheet is the wrong tool, and it will not teach you how to control or confront a partner. Urgent local help is the right door.

Two neighboring chapters draw different pictures. The Instruction Manual for My Bad Days (057) says how you act when distressed and what helps or worsens that hour. What I Don’t Show the World (011) is about parts of you kept from the wider public, and what might make them safer to reveal. The Invisible Need (061) starts from a signal you already sent. Stay here with situational and verbal cues that open or close you with this partner, on ordinary days, including days that are going fine.

## Before you begin

Use one shared page with two regions, or two cards per person labeled "I open" and "I guard." Large type, speech, and a screen reader all work. Eye contact is unnecessary. Leaving a cue unnamed is allowed. You do not justify every closed door. "I guard when the topic is my family, and I am not explaining why tonight" is a complete entry.

Include sensory life if it belongs: light, noise, scent, pain, pace, and privacy. Include the clock. Include other people in the room. Directness and politeness differ by culture and by the families you grew up in. The map is personal. A cue that feels like respect to one of you can feel like distance to the other. Write both, and do not sand them into a compromise cue that neither of you actually lives.

At a distance, entries may include response time and a word for when a reply will be late. "If I send 'later,' I am still in the conversation and I will answer after work." That is a safety cue of a practical kind. A fair reading does not turn the word into round-the-clock availability.

Agree that you will compare overlaps and differences without grading them. Then you will choose one opening condition to try in a later conversation, and one guardedness cue the partner will respect rather than push through. One of each. A renovation of the whole house is a different project.

## The experience

**1. Fill "I open."** Each person adds actions, situations, and words. Aim for a handful, not a constitution. Examples you might recognize, and then replace with your own: the phones are in another room; questions come one at a time; you are side by side rather than face to face; someone says "you can stop"; the hour is after food; the room is dim; a hard topic is booked rather than sprung.

**2. Fill "I guard."** Same categories. A joke at a sore spot. A rushed tone. Being asked something personal in front of friends. A "why are you like this." Being interrupted. A scent or a volume that makes thinking expensive. A demand for eye contact. A serious talk launched in the car when one of you cannot leave. Write the cue, and write what guarding looks like if you know: shorter answers, humor, a subject change, silence, a wish to reschedule.

**3. Read both maps aloud or trade the page.** Notice overlaps with a little pleasure. Two people who both open when phones are away have an easy condition to keep. Then notice differences without repairing them. One of you opens on a walk. The other guards on walks because the street is loud and the talk cannot be paused without stopping in traffic. Both entries stand.

**4. Choose one condition to try later.** Not in this sitting, unless you both want to. "Next hard conversation, phones in the other room, and we sit at an angle." Write when you will try it, and write that either person may still decline to open. The condition is a setup. Opening stays your choice.

**5. Choose one guardedness cue to respect.** "If either of us says 'not in front of them,' family jokes stop." "If one of us says 'too fast,' the other repeats the last question more slowly or lets it drop." Respect here means you stop pushing. It does not mean you agree that the topic is forever closed. It means this moment is closed. You may ask another day, in a condition from the open region, and the answer may still be no.

If a cue is really about a bad day, you can wave at chapter 057 and still keep it on this map. A pain flare can be both a bad-day instruction and a safety cue. Write it where you will use it.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 15 minutes.** Three cues in each region, for each of you. Read them. Name one overlap or one difference. Pick the condition and the guard if they are obvious. Skip the long comparison. Three honest cues beat twelve polite ones.

**Standard, 40 minutes.** Both regions filled with actions, situations, and words. A real comparison. One condition scheduled. One guardedness cue agreed as a stop. Each person gets to leave at least one cue unnamed, on purpose, so privacy stays visible.

**Extended, 70 minutes.** The standard map, then a margin for sensory needs, pace, and distance. Add the late-reply word if you spend time apart. Practice one guardedness cue in the room: one of you names it, the other shows what respect looks like in a sentence, and you stop. Do not practice by actually hurting the sore spot. Practice the stop, not the injury. "If I say 'family,' you say 'stopping,' and we change the subject." That rehearsal is enough.

## Going deeper

If the map feels thin, add the words that help and the words that close you, as quotations you are willing to share. "Tell me one thing at a time" may be an opening phrase. "Calm down" may be a closing one. You are allowed to want directness. You are allowed to want a softer on-ramp. Write the one you want, and let your partner write a different one. Asymmetry is the point.

You may also mark public and private versions of the same topic. Some stories can be told at the table with friends in a light way, and the heavier version only in the warm room. That split is a sophisticated kind of safety. It needs no apology.

Stop before the map becomes a list of old injuries. One sore spot, named as a cue, is depth. A chronology of every time you felt unsafe is a different project, and if that chronology includes violence, you are in the wrong chapter.

## Variations and replay

Rewrite the map when life changes the cues: a new housemate, a change in pain, a job that ends late, a season of grief. Date it. Old cues can retire.

Cards work if a shared page feels like too much eye traffic. Each person fills cards, then you place the "I open" cards in one row and the "I guard" cards in another. You can remove a card from the table without explaining it.

On a call, screen-share the page or edit a note together. Cameras optional. If one of you needs more time, fill the regions before the call and use the call only to compare and to choose the one condition and the one guard.

Replay by trying the chosen condition once, then adding a line to the map about whether it did what you hoped. A condition that looked good on paper and felt like a performance in the room gets revised. Respect for the guardedness cue can be replayed whenever it appears. You do not need a new meeting to honor a stop word you already chose.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. Their rooms are an illustration.

Rowan's open region: phones in another room; one question at a time; a booked conversation rather than a sprung one; sitting at an angle. Rowan's guard region: jokes about family when friends are there; "why are you like this"; being asked for a feeling in the first minute through the door; bright overhead light when the topic is serious.

Vale's open region: a walk only if the street is quiet, otherwise the couch, side by side; a snack first; the sentence "we can stop"; texting a hard opener before saying it aloud. Vale's guard region: a rushed "just tell me"; being teased about pausing to think; serious talks in the car; strong perfume in a small room.

They notice they both like side by side, and they both dislike being hurried. They also notice a real difference. Rowan likes a walk as a default. Vale guards walks unless the route is quiet, because noise makes Vale's words late and the lateness feels like failure. They do not average this into "walks are good." They write: quiet walk, maybe; loud walk, no.

The condition they will try: phones in the other room for one conversation this week, sitting at an angle, with permission to stop. The guard they will respect: no family jokes in front of friends, and if either says "not here," the subject changes without a debrief at the party. Rowan says, plainly, that the phone condition does not mean Rowan will bring up the hard topic. Vale says the same. The map ends with two closed doors still allowed to be closed.

## Afterward

Leave the page where you can find it, and where it will not be used as a score.

If you want questions:

- Which cue was easiest to admit, and which did you leave unnamed on purpose?
- Where do your maps differ in a way you will protect rather than fix?
- What does respect look like, in one sentence, for the guardedness cue you chose?
- Did either of you feel pressure to become more open because the map exists?

If the answer to the last question is yes, say so, and take the pressure off in the same minute. The map is information, and a poor invoice.

## Our Story

The shared map can live with this journal or on its own sheet. Private memoir pages are optional to share. A cue you thought of and chose not to write can stay unwritten. Say that if it helps, then let it be ordinary.
