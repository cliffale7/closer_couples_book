## The hook

The job is the first 10 minutes of a hard day: what shows up, what helps, and what makes that window worse. 028, Instructions for Loving Me, is operating notes, warning signs, and maintenance, for ordinary weeks. This page is the day that has already gone wrong.

A hard day rarely introduces itself. It comes in wearing your face and doing something slightly off. One of you reorganizes a drawer that was minding its own business. One of you answers in three-word sentences and then apologizes for the three words. One of you gets sharp about a light, which is a strange hill at 6:40 p.m. unless the day has already spent the polite part of the mind.

The person who loves you meets that behavior first. The explanation, if it arrives, arrives later. This chapter asks each of you to write the behavior down on an ordinary day, and to put beside it the supports that help and the ones that make the hour worse. You are making a current description. You may revise it the next time you learn something. Your partner may follow it as care. They may also say an instruction is more than they can do.

Bring a frayed day, the kind that still lets you get home. Leave danger and self-harm off this page. If someone is at risk, stop and use urgent local help. Do not describe methods of harm here.

## Why it might matter

You have seen each other tired, so silence gets labeled anger, a short tone gets labeled "mad at me," and a pain flare gets labeled mood. Sometimes the label is right. Often it is a story invented to fill a gap. The manual puts the writer's own account in that gap: what the pattern tends to look like, and what they want in the first 10 minutes, which is usually when the other person is deciding whether to talk, touch, fix, or vanish.

Clinicians who work with couples sometimes speak of love maps, bids, and repair, metaphors for staying oriented to each other under strain. That is practitioner language. It is a neighborhood this page lives near. It is a study of nothing on this page, and there is no success rate to borrow. What you can use is plainer. A short written account gives your partner a better first move than a guess, on a day when you may have few spare words.

Two nearby chapters do other jobs. Instructions for Loving Me (028) is a broader, playful manual of needs and upkeep. The Reassurance Menu (029) sorts helps for lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, or disconnected hours. Stay here with the bad-day pattern itself: how you act, what misleads, what helps, and what worsens it.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can write. Paper, a notes app, large type, or a screen reader all work. Keep the lines short enough to read when someone is tired.

Each of you writes your own page. Then you read the other person's page back. You leave their sentences in their voice. A more convenient version, edited by the reader, is a different document.

Choose an ordinary hard day as the subject. A rough meeting. A pain flare. Too much noise. A short night. Grief that is present and still allows this conversation. If the only scenes you can picture are dangerous or violent, this chapter is the wrong room.

Agree that "pause" ends a line, a page, or the sitting. Passing is a complete answer. Changing a prompt is allowed.

A few limits belong on the table before anyone gets noble:

- The page is revocable. Next month's version may be wiser.
- Following it is care. The reader may decline an instruction they cannot carry.
- A line saved for later ammunition has left the spirit of the exercise. "Your sheet says you shut down" is a misuse of a page written in good faith.
- Write the inconvenient truth. A help list no one could actually do is a wish list.
- Rest is a real support. Lying down counts as being in the day with someone.

Distress wears more than one coat. It can look like silence, irritability, a joke with a blade in it, a sudden exit, or a body that hurts. Fair instructions include "do not touch," "do not talk yet," "text me," and "sit in the next room." If you live apart, leave room for a call script and a no-call option. Any touch named on the page can still be declined in the moment.

## The experience

If you are playing the standard version, write the pattern of a hard day under the headings below. About 15 minutes is the limit on that draft. You can write at the same time. Title the page with your name and today's date. One to three short lines under each heading is enough.

1. **Early signs.** What shows up before you have a speech. A quieter voice. A particular sigh. A reach for your phone. A jaw. A way of standing at the sink as if the sink had opinions.
2. **What I do that can mislead you.** The behavior that looks like one story and means another. "I get efficient and bossy, and it can look like fury at you." "I go blank, and it can look like I have left the building."
3. **What helps in the first 10 minutes.** Specific enough to do on a weekday. Tea started in the next room. A text that needs no answer. Lights down. Questions waiting until you ask for them. Company without a plot.
4. **What makes it worse.** Cheerful interrogation. A stack of solutions. Being touched when your skin is loud. Being left with no word at all, if that is your version of worse. A joke at your expense. Being asked to choose dinner while you are still in the doorway.
5. **What is not about you.** The sentence your partner is allowed to keep. "A sharp tone after a shift is usually the shift." "Quiet in the car is often overload, and you did not schedule the overload."
6. **When to give space.** How space looks. A closed door and a time you will reappear. A text: "Quiet room until 8." Space with a return, when you can name one.
7. **When to stay nearby.** The other chair. The next room with the door open. A shared silence. A show neither of you has to follow.

Trade pages. The reader reads the writer's page aloud, or the writer reads and the reader follows along. Then the reader asks one clarifying question. One. "When you say nearby, do you want me in the room or in earshot?" "Does the no-questions rule last 10 minutes, or until you speak?" The writer answers, passes, or revises a line on the spot.

The reader does not bargain the writer's inner life into a handier shape. They may say, "I can do the tea and the quiet. I miss jaw changes on days when I am also fried. Can we use a word?" That is a capacity limit, and it belongs in the talk. Shrink the instruction, or leave it as information the reader will sometimes miss. Missing it is human. The page is a guide, and a missed cue is a moment to repair later, in The Repair Shop (060) if you want that structure. The page is a poor scorecard.

If you want a practice round, use a mild hypothetical after both pages exist. "Suppose I walk in and start cleaning a clean counter. Using my page, what is your first move?" Answer from the page. The writer says whether it caught the instruction. Keep the scene mild. Do not manufacture a live bad day to grade each other.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 15 minutes.** Each of you writes five lines: early sign, what misleads, what helps in the first 10 minutes, what makes it worse, and what is not about you. Trade. Each reader asks one question. Skip the hypothetical. Both halves are still here, the visible pattern and the supports, in smaller handwriting.

**Standard, 40 minutes.** All seven headings, a read-back, one question each, and a short talk about any instruction the reader cannot carry. Revise one line if the talk taught you something. Date the page.

**Extended, 70 minutes.** The standard page, then the mild hypothetical, then a margin note for distance or pain. If you are often apart, add a call script ("I am at my limit; I want 10 quiet minutes, cameras off") and a no-call line ("I will text 'landed' and talk tomorrow"). If pain or overload is common, add where you want your body, and whether speech is available. Close by naming one line you want remembered this month.

## Going deeper

If the first page came out polite, write a smaller note called "The part I skip." This is still an ordinary hard day. You might write, "I pick a fight about the dishes because 'I am disappointed' feels too bare," or "I become extremely reasonable, and the reasonableness keeps you at the door." Share it only if you want it shared. The same rules apply: specific, doable, revocable, and useless as a later exhibit.

You can also mark the page with the clock. Some people can be reached at noon and are gone at 10 p.m. A manual that ignores the hour will be followed badly. Add the hour.

A second pass, weeks later, can record what your partner did that matched the page, and what you wish you had written. Praise the match in one concrete sentence. Edit the page. Skip the performance review.

## Variations and replay

Rewrite when a season changes: a new job, a new pain pattern, a grief, a month of travel. The date at the top lets the old version retire without a speech.

If writing is the easier channel, the whole exchange can stay on the page. Reading aloud is optional. Eye contact is optional. If speech is difficult, the clarifying question goes underneath in writing, and the answer goes on the next line.

At a distance, use a shared note. Agree whether the read-back is a call or a written echo. A written echo is a full version of this chapter. Include the no-call option on purpose, so silence after a hard day has a shape instead of a panic.

After the next ordinary hard day, add one line while it is still recent and still small: what the page got right, and what you wish had been on it. That revision is the replay. The first manual is a draft from memory, and memory flatters.

When the question is wider than a bad day, The Safety Map (062) charts cues that open you or bring your guard up, including on days that are going fine.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are a demonstration, and they are not the two of you.

Vale writes on a Wednesday when nothing is on fire.

Early signs: I answer with "yep" and start wiping counters that are already clean.

What I do that can mislead you: The wiping looks like a verdict on the house, and sometimes on you. It is usually a meeting that went sideways and a body that wants a task.

What helps in the first 10 minutes: Start the kettle. Say, "I am in the kitchen. No questions." Leave my phone alone.

What makes it worse: "What happened?" three times. A hug from behind while my hands are wet and my shoulders are up by my ears. A plan for how I should have handled the meeting.

What is not about you: The counter is a review of my afternoon.

When to give space: If I say "ten," give me 10 minutes with the door open.

When to stay nearby: After that, sit and read. I may talk. I may not.

Rowan reads it back and spends the one question carefully. "Is the kettle for drinking, or is the sound the help?" Vale says, "The sound. I will pour it if I want it." Rowan adds a limit. "I can do the kettle and the quiet. On my own bad days I may still ask one question out of habit. You can point at the page." Vale says that pointing is allowed and that perfection is not the assignment.

They try one hypothetical. Vale mimes the counter. Rowan says, "Kettle. I am in the kitchen. No questions." Vale says, "Yes. And if you forget and ask, I can say 'page' without making it a trial." They stop there. They do not stage a crisis to see if the manual survives it.

## Afterward

Stay with the pages for a few minutes. You do not need a lesson statement.

If you want questions, use these:

- Which line on your own page was the one you almost smoothed over?
- Which line on your partner's page changes your first 10 minutes?
- Which instruction needs a smaller, more possible version?
- Where will the page live so you can find it, and so it will not be waved in an argument?

A shared note, a folder, the back of a cookbook. A manual in a drawer no one opens is handsome stationery.

## Our Story

The pages can live in this journal or on their own sheet. Private memoir pages are optional to share. One of you may keep a line the other never reads. Say that if you need to, then treat it as ordinary.
