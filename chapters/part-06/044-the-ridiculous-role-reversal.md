## The hook

You already do a small impression of each other when you tell a story at dinner. The hands change. A phrase gets quoted with extra varnish. This chapter turns that varnish up until it squeaks, for 2 minutes, inside a scene you both agreed to. Then it hands the floor to the person who was played.

They sort what you did into three piles: accurate, flattering fiction, and that stung. The sort is half the game. Without it, you have a roast, and a roast is a different, worse party.

## Why it might matter

An affectionate impression says you have been paying attention to your style. Style is how you thank a server, how long you look at a menu, the gesture you make when you are stalling. Being recognized in those details can feel different from being praised. Praise says you are wonderful. Recognition says you are particular.

Exaggeration is the ridiculous part, and it needs a fence. You magnify a joke you already share. You do not invent a new sore spot for the sake of the scene. You do not borrow identity, a body, a disability, a family, or a distressed mood and call it comedy. The person portrayed gets the last sort, including the right to say that something stung. What stung is named before anyone explains that it was only a joke. The discussion is not a debate the impersonator wins.

Entry 018, The Silent Conversation, is communication by expression and gesture, with the words set down. Entry 101, The Borrowed Brain Experiment, compares reasoning on a scenario. This chapter is style and manner, deliberately enlarged, then corrected by the person who wears that manner on ordinary days.

## Before you begin

Agree that either of you can stop a portrayal mid-scene. A raised hand, or the word "stop," ends it. Stopping is a success of the rules. You move straight to the sort, or you drop the scene if the portrayed person would rather. No one owes a laugh.

Each of you writes one veto on a slip and shows it before the timer starts. A veto might be a family phrase, a speech pattern, a body habit, a sore mood, an accent, the way pain makes you move. The portrayal leaves the veto out. If it hits the veto anyway, stop. Do not imitate a stutter, a tic, an accent tied to ethnicity or place, or a mobility pattern. Do not perform your partner's anger, sadness, or desire. Do not use their body, their mental-health history, or their family as the material.

Name the inside joke you are willing to see enlarged. A phrase. A pace. A courtesy that runs slightly too long. If you cannot think of a joke, use manners only: how you order, how you pause, how you thank someone. Manners are enough to be funny.

Choose the scene together from the menu below, or invent a mundane one at the same altitude. Both of you will play the same scene as each other, so you are comparing style inside one situation.

If a voice impression is difficult, unwanted, or just not your instrument, write a short monologue in your partner's cadence, or play a single gesture while seated. Trade written dialogue if hearing makes audio hard. Long-distance works on video or as exchanged scripts. Nobody has to stand.

## The experience

**Scenes.** Pick one.

1. Ordering dinner while hungry and still trying to be polite to the menu.
2. Narrating a weekday morning from the alarm to the door.
3. Telling a harmless party story about a lost umbrella.
4. Choosing a film, and taking slightly too long to do it.
5. Returning a coffee order that is a little wrong, with excessive courtesy.
6. Explaining a simple recipe while the pan is already hot.

**The portrayal, 2 minutes.** The portrayer "is" the other person inside the scene. Seated. One gesture or one phrase, already an inside joke, gets enlarged. The rest can be ordinary. You may talk to an invisible server, an invisible door, or the actual partner as if they were the audience at that party. You may not leave the scene for a confession, a conflict, or a sexual caricature.

The person being portrayed watches without correcting. Laughing is allowed. A straight face is allowed. Notes are allowed if you want them for the sort. Interruptions wait, unless you need to stop the scene.

**The monologue option.** Fill this shape in your partner's voice, on paper, about 120 words, then read it or hand it over:

"When I am in this scene, I tend to ___. The phrase I reach for is ___. What my hands do is ___. The story I think I am in is ___."

Exaggerate one of those four lines. Leave the veto untouched. Reading time still caps at 2 minutes.

**The sort, about 4 minutes, and it is required.** The portrayed person has the floor. They sort lines into the three piles, in this order if anything stung, and in any order if nothing did.

- That stung. Name it in one sentence. "When you stretched my pause into a full minute, it felt like a comment on how slow I am." The portrayer listens. They apologize in one sentence, or they offer a redo of that single beat. They do not explain the joke until they are invited. What stung is information for an apology or a redo. It is not material for a second joke.
- Accurate. "That is me, and I claim it." One or two examples are enough.
- Flattering fiction. "That was kinder than the truth," or "I wish I were that polite to bread."

If nothing stung, the portrayed person still touches the category. They say, "Nothing stung. I checked." Skipping the category because the laugh was loud is how the roast sneaks out the side door. The check is the game.

The portrayer may ask, after the sort, "Is there a line you want retired?" They may not argue a sting into a compliment. If a redo is offered and accepted, it lasts 20 seconds, and then the sort stands.

**Switch.** Same scene, reversed roles. The second portrayal does not "correct" the first. It is a new impression, followed by a new sort of equal length. Both people portray. Both people are sorted. That balance is the chapter.

**If it snags.** The scene slides toward a real annoyance: stop, name the slide, and either return to the umbrella or end. The laugh is huge and the sort is about to be skipped: set a second timer for the sort and let it run. Someone feels too seen: they can retire a line, keep the accurate pile, and decline the redo. Someone cannot find an inside joke: manners, pace, and courtesy are a complete costume.

**What you might print.** Two veto slips. One scene card with the six options. Three small cards labeled Accurate, Flattering fiction, and That stung, to place on the table as a sorting mat. A timer with a gentle end, not an alarm that sounds like a scolding. No score. Recognition is not a point.

**Ending.** The game ends when both sorts are finished, including both sting checks. If you want a last beat, each of you may offer one accurate line you liked hearing, and the other person may accept it or wave it off. Then the coats, metaphorically, go back to their own sleeves.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 15 minutes.** One scene. Each portrayal lasts 60 seconds. Each sort lasts 2 minutes. Veto slips still happen. Both directions still happen. This is a sketch, not a single impersonation of one partner while the other hosts.

**Standard, 30 minutes.** Vetoes, one scene, 2 minutes of portrayal and about 4 minutes of sort, each direction. Leave a minute between directions so the second portrayer is not answering the first sketch like a rival.

**Extended, 55 minutes.** Play the standard game. Take a short break. Choose a second scene from the menu. Before you start it, each of you may keep the same veto or write a new one. Use the monologue for one scene and the spoken impression for the other, if you want both instruments. The sorts stay the same length as the performances, or longer. Extended does not mean a sharper joke. It means a second situation, so a mannerism can turn out to be about menus and not about mornings.

## Going deeper

Pick one accurate line from the sort and ask, only with permission, "Where did that habit come from?" The answer might be a job, a parent, a crowded house, a love of precision. Hear it. Do not turn the origin into a new impression on the same night.

You may also ask, "Which flattering fiction would you like me to keep as a private compliment, and which one should I drop?" Some exaggerated kindness is a gift. Some of it is a portrait of a person they are tired of pretending to be.

Leave real conflict in its own chapter. If the sting opens a recurring tenderness, apologize, retire the bit, and stop. You are not in a therapy exercise where partners switch positions inside a live fight. You are two people who borrowed each other's coats and then checked the fit.

## Variations and replay

**Gesture only.** 30 seconds. One seated gesture, enlarged. Then a full sort, which may be longer than the gesture. This is a good variation when voices are tired or when impressions of speech feel risky.

**Script exchange.** Write the monologue in advance. Exchange sealed. Read silently. Sort on a call without performing at all.

**Party-story limit.** Use only scene 3, the lost umbrella, so the content stays harmlessly plotless and the comedy has to live in the telling.

**Replay.** New scene, same vetoes unless you lift one. Notice which accurate lines have become dear. Notice which joke has expired. An expired joke can be thanked and buried. Burying it is a form of attention too.

The Great Debate of Absolutely Nothing, entry 045, is the chapter for formal seriousness about empty subjects. Come to this one when the subject is each other's manner, and the correction belongs to the person portrayed.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you.

They choose scene 1, ordering dinner. Vale's veto slip says "my shoulder when it hurts." Rowan's veto slip says "my mother's way of saying 'well.'" Those slips sit on the table, face up.

Vale portrays Rowan, seated, menu in hand. "Good evening. I will have the soup, provided the soup understands its responsibilities. If the menu disagrees, I am prepared to negotiate with it for a reasonable period. I will also thank the bread for waiting."

Rowan sorts. "Nothing stung. I checked. Accurate: I do interview the menu, and I do thank people a half-second longer than the moment requires. Flattering fiction: I am not that polite to bread. I would like the bread line retired, not because it hurt, because it makes me into a mascot."

Vale says, "Retired." Vale does not explain the joke.

Then Rowan portrays Vale in the same booth. Rowan taps the menu once, orders the first hearty thing, and adds, "And whatever you would eat if you were cold." Rowan almost reaches for a shoulder shrug, remembers the veto, and lets the hands stay on the table.

Vale sorts. "That stung, a little: 'whatever you would eat if you were cold' felt like a portrait of me as someone who cannot choose. Please retire it." Rowan says, "I am sorry. Retired." Only then does Vale continue. "Accurate: I decide fast when I am hungry. Flattering fiction: I am not that considerate of the server's imaginary inner life. The fast decision, you can keep."

They do not do a second joke about the sting. They eat, in the fiction of the example, something hearty. The bread is not thanked again.

## Afterward

Say out loud which lines are retired. Retired lines do not become nicknames on the way to the car. If you want, each of you can repeat one accurate line you are happy to claim. Claiming it makes the recognition mutual.

If a sting is still warm, let the apology be the last word on it tonight. You can ask tomorrow whether the apology landed. You do not have to settle it by being funnier.

The game gives nobody permission to touch, to diagnose, or to perform the other person for an audience who was not in the room. If you retell the scene later, retell the sort as well. The sort is the part that makes the ridiculousness kind.

## Our Story

Write the retired lines if you want them remembered as retired. The private pages are optional to share. A veto slip can be dated and kept, especially if the same veto should travel into the next time you play.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- When I was portrayed, what was accurate, what was flattering fiction, and what, if anything, stung?
- What did I need after I named a sting, or after I confirmed there was none?
partner_b:
- When I did the portraying, which inside joke did I enlarge, and what did the sort ask me to retire?
- Which gesture or phrase of my partner's felt like recognition I am glad I noticed?
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```

## Notes

Affectionate impressions are an ordinary comic custom among people who know each other. This chapter is that custom with a required sort. No research finding is claimed. Being imitated is not treated here as empathy training, a look into the unconscious, or a clinical role-play. Rowan and Vale are fictional. Any sample dialogue is an illustration, not a script you must perform.
