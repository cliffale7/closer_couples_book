## The hook

End on one trait carried back from a shared plot. Keep the names you already call each other. Change the job, the gravity, and the century. In one sentence you will choose a world. In about 10 minutes you will find out how these versions meet, what they find attractive, and what small disaster they repair together. Then you will come back to the room and name that trait in the person who is actually here.

Without the plot, this is only a costume. Without the return, it is only collaborative fiction. 039, The Romantic Time Machine, ends on the quality that survives a different meeting, and it keeps your real selves in the costume. This chapter wants the mishap, and then one trait brought home. The misadventure should be funny or tender. It should be solvable. It is not a coded retelling of a real injury, and it is not a chance to build a better spouse.

## Why it might matter

Plotting is a low-stakes way to watch which traits you already trust. When the cake collapses, the map is upside down, or the ticket has vanished, the repair inside the story can borrow something true: calm hands, a memory for steps, humor when the plan frays, the willingness to ask a clear question. You feel that trait in costume first, then you name it in ordinary life, where it already lives.

Attraction, in the story, is specific. Not "they are perfect." A way of apologizing to a tray of buns. Letters that lean forward. A voice on a radio that works only at dusk. Specific attraction is more interesting than a halo, and it gives you language you can bring home if you want it.

Entry 039, The Romantic Time Machine, keeps your real selves and asks what would still draw you if you met under other circumstances. This chapter changes the versions and wants a plot, including a mishap. Entry 104, The Two-Person Civilization, invents a society's principles and then stresses them. This chapter is a couple's caper, not a constitution. Entry 041 tells true stories with one beautiful fiction beside them. Here, the whole scene is invented, and the truth arrives only at the end, as a trait you recognize.

## Before you begin

You need a timer and a willingness to trade sentences. Paper is optional. Oral telling and alternating written paragraphs are equal. Cameras can be off. Low light is welcome. Long-distance is a natural fit. No props are required, and no one has to act the disaster out. Language can be plain. Mythic diction is not the price of admission.

Either of you may reject a world. "Another world" is a complete sentence. Draw again. If you reject three, take the undersea library and make the plot very small. Do not cast a real third person as a villain. If the story needs a stranger, invent one and give them a gentle exit.

Romance in the story is fictional. It is not a request to reenact a meeting, a kiss, or anything else. Either of you can steer a beat toward tenderness and comedy and away from sex. If a beat starts to accuse the real partner ("and then, like always, you forgot the tickets"), stop and rewind the sentence until the fault belongs to the fictional situation. A lost ticket is a situation. A character indictment is a different conversation, and it does not belong in the caper.

Use the first names you use for each other. If you would rather not put those names into a story, initials are enough. Add a role each. The role is a job in the world, not a personality transplant.

## The experience

**Choose the world, one sentence.** Read the menu and pick one, or write a sentence of your own at the same scale. The world should be describable before anyone falls in love or breaks anything.

1. An undersea library where the books are sealed in glass and the librarians negotiate with fish.
2. A failing bakery during a festival, with one working oven and a line around the block.
3. A small research outpost on a windy plateau where the radio works only at dusk.
4. A night train whose dining car serves only breakfast.
5. A lighthouse supply shop that also rents umbrellas during storms.
6. A map room in a city that moves its streets on Thursdays.

Reject any world whose joke depends on oppression, cruelty, or a real harm. Reject any world that requires combat. The menu above is built to avoid those plots. If your own sentence drifts there, thank it and take the bakery.

**Assign roles.** Keep your names. Add a job that could exist in the sentence. Oven-minder. Window-letterer. Dusk-radio technician. Breakfast conductor. Umbrella clerk. Thursday cartographer. Say the full title once, in the name you use: "I am ____, window-letterer." Then keep using your names. The illustration later in this chapter is fictional. It is not the role you play.

**Three beats, about 10 minutes, trading sentences.** Set a timer. You are aiming to arrive at all three beats, not to finish a novel.

1. How these versions meet.
2. What attracts them. Each of you should say what your own alternate notices. Attraction can be competence, humor, a moral choice, a voice, a kindness. It does not have to be a body, and it does not have to be mutual in the same way.
3. A misadventure they get through together, with a repair inside the fiction.

If the story stalls, use this handoff, which is enough to finish:

- One of you says how you meet.
- The other says what they notice.
- The first breaks something small.
- The second gets you out, using a trait the real partner actually has.

**Misadventures that stay the right size.** Pick one if you are staring at the timer.

- A lost ticket, found in a book that was shelved by color.
- A collapsed cake, rebuilt into something shorter and honest.
- A map read upside down until someone starts again from the title.
- A radio that works only when someone hums the first note.
- Two invitations for the same hour, solved by combining them into one peculiar event.
- An order written in butter, legible only when the page is tilted toward the oven light.

The repair should borrow a real trait: patience with instructions, memory for names, calm hands, humor under mild chaos, a good eye for the next step, the ability to ask a stranger one clear question. Name the trait lightly inside the story. You will name it again, plainly, at the end.

**The return, required.** When the timer ends, even if the cake is only half rebuilt, stop the fiction. Each of you tells the other, "One trait your alternate shares with you, the person in this room, is ___." If the story made an alternate into an improved partner, correct it in this sentence. The trait should be one the real person already has, not one you wish they would develop. If you cannot find a shared trait, say so, and name a detail you liked in the fiction that you are willing to leave in the fiction.

**Fatigue cut inside the rules.** Hit the three beats in 5 minutes: one sentence per beat from each of you, then the return. The short version of the whole chapter is built on this cut.

**What you might print.** A half-page with the six worlds, the handoff lines, and the return sentence with a blank. No map to follow, no objects to gather, no costume. If you write the story, alternating paragraphs on one page is enough. Date it if you want it kept.

**Ending.** The game ends when both return sentences have been spoken. Close by repeating the two traits in ordinary language, without the job titles. "Calm hands. Reads the instructions." Those phrases, or whatever yours are, are the souvenir. The undersea library can close.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 15 minutes.** Pick a world with almost no debate. Use the 5-minute, one-sentence-per-beat version, including the handoff if you stall. Speak both return lines. This still has a meeting, a spark, a mishap, and a way back. It is the right length when language feels heavy.

**Standard, 35 minutes.** A minute or two on the world and the roles, about 10 minutes of traded sentences, then the return, then a few minutes to say which beat you liked telling. If a beat was thin, you may add three sentences to it before the return, not after. The return stays last.

**Extended, 60 minutes.** Play a full caper. After the return, take a break, choose a second world, and play a shorter caper of about 8 minutes. The second misadventure must use a different real trait, so you are not telling the same compliment in a new hat. End by choosing which of the two stories, if either, is worth writing down in a page. Writing it down is optional. The traits are not.

## Going deeper

A deeper round keeps the three beats and adds a constraint: the misadventure is solved without a special skill the alternate just acquired. The repair has to use something the real person could do this week. That constraint prevents the story from handing your partner a fantasy upgrade.

You may also retell the attraction beat more slowly. Each of you adds two concrete details. What did they notice first? What did they respect? Leave the details in the fiction unless you want to offer one of them as a real compliment, in which case offer it in your own voice after the return, where it can be accepted or declined.

Do not deepen the plot by adding a villain who resembles a real person, a sexual scene you expect to reenact, or a conflict from your actual history with the serial numbers filed off. If that material shows up, say "that belongs to us, not to them," and give the alternates their lost ticket back.

## Variations and replay

**Paragraph pass.** Set a 3-minute timer three times, once per beat. Each of you writes a short paragraph during the timer. Read the meeting, then the attraction, then the mishap. The return is spoken.

**Single-sentence world from a hat.** Write three original worlds on slips, draw one, and allow one rejection. A good original world is still one sentence and still free of a real wound.

**Low-plot evening.** If invention feels like work, use the handoff exactly, then sit with the return. A complete game can be four sentences and two traits.

**Replay.** New world, same names, different trait. Over time you may collect a small list of traits you have been willing to call true. That list is a nicer artifact than a single perfect story. If a world stops being fun, retire it. The bakery will not be offended. It was already failing.

The Romantic Time Machine is the chapter for real selves in an altered meeting. The Two-Person Civilization is the chapter for laws and pressure. Use this one when you want a caper and a way home.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you. The traits in the example were chosen so the tone is obvious. They are not a hint about your household.

World: a failing bakery during a festival, one working oven, a line around the block.

"I am Rowan, oven-minder." "I am Vale, window-letterer."

Meeting. Vale is hired at noon because the regular letterer has gone to a quieter festival. Rowan is already apologizing to a tray of buns that rose with too much ambition.

Attraction. Vale's alternate notices that Rowan apologizes to the buns as if they had feelings worth respecting. Rowan's alternate notices that Vale's letters lean slightly forward, as if the words were eager to be read from the street.

Misadventure. The festival cake collapses into a sweet landslide. They have been reading the butter-smeared recipe from the bottom. Vale turns the card and starts from the top. In this illustration, that move is Vale's habit of reading the steps when everyone else has begun to improvise. Rowan rebuilds the cake with calm hands into something shorter and honest. They sell it as a festival pavilion. The line around the block decides that pavilions are a known dessert.

Return. Vale says, "One trait your alternate shares with you, the person in this room, is calm hands." Rowan says, "One trait your alternate shares with you is that you read the instructions when the rest of us are already guessing." They drop the bakery titles. Calm hands. Reads the instructions. The fictional oven cools.

A stall, also fictional, from another evening: they chose the night train and went silent after the meeting. They used the handoff. Vale said they met when the dining car ran out of forks at dawn. Rowan said they noticed that Vale asked the cook one clear question instead of a speech. Vale broke the plot by losing both tickets in a teapot. Rowan got them out by remembering the car numbers, a memory-for-detail trait Rowan actually has in the illustration. The story took 4 minutes. It still counted.

## Afterward

Say the two traits once more if they pleased you, and leave the world in the world. If one trait felt like pressure ("you are the person who always fixes things"), revise it before you sleep. "Calm hands when a cake falls" is kinder and truer than "responsible for every disaster."

If you want to keep the story, write a half page while it is fresh. If you want to keep only the traits, write those and let the fish and the festival go.

The caper is not evidence of destiny, compatibility, or an unresolved fight. It is a story you built, plus two qualities you were willing to recognize. Recognition can be thanked. It does not have to be repaid in plot.

## Our Story

The shared page can hold the world sentence, the three beats in miniature, and the two traits. Private pages are optional to share. A doodle of a collapsed cake is a legitimate memento. Date it if it stays.

- How did our alternates meet, and what did mine find attractive in yours?

- What misadventure did we repair, and which real trait did the repair borrow?

- What we discovered

## Notes

Collaborative make-believe, bakers and outposts and night trains included, is a folk form of play. This chapter uses that form for a three-beat caper and a return to a real trait. No research finding is claimed. Shared storytelling is not offered as narrative therapy, a compatibility test, or proof of destiny. Rowan and Vale are fictional, and their bakery is fictional too.
