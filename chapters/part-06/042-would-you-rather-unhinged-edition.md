## The hook

Some choices are too silly to be advice and too pointed to be small talk. You take them anyway. One of you reads two invented options. Both of you choose in secret, reveal at the same moment, and give a single sentence about why. Unhinged, here, means surprising. It means a goose with opinions, a year of only instrumental music, a last good peach with a guest in the doorway. It means you get to be strange on purpose.

It does not mean cruel. It does not mean a sexual dare, a jab at a body, or a question aimed at a real wound. The answer is hypothetical. You may not cash it later as consent, as a promise, or as a confession.

## Why it might matter

A forced choice is a toy. The reason sentence is where a person actually stands. "The unicycle, because the float would make me responsible for waving" tells you something a biography rarely bothers to include: how they handle dignity, inconvenience, and an audience.

Four tones keep the evening from collapsing into one joke. Ludicrous logistics let you laugh before anyone is brave. A revealing pair asks for self-knowledge at a safe altitude. A moral pair offers a value collision with no tragic victim and no real plan for harm. A romantic pair spends comfort or time in imagination, and stays in imagination unless you deliberately make a plan another day.

The third door is part of the mechanism. Either of you may reject the frame. A clever question can still be a bad frame, and saying so is a legal move, not a failed sense of play.

This is a game of paired hypotheticals, including silly ones. Entry 082, The Moral Gray Area, is a conversation about competing values with no obvious right answer. Entry 085, What Would You Do If…?, considers unexpected dilemmas in a life. Play those when you want the ethics at full size. Play this when you also want the goose.

## Before you begin

You need a way to choose privately. A coin under a hand works. Heads means the first option, the one before "or." Tails means the second. If coins feel fiddly, write L or R on a scrap, place it face down, and turn both scraps over together. On a call, type the letter into a note and send on a count of three.

Decide who speaks the reason first. A fair rule: the person seated on the left speaks first on odd cards, and the other person speaks first on even cards. On a call, alternate by card. Speaking first keeps the second reason from becoming an echo.

Agree on a small limit before you start. Standard play is eight cards, two from each suit. You may stop earlier. Discarding a card is allowed before the coins go down. A discarded card is not a forfeit, and it does not hand the turn to the other person's preference. Passing after you reveal is also allowed: say "pass" instead of a reason. The choice you showed can be forgotten. It is not evidence.

If the night is loud, tired, or raw, stay in the ludicrous suit. Hearing and reading are equal. Pass the options in text if one of you wants them in print. Large type helps. No one has to stand, travel, or touch.

A hypothetical yes is not consent to do the thing, spend the money, change a habit, or be touched. Romantic options stay imaginary unless, later and separately, you both make a plan in ordinary language.

## The experience

Shuffle each suit, or have one person read the numbers while the other says "stop." Draw so you do not always play the first card printed in the book.

**A turn.**

1. Read the pair aloud, slowly enough that both options arrive intact. Point to which one is heads or Left, and which one is tails or Right.
2. Sit with it in silence for up to 30 seconds. Choose privately.
3. Count "three" and reveal.
4. Each of you gives one sentence of reason, in the order you agreed. One sentence is the rule. A second sentence is a luxury you may skip. You are not building a case.
5. If you want the third door, say "Third door" before your reason. Then reject the frame in one sentence, or offer a third option. You still listen to your partner's choice. Their choice does not stick to you.
6. Optional, if you matched: the person who spoke second may add a condition, still one sentence. "Only if the goose pays rent." Conditions are jokes or clarifications. They are not negotiations you have agreed to live by.

Then draw the next card. Do not stack questions until someone is too tired to pass. The stop is part of the design. When the planned number is done, stop even if the deck is still funny.

**What a third door sounds like.** "Third door: both options assume we want a performance." "Third door: I already live a version of the first, and the question is tired." "Third door: I want the walk and the evening, and I refuse the scarcity." All three are complete moves.

**What you might print.** 24 cards, about four by six inches, one pair on each face. Suit name at the top. The two options labeled Left and Right. Footer on every card: "Third door is open. A choice is hypothetical." Backs show only the suit, so you can draw blind. 14-point type or larger. Four piles on the table. No score sheet. A match is interesting. It is not a compatibility result, and it does not reveal character. Do not total the matches. If you are curious how often you landed together, you may say the number once, as a remark, and then leave it.

**Ending.** After the last card, each of you names one choice that should stay imaginary. That sentence seals the game. If a card made you want a real plan, say "I might want to talk about this later," and write the topic on a slip. Do not have the real conversation inside the game. The slip can wait for another night, or it can be thrown away.

### Ludicrous logistics

These six pairs are original to this book. They are the absurd suit, and they are ready to play.

1. Would you rather live in a house where every door is a curtain, or a house where every curtain is a locked door?
2. Would you rather commute by unicycle with a dignified basket, or on a parade float that travels only at walking speed?
3. Would you rather carry a phone that speaks only in weather forecasts, or own a kettle that sings a full opera when it boils?
4. Would you rather share the home with a well-mannered goose who has opinions, or with a houseplant that must be read to every night?
5. Would you rather eat breakfast as soup, using a tiny fork, or eat dinner as a sandwich the size of a hardcover book?
6. Would you rather wake each Sunday to find the furniture politely rearranged by an unseen helper, or discover each Monday that your two shoes are different sizes?

### Revealing, and still safe

Self-knowledge at a humane volume. Either of you may discard any of these.

7. Would you rather have a year of only instrumental music, or a year of only live conversation at dinner?
8. Would you rather be known for your competence and rarely asked how you feel, or be known for your feelings and rarely asked to fix anything?
9. Would you rather keep one hobby entirely unseen, or share every hobby and lose the private one?
10. Would you rather spend a week saying every small appreciation out loud, or spend a week receiving appreciation without deflecting it?
11. Would you rather wake with the sun and miss the morning's noise, or stay up late and own the quiet after midnight?
12. Would you rather be the person who remembers everyone's drink order, or the person who remembers the sentence someone did not finish?

### Morally complicated

Value collisions. No tragic victim. No real plan to harm anyone. If a card brushes an actual dilemma you are living, discard it and say "too close."

13. Would you rather tell a friend their new haircut is "brave" when you dislike it, or stay warmly quiet and let them enjoy the mirror?
14. Would you rather spend the last good peach on a guest, or save it for the person who bought it and serve the guest something else?
15. Would you rather admit you broke the ugly mug everyone pretends to treasure, or buy a quiet replacement and let the mystery stand?
16. Would you rather give up the better seat on a long ride, or keep it because you are the one who gets queasy?
17. Would you rather correct a small public mistake that is making someone look clever, or let the cleverness stand because the correction would embarrass them?
18. Would you rather do a promised favor you dread on the only free afternoon, or ask to move it and offer a better hour?

### Romantic, and still imaginary

These cost comfort or time inside the fiction. They do not assume a script, a wedding, or a shared address. If you live apart, "home" means wherever you each start the evening.

19. Would you rather keep a standing walk on Tuesdays in any weather, or protect one carefully planned evening a month?
20. Would you rather learn a dance badly together for six weeks, or learn one favorite album well enough to talk about it for an hour?
21. Would you rather trade one hour of solo screen time for a shared puzzle on most nights, or keep the hour and guard one unhurried morning each weekend?
22. Would you rather leave a three-line note on ordinary Tuesdays, or cook one meal a week for pleasure rather than for logistics?
23. Would you rather take the long way from where you usually part, twice a week, or stay in and keep a 20-minute ritual that uses no screen?
24. Would you rather let your partner plan a surprise afternoon inside a budget you both already set, or plan it together and give up the surprise?

## Three ways to play

**Short, 15 minutes.** Four cards, all from the ludicrous suit. Reasons stay one sentence. Skip the romantic and moral suits. This is the right cut for sensory overload, a short evening, or a mood that wants nonsense and nothing else.

**Standard, 35 minutes.** Two cards from each suit, eight in all. Read, choose, reveal, one sentence each. Use the third door whenever the frame fails. End with the sealing line: one choice that stays imaginary.

**Extended, 70 minutes.** Play 12 cards, three from each suit. After the eighth, pause and each rewrite one card you disliked into a fairer pair. Play the two rewrites with the same reveal rules. Rewriting is part of the fun. It is also how you teach the deck your actual boundaries. Close by naming the imaginary choice, and, if you wrote a "later" slip, decide whether it lives in a pocket or in the recycling.

## Going deeper

A deeper round uses the revealing and romantic suits only, and it lengthens the reason from one sentence to three. The first sentence is the choice. The second is the cost you noticed. The third is whether the cost is interesting or merely annoying. You still reveal together, so nobody's longer speech becomes a lecture the other must survive.

You may ask one follow-up: "What would make the option you refused into a yes?" The answer stays hypothetical. If it starts to sound like a scheduled promise, move it to the later slip.

Do not deepen this game by pressing on a wound, by adding sexual stakes, or by treating a match as proof that you are the same kind of person. Sameness is not the prize. A clearly loved difference is often the better story. If you want dilemmas with no game clock, use The Moral Gray Area or What Would You Do If…? on a night when absurdity would get in the way.

## Variations and replay

**Single suit night.** All eight cards from one suit. A ludicrous night and a romantic night are different parties. Keep the third door in both.

**Silent reasons.** Write the one-sentence reason, then read them aloud together. Useful when one of you thinks better on paper, and useful at a distance.

**House rule card.** Once per night, either of you may replace a printed pair with one you invent on the spot, using the same tone as the suit you are in. Read it, then give the other person a veto before any coin goes down. A veto discards the house pair with no replacement penalty.

**Replay.** The deck has 24 pairs so a second night can avoid the greatest hits. When a pair goes stale, retire it. Add a new one in the same tone if you want the pile to stay full. Retire any pair that starts to humiliate a body, an identity, or a real argument you are actually having.

If the moral suit stops feeling like play, you have learned something useful. Leave that suit in the box for a while. The ludicrous suit will still be there, wearing its ridiculous hat.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you.

They draw card 4. Rowan covers heads. Vale covers tails. On three, Rowan has chosen the goose. Vale has chosen the houseplant.

Rowan, one sentence: "The goose can be negotiated with, and I respect a creature who states its opinions at the door."

Vale, one sentence: "I will read to a plant, because a plant does not follow me into the bathroom to continue the argument."

They do not total the split. They move on.

Later they draw card 7, the year of instrumental music or the year of live dinner conversation. Both coins show heads. Vale speaks second and adds a condition: "Only if percussion counts, and only if breakfast is allowed to be quiet." Rowan says, "Third door on the condition. I want some dinners with talking and some without a law." The third door closes the card. Nobody has agreed to a year of anything.

On card 19, Vale says "Third door" immediately. "I want the Tuesday walk and the planned evening. The question pretends we only get one kind of time." Rowan had chosen the walk, explains in one sentence that weather makes a plan feel real, and then writes nothing on a later slip. The walk remains imaginary. They get up to find out whether the actual kettle sings. It does not. This disappoints neither of them very much.

## Afterward

If you are still quoting the goose at the sink, the game worked. If a card left a real wish sitting on the table, you already have a way to carry it: the later slip, in plain language, outside the rules. Wishes can wait. They do not have to be paid tonight.

Tell each other which suit you would play again, and which suit you would shelve. Shelving is information, not a disappointment. A night that stayed in the ludicrous suit is a complete night.

Nothing you chose under a coin is permission for the morning. If you want the puzzle, the note, or the long way, ask for it in a new conversation, where a yes can be about the real week and a no can be about the real week too.

## Our Story

The private lines are for each of you. Sharing them is optional. A pocket full of later slips can be taped here, dated, and marked "imaginary" or "maybe a real plan." Either marking is allowed. Neither marking is a promise by itself.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- Which suit was the most fun to choose in, and which suit made me reach for the third door?
- What did my partner's reason sentence show that the coin by itself could not?
partner_b:
- Where did my own choice surprise me once I heard the reason out loud?
- Which of my partner's answers should stay play, and which, if any, do I want to discuss later as a separate plan?
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
