## The hook

The moon has been showing off again, and someone must answer for it. You will argue opposite sides of a proposition that cannot matter, with exaggerated seriousness, for about 3 minutes. A coin decides who defends the moon. When the timer ends, you both put the opinion down in your ordinary voice and say that you do not hold it.

The seriousness is a costume. The content is nothing. Soup. Socks. Spoons. Tuesday. If a joke proposition is actually a complaint about your partner, the game has slipped, and the right move is to stop.

## Why it might matter

Shared absurdity is a cooperative game wearing the clothes of a fight. You practice picking up a position you did not choose, decorating it with fake evidence, and then abandoning it on purpose. Abandoning it is the closing move, and it is the part that keeps the joke from following you into the kitchen as a barb.

There is a particular pleasure in gravity spent on nothing. A definition, a fictional survey of spoons, a grave warning about the future of cereal: these are tools borrowed from real debate and used where they cannot do harm. You get the music of argument without a grievance.

Entry 059, The Gentle Disagreement, is where a manageable real disagreement goes, with warmth and curiosity. This chapter is allowed to sound serious only because the subject is empty. It is not a warm-up that slides into an unresolved fight. Entry 086, The Unpopular Opinion Club, is for actual opinions you hold and rarely air. If you truly care about the topic, it does not belong on these slips.

## Before you begin

You need six motion slips, a coin, a timer, and a little paper for notes. Sitting is the whole stage. There is no podium. On a call, read the motion aloud or paste it in text. If the room is easily overloaded, skip applause and keep the seriousness quiet. A grave whisper is still grave. Hearing differences are handled by text. One motion is a complete game if you are tired.

Either of you may throw out a proposition that lands on a real wound. Discarding is a success. Stopping a round mid-sentence is a success. You do not have to prove you can take a joke.

The proposition must be empty of real grievance. It cannot be about your partner's habits, body, family, work, or politics, and it cannot be a household chore in a funny hat. It cannot require an example from your actual week. If the best argument you can think of is something that happened on Thursday between the two of you, you are in the wrong sport.

No one is scored for intellect, persuasion, or rank. If you want a token for committed absurdity, use a piece of string as a ribbon. The ribbon is returned at the end. Nobody leaves the room wearing a title.

## The experience

**Draw.** Put the six motions in a bowl, a hat, or a face-down pile. Draw one. Read it aloud. If either of you says "too close," bury it and draw again. If you bury three, stop for the night and play another time. The motions are trying to be nothing. Let them.

**Assign sides with a coin.** One of you flips it. If you both reach for the coin, the person who did not draw the motion flips. Heads: the flipper defends the motion. Tails: the flipper opposes it. The other person takes the remaining side. You can end up sincerely attacking socks. That is the assignment working. You are not stuck defending a real pet peeve, because the motions do not contain one.

**Notes, 30 seconds.** Each of you writes two serious points. Use the footholds printed under the motion if you go blank, then add one of your own. A point may be:

- a definition that favors your side,
- fake evidence, clearly fake ("a survey of spoons," "the minutes of a cloud"),
- a grave warning about what the other side will cause.

Ban anecdotes from your actual week, your families, and the news. If a real proper noun wants to enter, show it the door.

**The debate, about 3 minutes.** Set a timer. The affirmative speaks for 70 seconds. The negative speaks for 70 seconds. If you want rebuttal, add 20 seconds each, and stop when the timer ends even if the warning about cereal is unfinished. Address the motion, not the person. Use your debate voice: formal, committed, slightly too dignified. Volume is optional. Interruptions are out. You may say "the honorable opposition." You may not say "you always."

**Applause, optional.** When the timer ends, you may applaud the more committed absurdity. Commitment means they stayed inside the bit, used a straight face, and did not reach for a real complaint. If you cannot agree, applaud both. If sound is too much, a silent nod counts. This applause is not a ruling that one of you is smarter.

**Abandonment, required.** Both of you, in an ordinary voice, not the debate voice, say this sentence: "I do not hold this position." Say it even if, by some accident, you do think soup is a beverage. Inside this game, the sentence means the bit is over. The costume comes off.

**The glad list, required.** Each of you names one real topic you are glad was not on the card. Name it in a few words. Do not open it. Do not comfort it into a second debate. "I am glad the card was not about the dishes." "I am glad it was not about my family." Then leave those topics where they are. If the game has stirred an actual disagreement, stop playing and decide whether you want The Gentle Disagreement on another day. Do not convert this table into that one.

**If a joke turns into a barb.** Stop. Say, "This has become a real complaint." The other person does not argue you out of that sentence. Tear the motion in half, or close the note, and do something plainly friendly that is not a debate: water, a window, a minute of quiet. Ending is the skill.

**What you might print.** Six slips, large type, one motion each, with the affirmative foothold and the negative foothold underneath. A small card that reads, in ordinary type, "I do not hold this position." You can put it on the table and turn it face up when the timer ends, as a visual cue that the voice has to change. No score sheet. The string ribbon, if you use one, has a home in a pocket and a duty to be returned.

**Ending.** The round ends when both abandonment lines have been spoken and both glad-list topics have been named and left closed. If you play a second motion, it is a new round with a new coin. It is not a chance to answer the first debate more cuttingly. When you are finished for the night, the last thing said in a debate voice should already be behind you. The last thing said in the room can be ordinary. "Tea?" is a perfect closing argument.

### Six motions

These motions are original examples, written so you can play without inventing a single one. Footholds are optional handles. You may ignore them and build your own false evidence.

**1. This house believes soup is a beverage.**
Affirmative foothold: define a beverage as any liquid a person can drink from a mug when a spoon has left the room, and cite a fictional survey in which spoons reported feeling unemployed.
Negative foothold: define soup as a food that requires a shoreline, and warn that beverage status will encourage cereal to file the same petition.

**2. This house believes socks deserve opinions.**
Affirmative foothold: define an opinion as a consistent preference, and submit the testimony of a left sock that has refused a sandal since April, in an April that does not need to be yours.
Negative foothold: define opinion as something that can be revised in conversation, and warn that consulting socks will delay every departure until winter.

**3. This house believes the moon is showing off.**
Affirmative foothold: define showing off as appearing at parties uninvited and looking better than the lamps, and enter into evidence a fictional guest list kept by the tide.
Negative foothold: define the moon as a rock with a schedule, and warn that flattery will make it late.

**4. This house believes spoons are the only honest utensil.**
Affirmative foothold: define honesty as the willingness to hold soup without stabbing it, and quote the minutes of a fork who confessed to drama.
Negative foothold: define honesty as fitness for purpose, and warn that a spoon-only kitchen will be helpless before a steak, a steak that remains theoretical.

**5. This house believes Tuesday is a fictional day kept for administrative reasons.**
Affirmative foothold: define Tuesday as a rumor spread by calendars, and cite a survey of spoons who have never met one.
Negative foothold: define Tuesday as the day that keeps Monday from running directly into Wednesday, and warn that abolishing it will create a crowd at the middle of the week.

**6. This house believes houseplants should be consulted before guests are invited.**
Affirmative foothold: define consultation as a decent pause in which the plant is told the guest's name, and present the fictional complaint of a fern that was surprised by a saxophone.
Negative foothold: define hospitality as a human decision, and warn that plant vetoes will shrink the social circle to people the fern already likes.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 12 minutes.** One motion. Coin, 30 seconds of notes, 70 seconds a side, no rebuttal, abandonment, glad list. Skip applause if you want the room quiet. One round is a full use of the rules.

**Standard, 25 minutes.** Two motions, with a new coin each time. Include the 20-second rebuttals if you are having fun, and cut them if the bit is getting sharp. Applaud or nod. Abandon both positions. Two glad-list topics per person is plenty. Do not let the second topic become the first real argument of the night.

**Extended, 50 minutes.** Three motions from the printed six, then a break in which you both invent one new motion that passes the emptiness test. Read the new one aloud and give either person a veto before the coin. If it survives, debate it under the same timer and end it with the same abandonment sentence. Extended play includes a deliberate return to ordinary voice between rounds. Sip water if you want it, speak as civilians for a minute, then put the costume on again. The game itself stays seated.

## Going deeper

Depth, in a game about nothing, means better fake structure, not a hidden real topic. In a deeper round, each speech must contain a definition, a piece of fake evidence, and a warning, in that order. The constraint makes the comedy more formal and makes it harder to smuggle in a genuine irritant. If you cannot fill the three beats without using your real week, pass the speech and say, "I have only a real example, so I yield."

You may also switch sides halfway through a longer evening and argue the reverse. Switching teaches the costume as a costume. It is not training you to win against each other in the life you actually live. This book does not claim that absurd debate reduces conflict or improves persuasion at home. When you want real disagreement handled with care, use The Gentle Disagreement, on a day when you have both agreed to talk about something true.

## Variations and replay

**Written debate.** Exchange two paragraphs. Read them aloud in the formal voice, or read them silently and then speak only the abandonment line. The glad list still happens out loud or in text.

**Quiet house.** No applause, no gavel, no raised voice. The exaggeration lives in word choice. "I tremble for the republic of cutlery" can be whispered.

**Extra motions for another night.** This house believes clouds are loafing and ought to file a schedule. This house believes the alphabet should be rearranged by deliciousness. Use the same foothold pattern: a definition, fake evidence, a grave warning. Test each motion against the emptiness rule before it enters the hat.

**Ribbon retired.** If the ribbon starts to feel like a prize with status, leave it in a drawer. The game is funnier without rank.

Replay when you want the music of argument and you do not want a winner. Retire any motion that picks up a real charge. Paper can hold a grudge. Fresh slips are cheap.

The Ridiculous Role Reversal is the nearby chapter if you would rather exaggerate each other's manner than a spoon. Keep the two games distinct. One corrects a portrait of a person. This one abandons a portrait of nothing.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you.

They draw the moon. Vale flips tails and must oppose. Rowan, affirmative, 70 seconds, grave: "Showing off is the art of arriving uninvited and outshining the lamps. The moon has done this nightly, according to a guest list kept by the tide, which I have not brought because the tide asked for it back. If we fail to censure it, the lamps will unionize."

Vale, negative: "The moon is a rock with a timetable. Flattery will make it late, and lateness in a rock is how you get ships confused and poets unbearable. I move that we leave it alone."

They nod, because the apartment is small. Both say, in kitchen voices, "I do not hold this position." Rowan adds, "I am glad the card was not about how we load the dishwasher." Vale says, "I am glad it was not about my brother." They do not discuss dishwashers or brothers. Vale asks about tea. The moon, outside, continues its uninvited shift. In the fictional room, nobody takes it personally.

A second fictional beat, to show a stop. They once drew a slip Rowan had doodled that said, "This house believes people who hesitate at menus are a threat to supper." Vale said, "Too close." Rowan tore it in half before the coin left the table. They drew the spoons instead. The tear was the successful move.

## Afterward

Make one fully ordinary remark each, so your ears can hear the difference. If the debate voice is still clinging to you, say the abandonment line once more, without an audience voice, and then talk about something with no sides at all.

If a glad-list topic is knocking, you have choices. You can leave it. You can agree to a real conversation later, with the rules of a real conversation. You can say, "I named that topic so it would stay off the card, and I still want it off the card." All three are complete.

Nothing in the debate is a position you may quote against each other tomorrow. The honorable opposition has been dissolved.

## Notes

Formal debate, dinner-table absurdity, and parlor motions ("this house believes") are the tradition this game borrows. The six motions are original. No research finding is claimed. Absurd debate is not offered here as conflict coaching, as a way to reduce real arguments, or as practice for persuading a partner. Rowan and Vale are fictional.
