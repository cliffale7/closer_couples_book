## The hook

You can know the drink, the joke, and the way someone drops their keys, and still be describing a person they were three years ago. This is a trivia game about that gap. You each write, privately, what you believe is true of the other. You reveal one item at a time. You compare knowledge, assumptions, and surprises. The title is a joke you are allowed to enjoy: you do not need to be newly wed, or wed at all. You need to be two adults willing to update the file.

Nobody gets a podium. A match is a pleasure. A miss is information about an assumption, not an offense, and not a lack of love.

## Why it might matter

Everyone who loves someone carries a working model of them. Some of the model is current. Some of it is a season you liked and kept. Some of it was borrowed from their family, from an old story, or from your own habits, then quietly applied to the person across from you. The model is useful. It is also capable of lag.

Writing the guess down, before you hear the correction, makes the lag visible. Speech is slippery. A sentence on paper is easier to sort. When you reveal in pairs, you get three kinds of result, and all three are worth having. Knowledge: you were close, and the closeness is specific. Assumption: you answered for an older version, or for someone who is not in the room. Surprise: they have a stress, a comfort, or a wish you had not been told, and the blank is an invitation rather than a verdict.

Entry 101, The Borrowed Brain Experiment, asks you to answer a scenario as you think your partner would, then compare the reasoning, not only the final decision. This chapter compares trivia about habits, tastes, and knowledge, and it lingers on the surprises. One studies how a decision gets made. The other studies what you think you already know. They are neighbors, not the same room.

## Before you begin

Print or copy two answer sheets. Each sheet has a line for every question you plan to use, and three empty marks you will fill only at the reveal: Now, Older, Borrowed. You each write about the other person. You do not write about yourself until the reveal, and only if you want to correct the record.

Sit so you cannot read each other's pages. On a call, use two notes and share a screen only when that number is up. Large type is welcome. A screen reader is a fine way to hear your own guesses before you read them aloud. Speech is optional at the reveal. You may trade papers and read in silence, then talk.

Skip any item. Write "pass" on the line. A pass is read as a pass. Your partner does not fill the blank as a penalty, and does not owe you the answer. Leave sex, family secrets, money, and any third person's story off the sheet. If a question would require a fact you have never been told, the interesting result is "I have not said that," which you may follow with the fact if you want it known. An unknown fact is a gap in the conversation. It is not proof of neglect.

Optional: a small pile of paper stars, buttons, or torn slips. If a prediction matches, the person who was described may drop one star on the table. Stars stay scattered. You will not add them up. If a podium begins to form, sweep the stars into a pile and call them confetti. Confetti has no ranking.

Agree the set before you write. Short uses four questions. Standard uses eight. Extended uses 12. The questions below are original to this book.

## The experience

**Write, privately, for 8 to 12 minutes.** Answer in a phrase or a sentence. Specific beats clever. "Tea, too hot, in the blue mug" will teach you more than "a drink." If you do not know, write your best current guess and add a question mark. The question mark is honest, and it still counts as a guess you are willing to check.

**Reveal, one question at a time.**

1. One of you reads the question. Trade this job so nobody becomes a permanent host.
2. Both of you read your prediction. If speaking is tiring, exchange the lines in print.
3. The person being described speaks first, if they want to. They give the current answer in one sentence. They may add "that matches," "that is close," or "that surprises me."
4. They mark the guess, out loud or on the sheet: Now, Older, or Borrowed. Now means it fits the person in the room this month. Older means it fit a previous season and has gone stale. Borrowed means it seems to come from someone else, or from the guesser's own life.
5. If it matches and you are using stars, drop one. Do not applaud a miss into shame, and do not applaud a match into a trophy.
6. If the described person wants to update the record, they do it in one sentence. "The annoyance this month is the flickering bulb, not the dishes." That sentence is the new knowledge. It retires the old guess.

Go to the next question. Either person may skip a reveal even after the words are written. Fold the line over and say "leaving this one." Folded lines stay folded.

**The debrief, which is the point.** After the last question, each of you answers this once: "Which guess described an older version of me, or an assumption you borrowed from someone else?" Give the guesser a chance to say where the assumption came from, if they know. "I borrowed it from your sister's story about college" is a useful origin. "I borrowed it from my last relationship" is also useful, and it does not need a trial. Thank the correction. Do not defend the outdated file as if it were loyalty.

**Questions.** Each line is something you write about your partner.

1. In the first 10 minutes after getting home, my partner usually...
2. The small household sound my partner notices before I do is...
3. My partner's current low-grade stress, the one that is not a crisis, is...
4. When the day has been long, the taste my partner wants is... (salt, sweet, sour, bitter, hot, or name another)
5. The ordinary object that is currently my partner's comfort is...
6. A petty annoyance my partner has mentioned more than once this month is...
7. A wish my partner has for the coming month, small enough to actually happen, is...
8. The joke or phrase my partner uses when stalling is...
9. The time of day my partner is most like themselves is...
10. What my partner rereads, rewatches, or replays when they want the world familiar is...
11. My partner prefers good news delivered... (immediately, in person, after the bag is down, or another way)
12. A skill my partner is quietly proud of, which rarely comes up, is...

If you do not share a home, read "getting home" as "arriving wherever they land at the end of the day," and read "household sound" as "a sound in the place they are today." The questions should still make sense.

**What you might print.** Two identical sheets, 14-point type, with the question, a long blank, and three small circles labeled Now, Older, and Borrowed. A cover sheet so the pages can travel to a call unopened. No buzzer, no host's podium, no score box. If you want a header, use the chapter title and the date.

**Ending.** The game ends when the debrief question has been answered by both of you, or gently declined. Close by each updating one fact in a sentence, if you want the record current. "This month, the comfort object is the gray sweater, not the radio." Then stop. Do not add a lightning round. Lightning rounds are how a friendly miss turns into an exam.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 20 minutes.** Use questions 1, 4, 5, and 7: a habit, a taste, a comfort, and a wish. Write for about 6 minutes. Reveal with the marks. Ask the debrief question once each. This cut still includes a surprise and a possible older version. It drops the annoyance question on purpose, so a short night does not have to include a complaint.

**Standard, 40 minutes.** Use questions 1 through 8. Write for about 10 minutes. Reveal in pairs with the three marks. Use stars if they stay a joke. Debrief. One updated fact each, if you want it.

**Extended, 65 minutes.** Use all 12. After the debrief, each of you writes one new question the deck missed, still about a habit, a taste, a stress, a comfort, or a wish. Both of you answer that new question about the other, privately, then reveal it with the same marks. The new question has to be one the person being asked could know from ordinary life. If it depends on a secret they never offered, scrap it and enjoy the 12 you have.

## Going deeper

Depth here is currency. Play the standard set, then ask, for two items only: "When did this become true?" A comfort object that arrived last winter is a different story from one that has survived three apartments. You are not digging for pain. You are dating the model.

A second deeper question, once each: "What do you wish I would stop assuming?" The answer can be as small as "that I want company the moment I get home." If the answer is larger than the evening, thank them and pick it up in a conversation that is not a game. Entry 083, The Secret Nobody Would Guess, is a better door for a volunteered surprise than a trivia sheet is.

Do not deepen the night by adding sexual-embarrassment questions, traps about fidelity, bodies, or ex-partners, or a final score. A high number of stars would still not mean you will stay together. A low number would not mean you have failed at love. You would have learned where the file is out of date. That is the whole prize.

## Variations and replay

**Month stamp.** Play on the first evening of a season. Date the sheets. When you play again, read one old sheet aloud before you write the new guesses. The comedy is watching last year's petty annoyance lose its job.

**One-theme night.** All of the questions you use must be about comfort, or all about the coming month. Narrowing the theme keeps the game from feeling like a census.

**Sealed notes.** Write in advance and open on a call. Useful across distance. Agree that either person may leave a seal unbroken.

**No stars.** Some pairs will have more fun without tokens. The marks Now, Older, and Borrowed are the real score, and even they are labels, not points.

On replay, notice whether your guesses have moved closer to the present. If they have, you have been listening. If a borrowed assumption keeps returning, that assumption has a source worth naming once, kindly, so it can retire.

The Borrowed Brain Experiment is waiting when you want to compare how a decision gets made. Would You Rather: Unhinged Edition is waiting when you want hypothetical choices instead of facts. This chapter is for the ordinary, current, slightly messy record of a person you already like.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you. They play the short set at a kitchen table.

Vale's guess for question 1: "Checks the mail, even when it is Sunday, then stands at the sink."
Rowan's correction: "That matches the first apartment. Now I put the bag down, wash my hands, and see whether the sourdough has done anything dramatic."
Mark: Older.

Rowan's guess for question 5: "The chipped blue mug."
Vale's correction: "The mug is yours. My comfort this month is the window I open six inches, even in bad weather. I have never made a speech about it."
Mark: Borrowed, and also a surprise. Vale drops no star. Vale looks pleased anyway. "You borrowed the mug from the way I use yours when I am cold."

Rowan's guess for question 7: "A weekday with no plans after 8."
Vale: "That matches. That is the wish."
A paper star goes onto the table. They do not make a second star compete with it.

In the debrief, Rowan says the mail-checking guess came from a story Vale's brother tells. Vale asks that the brother edition be retired. Rowan agrees in one sentence. The fictional evening ends there, with the window open six inches because Vale got up and did it, not because the game required a ceremony.

## Afterward

Read one updated fact back, so the new version has been heard. If you want, thank your partner for a guess that was exact, and for a miss they corrected without a speech. Exactness is sweet. A clean correction is also sweet.

If you feel tender about an older version of yourself that your partner still loves, you can say that. "I like that you remember the mug era" and "please use the window era" can both be true. Neither sentence has to win.

Leave the stars on the table until you are done laughing at them. Then throw them away, or keep one with the date. A kept star means "we played," not "we scored."

## Our Story

These pages can hold the guesses you want to remember and the corrections you want on record. The private halves do not have to be shown. Sharing them is optional.

If you keep an answer sheet, date it and note which version you played. An old sheet is a fine then-versus-now document. It is not a report card.

session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused

- Which of my guesses described my partner now, and which described an older version?
- What assumption did I borrow from someone else, from a past season, or from my own habits?

- Which description of me was surprisingly exact, and which one missed the person I am this month?
- Where did I feel known, and what one-sentence update did I want entered into the record?

- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything

- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now

## Notes

Predicting a partner for the pleasure of the comparison is an old party custom. A long-running television contest made a prize version of that custom famous. This chapter is a private game for any adult pair. It uses none of that show's questions, host, set, or branding, and the show has no role in these pages. No research finding is claimed. A wrong guess is an assumption you can see. It is not a score that predicts a future. Rowan and Vale are fictional.
