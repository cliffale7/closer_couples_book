## The hook

Imagine a small museum with your name on the lintel and four galleries: an object, a person, a song, and an event. You are the curator. Your partner is a visitor who is allowed to stop in front of a piece and ask one question. The collection represents a life, the way a good small museum does, by choosing what gets a plaque and letting the rest stay in storage.

If you only tell stories, with no labels and no visitor, you have skipped the museum. If the visitor never gets to stand in front of a piece, you have given a speech. The frame is the mechanism. Order, a label, and a person who may ask: that is the hour.

## Why it might matter

A life told in order of importance tends to become a stack of achievements or a stack of injuries. A museum asks for a different selection. An object can hold a stretch of years that never made a story you repeat. A person can be the one who taught you a gesture. A song can mark a room. An event can be a single scene rather than a decade with a title. Together they suggest a life with rooms, not a mood board and not a résumé.

Memory palaces, house tours, and "soundtrack of my life" games are folk forms. Museums add labels, sequence, and a visitor with a question. The four galleries are a design for this book. They are not a tested method, and this chapter claims no study showing that metaphor, memory palaces, or tours improve a relationship. The pleasure available here is simpler. You choose what stands for you, and someone you love agrees to be a guest in that choice.

One real object on the table is a pleasant anchor. Imaginary exhibits are enough. A shoebox diorama is an option for people who like making things. It is not the activity. If the craft becomes so elaborate that you never tour, the museum has hired a builder and forgotten the visiting hours.

The Museum of Unsaid Things, later in the book, is a different exhibit. That chapter makes labels for what was left unsaid, pairing what happened with what someone wished the other person understood. This tour is objects, people, songs, and events that represent a life. The exhibits may be entirely imaginary.

## Before you begin

Divide a sheet into four headings, or label four cards: Object, Person, Song, Event. A timer. The tour can stay entirely spoken. If you want one real object, choose it before you start so the search does not become the evening.

Agree that the curator sets the order and the altitude. The visitor may ask one question per exhibit and does not add a rival story until the tour is over. The visitor does not correct a label. If a fact is wildly out of date, the curator is the person who updates the plaque.

Some people-exhibits will involve family harm or an ex-partner. The curator chooses the plaque's detail. A closed stack, in the longer version, may be named and left unopened. The visitor does not demand the story behind it.

Songs: name the piece and the scene it marks. Leave the lyrics in the recording. People: the living and the dead can be included, with your judgment about what is yours to tell. Events: one scene, not a decade.

The tour can be spoken if reading a label is hard. Tactile objects are optional. If hearing the tour is hard, write the labels, read them in silence, and ask the questions in writing. The "walk" can be from chair to chair or entirely in place if movement hurts. On a call, a camera may show one object, or you can share a page of labels. Four exhibits is the ceiling for a first tour. A crowded room and background music are unnecessary. Either of you may pause between galleries.

## The experience

Each curator prepares four exhibits. Every label needs three lines. What it is. Which stretch of life it holds. Why it is in the collection. A chipped mug can hold a winter of night shifts. A person can hold the year you learned to tell the truth faster. A song can hold a kitchen. An event can hold one missed train. If all four exhibits are achievements, add a replacement that is not a trophy. The museum is allowed to be proud. It is also a life, so it needs a piece that is ordinary, odd, or unfinished.

Choose who tours first. The curator speaks the labels in their own order. After each exhibit, the visitor may ask one question. A good visitor question asks to see the piece more clearly: "Where is the chip?" "What did that person repeat?" "Were you alone when the song was playing?" "What happened in the 10 minutes after the train left?" A question that replaces the plaque with the visitor's own story waits until both tours are over.

When the four exhibits are done, the visitor thanks the curator in one sentence that names a piece. Then you swap. Equal time includes equal restraint. You are done when each of you has curated a tour the other could actually visit, or has played the short pass described below. A museum with three galleries and a blank where a song should be is unfinished. Name a piece of music, even if the name is "the hum my uncle used in the car," and give it the three lines.

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 15 minutes total. Each of you makes a single pass through all four galleries, one sentence per exhibit: object, then person, then song, then event. The visitor holds questions until the pass ends, then asks one question about one exhibit. About 7 minutes each, including that question. If 7 minutes makes you rush the person or the event into a label you do not mean, save the standard tour for a day with a wider afternoon.

The standard version takes about 40 minutes. Time to draft the four labels, then a tour in the curator's order with one visitor question per exhibit, then the same tour in the other direction. Sit, or move from chair to chair if standing up helps you feel the rooms. Stay in place if that is the truer hospitality.

The extended version takes about 60 minutes. After the four open exhibits, the curator may add a closed stack: a piece that is named and not fully opened. The label is a title and one line about why it remains in storage. The visitor may ask whether the stack is closed for privacy, for timing, or for lack of words. They may not ask to see inside. If neither of you wants a closed stack, the extended version ends with the four galleries and a little silence in the lobby.

## Going deeper

The visitor may choose one exhibit and sit with it for 3 extra minutes. In that time the curator adds only details that fit on the plaque: a date, a texture, a second sentence about why it earned a case. No new gallery opens.

You may give one exhibit opening hours. "This part of my life is visitable on weeknights, and closed when my family is in the room." The hours are a boundary, and they belong on the label if you want them obeyed later.

A second visit, weeks later, can change the order. Put the event first, or the song. Sequence is an interpretation. Say what the new order emphasizes. The visitor still asks one question per piece and still leaves their own exhibit in their coat until their ticket comes up.

## Variations and replay

Write the labels as if for a wall, short enough to read while standing, then speak them. Or hand the written labels over and let the visitor read in silence before any question. Both are tours.

Long-distance, film one object for 10 seconds and tell the other three galleries by voice. If cameras are unwelcome, the spoken museum is complete. Replay after a move, a loss, or a year that changed the collection. Retire an exhibit in public. "This mug is leaving the museum" is a legitimate update.

If you like making things, a single card with four headings is the whole build. Save the diorama for a weekend when making is the point. This chapter's point is the tour, the question, and the life the plaques agree to represent.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. Their museum is not a model of taste.

Vale curates. Object: a chipped blue mug, holding the year of early shifts, in the collection because Vale still reaches for it on mornings that require courage without an audience. Person: a neighbor, Mrs. Adeyemi, holding the summer Vale was 19, in the collection because she returned a spare key and said, "Lock it yourself. I am next door, not in charge." Song: a recording of "Landslide," holding a week in a first apartment with no curtains, in the collection because Vale played it once through and then went out to buy a lamp. Event: the missed train on a March afternoon, one scene, in the collection because Vale arrived late to an interview, told the truth about the train, and was hired by someone who liked the lack of a performance.

Rowan asks, at the mug, "Where is the chip, and do you avoid drinking from that side?" Vale answers. At Mrs. Adeyemi, Rowan asks what Vale did with the key that evening. At the song, Rowan does not quote a line and does not offer a song of their own. At the train, Rowan asks what the 10 minutes on the platform felt like. Rowan has a story about a missed flight and leaves it in the lobby until Vale's tour is over.

Then Rowan tours. One exhibit is a borrowed library card from childhood. One is a friend who taught Rowan to apologize in the same week as the offense. One is the hum of a bus route, named as music because it functioned as music. One is the Tuesday Rowan kept a plant alive. Vale asks one question at each and does not correct the bus hum into "that is not really a song." In this museum, it is a song. They are done.

## Afterward

The visitor names the exhibit they can still see, and the curator says whether that was the one they most wanted seen. If the answers differ, you have learned something about the plaques, and nobody has to renegotiate the collection tonight.

Ask which label was hardest to keep at three lines. Ask whether a closed stack needs a note in the shared book or a private margin. A sketch or a photo enters the journal only if the curator agrees. The imaginary mug does not need to be proven with a camera.

Leave the rival stories, if you still want them, for a walk or another chapter. The museum closes when the second tour ends. You can eat something in the café you did not have to build.

## Our Story

Write the date, time, place, version, mood if you want one, intention, and initials if you want them. Mark done or paused. Private pages are optional to share. Store the exhibit title and one label line the curator wants remembered. A closed stack can be recorded as a title alone. Repeat the tour when the collection changes. Each visit can have its own page.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- The exhibit title I most want remembered, and one line from its label
- A closed stack I named, if I named one, and whether it stays closed
partner_b:
- The exhibit I stopped in front of, and the one question I asked
- A label I was tempted to correct, and what I did with that impulse
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
