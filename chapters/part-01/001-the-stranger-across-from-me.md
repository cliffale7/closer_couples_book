## The hook

You can know how someone takes their coffee and still be guessing about the year they felt most like themselves. Familiarity is a talented clerk. It files a person under "already known" and then stops asking. The file is often right about the coffee. It is quieter about the habit a new friend would misread, and about the belief that rarely survives a group conversation.

This hour borrows the manners of a first meeting. You interview each other as fascinating strangers, and a question belongs here only if a stranger could ask it with no shared history: a year they felt like themselves, a habit a new friend would misread, a belief they rarely say in a group. A question that needs this household, one you have never asked this person, is 005, The Things Nobody Thinks to Ask. The work is a timed question, asked once, followed only as far as the answer actually goes. If you slip into your usual shorthand, or if one of you hosts while the other performs, the premise has left the room. You can pause the timer and begin that turn again.

## Why it might matter

A long affection teaches you a dialect. You trade nicknames, glances, and the three stories that have become public property between you. Dialects are intimate. They also retire questions. A courteous stranger is still allowed to ask what a year felt like from the inside, which habit is being misread, and which belief you protect by keeping it off the table. Being asked that specifically, and having the detail received, is a different event from being praised for being easy to know.

Diary studies of ordinary interactions found that intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator, and that in Study 2 emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure (see L1998 in the notes). Those diaries did not test a stranger role-play. They are a reason to protect the listening, not a promise about this hour. A laboratory procedure of escalating questions among people who had just met is a different activity again (see A1997). This interview does not inherit that result, and it is not a recipe for falling in love.

John Gottman's clinical writing popularized "love maps" as a picture of knowing a partner's inner world. Use the picture if it helps you care about the map. It is a practitioner metaphor. It is not a score, and this chapter will not quote one.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can both see a timer. Two chairs are enough. Paper or a notes app is for drafting questions, not for a transcript of the answers. Phones can keep time if notifications are quiet. Curiosity is a sufficient aim. You do not need to announce that the goal is closeness. Later studies of that laboratory procedure did not find that declaring closeness as the goal produced more of it.

Agree on anything already off the table. A pass needs no substitute confession. The stranger manners are not permission to reopen a subject either of you has asked to leave alone. Stories about third parties, bodies, and childhood stay optional. If a question lands on one of those and the mood changes, the person asked can say "pass" or "smaller," and the interviewer moves on.

Either of you may pause the timer. One question at a time is the pace if the room feels crowded. If hearing is easier on the page, pass the questions in writing, sit where speech can be read, or type the answers. Spoken questions are enough if reading the page is not. Stay seated if movement hurts. On a call, the same timer rules hold, and cameras stay optional.

## The experience

Give yourselves about 5 minutes to draft, in silence. Each of you writes three questions a polite stranger could ask this particular person. Mix the kinds so the game cannot collapse into favorite colors. Use these shapes, and write your own wording.

A memory: a year, a season, or a room in which they felt most like themselves. A habit: something a new friend would misread, and what the misreading gets wrong. A belief: something they rarely say in a group, stated as a question they can decline.

A question qualifies if a stranger could ask it without a shared history, and if it cannot be answered with a single word. "How was work?" does not qualify. A question you have already worn out this month does not qualify either. If you only want to flirt-test or cross-examine, tear that card up and write another. The role is courtesy with a good ear, not a trap.

Choose who interviews first by a coin toss or by whoever has the more restless curiosity. Set the timer for the standard turn, about 10 minutes. The interviewer asks one drafted question, then may ask two follow-ups. Follow-ups stay with the answer. "Say more about the empty city" is a follow-up. "That reminds me of my cousin" is the interviewer climbing into the turn, and it ends the stranger premise. The interviewer does not answer inside their own turn. Silence belongs to the person being asked. If you feel the urge to fill it with your story, look at the timer instead.

When the timer ends, you swap. Equal time is part of the design. A person who passes still counts as having taken the turn. After both turns, set 2 minutes on the clock. Each of you names one fact that had gone dull because it felt already known. One sentence each. Then stop, even if a better story is waving from the hallway.

You are done when both of you have been interviewed for a full turn, or have passed, and each of you has named one dulled fact. If you only had the short version, you are done when each of you has asked one question, heard one follow-up, and kept the times even.

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 10 minutes. One question each, plus a single follow-up, with the clock split evenly. Skip the three-question draft. Write the one question on the spot, still in the stranger register, still easy to decline. Keep the 2-minute naming if you have it. If you do not, name the dulled fact in a single sentence as you stand up.

The standard version takes about 30 minutes. Draft three questions each. Interview for one full timed turn, with two follow-ups allowed and no answering inside that turn. Close with 2 minutes, one dulled fact each.

The extended version takes about 50 minutes. Play the standard version, then open a second round aimed only at a detail the first round left unfinished. Each of you names the loose thread before any new question is asked. The second turn is shorter, about 8 minutes, and it may not wander off to a fresher, easier topic. If neither of you has a loose thread, the extended version is over. Inventing one to fill the time is how shorthand sneaks back in.

## Going deeper

If the first round stayed in charming memories, use one follow-up on the belief they rarely say in a group. Ask it as a person who will be glad to meet a no. Write the answer if speaking keeps sanding it down, then read what you wrote. The page is still the turn.

After the dulled fact, you may ask one correction question: "What have I been treating as settled that is still moving?" Answer in a sentence, or pass. The asker repeats back the sentence they heard and lets the other person edit the repetition. That edit is the point. Praise is optional and slightly beside the point. Accuracy is the courtesy.

A third pass, on another day, can be done entirely in writing across a table or a shared note. Same timer. Same ban on answering inside the interviewer's turn. The stranger who writes is still a stranger, not a biographer collecting material for later.

## Variations and replay

Play it on a call with the timer visible to both of you. Cameras off can make the stranger manners easier, because a face you love is a strong invitation to use the old dialect. Play it in a café if you can still hear each other and still decline in public. If a crowded room makes questions feel like a performance, go home to the short version.

Replay is useful because the filing cabinet refills. A month from now the dull fact will be a different one. Keep the old questions in a drawer and write new ones. If a question worked only because it startled, it will work less well the second time, and that is a reason to draft again.

If one of you thinks better while moving, a slow walk can replace the chairs, with a right to sit the moment a story needs stillness. The designed version stays seated for pain, fatigue, or a day when walking would become the activity and the interview would become weather.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you. They are here so the rules have a shape.

They have been together long enough to split a grocery list without speaking. Rowan drafts three questions and then puts two aside. The one that remains: "A new neighbor would see you leave every gathering a little early. What would they get wrong about that?"

Vale looks at the timer. "They would think I am bored. I leave when the room gets loud enough that I start performing a version of myself I do not like. The year I felt most like myself was a night-shift year. The city was empty at 5 a.m., and I did not have to be interesting."

Rowan wants to say that this explains the way Vale hates big dinners. That sentence would be Rowan answering inside the turn. Rowan asks a follow-up instead. "What did you do on those mornings, once you were interesting to nobody?"

Vale laughs, then passes on the second follow-up, which was heading toward a family story Vale has already asked to leave closed. Rowan nods, writes nothing down, and lets the timer finish in quiet. When they swap, Vale asks which belief Rowan rarely brings into a group. Rowan talks for 4 minutes about a private rule for lending money, then stops before it becomes a speech about Vale's brother. In the last 2 minutes Vale says, "I had filed the early exits under mood. They are a preference for being unperformed." Rowan says, "I had filed your questions about money under worry. They are a rule." They make tea. The interview is over.

## Afterward

Stay with the dulled fact for a moment before you turn it into a theory of each other. If you want to talk more, talk about what the stranger frame made audible: a wording, a pause, a correction. A full transcript is optional and usually too much.

Ask each other, without a second interview, what was easy to ask, what you almost asked and put down, and whether any question should be retired for good. If the hour brushed a closed subject, say so in a sentence and leave it. You can also say nothing and wash the cups.

Carry one accurate detail into the week if you want to. A text that proves you remembered the empty city is plenty. A grand conclusion about who the other person really is will be slightly less accurate than the sentence they already corrected.

## Our Story

Write the date, the time, the place, which version you played, a mood if you want one, a one-line intention, and your initials if you want them. Mark the session done or paused. Private pages are optional to share. Keep your own lines in a personal note if you would rather the shared book hold only what you both choose. A repeated sitting gets its own lines. Two revisits are a pattern, not a limit.
