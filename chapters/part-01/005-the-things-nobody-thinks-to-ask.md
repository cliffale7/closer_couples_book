## The hook

These questions need this household. A stranger could not ask them. They depend on a life you already share, and on something you have never asked this person. "What is your favorite film?" can be asked of anyone. The answer is one word. You have both heard it, and the silence afterward is already furnished. The questions worth this evening are a little odd, specific enough to need a scene, and easy to decline without a trial.

You will write them. The samples later in this chapter are spare keys this book made up. Throw out any key that does not fit your actual door. A famous list copied from somewhere else fails the hour for a different reason: the mechanism is fresh curiosity between you two, not a ritual you could have found in a magazine.

## Why it might matter

Each of you writes three questions you have never asked this person. Spread them across beliefs, habits, memories, preferences, and private thoughts. Those five kinds are the field, and they have to need this household, not a stranger with a clipboard. A session that stays inside one kind, only childhood or only sex or only work, has wandered off, even if every question sounds serious.

Being asked is only half the turn. After an answer, the asker reflects one specific detail they heard before anyone draws the next card. That reflection is how the asker shows the answer landed. Skip it and you have a trivia night with better lighting.

Diary studies of daily interactions found that intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator, and that in Study 2 emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure (see L1998 in the notes). Those studies did not test unusual prompts, and they do not rank any question in this chapter. A laboratory hour of escalating questions among strangers is a different procedure again (see A1997). Nothing here is a closeness dose, and announcing closeness as your goal is optional atmosphere, not a technique with a result you can claim.

Question games are a folk pastime, from conversation cards to late-night "tell me something true." The categories, the ban on obvious prompts, and the required reflection are the design. There is no validated question bank behind them.

## Before you begin

Sit down with blank cards. Together, name any zones already off the table. Write them on a card you will not draw from, or say them aloud and leave them there. A pass needs no reason. A pass is not a clue. You do not circle back with a softer version of the same question.

A question qualifies if you have never asked this partner, if it is hard to answer in one word, and if it is easy to decline. Questions about other people's secrets, about current harm, or about a body your partner has marked private are out of the stack even when they sound specific. Unusual and invasive are different ambitions. Keep the first.

Speech or writing both work. Read questions aloud if the page is hard to see. Trade cards and write the answers if speech-reading is tiring. Stay seated. A call or a shared note can carry the same rules. If the room is overloaded, cap the session at the short set. A private thought can be answered in one sentence. That sentence counts.

## The experience

Each of you writes three questions you have never asked this person. Spread them across more than one kind. Beliefs, habits, memories, preferences, and private thoughts all belong. You do not have to hit all five in one night. You do have to leave the favorite-color family behind.

These examples are discardable. They are not a script, and they are not a ranked set.

Belief: "Which rule about money did you absorb before you could argue with it, and where does it still sit when you pay for something ordinary?"

Habit: "What do you do with your hands in the first minute you are alone in a kitchen?"

Memory: "Which hallway, bus stop, or parking spot still belongs to a version of you this relationship has not met?"

Preference: "What texture of quiet do you want at the end of a loud day, and what do people usually offer instead?"

Private thought: "Which compliment do you translate into a smaller sentence before you let yourself keep it?"

More, if you want a second look and then your own words. Belief: "What do you think a promise is for when nobody is going to check?" Habit: "Which small repair do you make to a room before you can sit down in it?" Memory: "What meal can you still taste from a year that produced no photographs?" Preference: "Which inconvenience would you defend because your days are more yours with it?" Use them only as a demonstration of altitude. Then write the three that could only be asked of the person across from you.

Shuffle the six cards. Alternate who asks. The asker reads the question once. The answer can be short. The asker gets one follow-up, still inside the answer, still easy to decline. Then, before the next card, the asker reflects one specific detail. "The hallway with the green tile" is a reflection. "Wow, that's deep" is a compliment wearing a costume. If the reflection is wrong, the answerer corrects it in a sentence. The correction is a success.

Six questions is the standard round, about 30 minutes. You are done when the six are asked or passed, each surviving answer has been met with one specific detail, and neither of you has conducted an interview in which you never answered. If you run out of honest curiosity before six, stop. Padding with a question you do not care about is how the hour becomes a questionnaire.

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 15 minutes. Two questions each. Keep the follow-up and the specific reflection. Choose the questions that scare you a little in a social way, not the ones that break a boundary you already named.

The standard version takes about 30 minutes. Three questions written by each of you, six drawn and answered, alternating, one follow-up, one reflected detail before the next card. Leave the unused examples in the book.

The extended version takes about 45 minutes. After the standard round, each of you finds your dullest question, the one that almost could have been answered with a word, and rewrites it into something more particular. "Do you like mornings?" becomes "What is the first sound in the apartment that makes a morning feel like yours?" Ask the rewritten pair. Reflect a detail. Then stop, even if the stack still feels clever.

## Going deeper

Sort the answers, on a scrap, into the five kinds, and notice which kind you both avoided. On another day, write only in the avoided kind. Avoidance can mean boredom. It can also mean a closed door. Treat a second silence as information, and leave the door shut if either of you wants it shut.

You may answer a private-thought card in writing, then read only the sentence you are willing to say. The unread lines stay with the author. The asker reflects the sentence that was actually spoken.

A follow-up that begins "and is that why you..." has turned into a theory. Replace it with a request for one more concrete piece: a room, a time of day, a sentence you say to yourself. Theories can wait until the cards are finished, and they are still optional then.

## Variations and replay

Play the short set in a parked car, on a walk if you can still decline without an audience, or across a kitchen counter while something uncomplicated cooks. If the public setting makes you perform, go back to the table. Long-distance, send the three questions only after both of you have finished writing, so nobody drafts toward a visible reaction.

Replay by burning the old cards, figuratively or literally, and writing new ones. A question that worked because it startled will work less well the second time. Keep a question only if the answer has genuinely changed, and say that this is a revisit, not a pop quiz on whether they remember their own life.

If one of you is weary, the reflection can be written and passed across the table. A written detail is still a detail. A nod is not.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. Their questions are not assigned to you.

They take a diner booth, late, pie untouched. Rowan draws a card Vale wrote: "Which hallway, bus stop, or parking spot still belongs to a version of you this relationship has not met?" Rowan says, "The bus stop on King, in a coat that was too big. I used to practice conversations there that I never had." Vale reflects, "The stop on King, and conversations you rehearsed." Rowan laughs, one real laugh, not a dodge. "Rehearsed is generous. I mumbled at the shelter glass and then got on the bus. Keep the stop. Lose the dignity." Vale keeps the stop and drops the dignity. The laugh is the landing. Nobody turns it into a second interview.

Vale's next card, written by Rowan, asks which compliment Vale shrinks before keeping. Vale says, "Pass." Rowan does not rephrase it, does not tease, and does not store the pass as a mystery to solve over the pie. The decline stays.

They stop while the laugh and the pass are still the point. One card stays unasked because it suddenly sounded like a trick. Rowan tears it in half. The tear is the right use of a question that failed the test of courtesy. A full six can wait for a night with more curiosity. Nobody calls the booth a breakthrough.

## Afterward

Name the question that surprised you, and the answer you are willing to keep. Unasked leftovers can sit in a personal note. They do not belong on the shared page unless the author moves them there.

Ask whether any reflection was wrong in an interesting way. A wrong detail, corrected, often teaches more than a perfect echo, because you learn the noun that actually mattered. If a question should be retired, retire it out loud so it does not return next month in a cleverer coat.

You can also be done without a lesson. Put the cards in a pocket or a bin. Make a snack. The hour was the asking and the detail, not a verdict on how well you know each other.

## Our Story

Record the date, time, place, version, mood if you want one, intention, and initials if you want them. Mark done or paused. Private pages are optional to share. Keep an unasked question in your own note if you might want it later, and keep it out of the shared book until you are willing to be asked. Revisit whenever you can write new cards. The lines below are a pattern you can repeat, not a limit of two sittings.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- The question that surprised me, and the answer I am willing to keep
- A detail I reflected back, and the correction you made if I had it wrong
partner_b:
- A question I wrote that I had never asked you before
- An answer I gave in one sentence on purpose, and the sentence itself if I want it stored
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
