## The hook

Name the piece, then tell what it holds, before anyone presses play. A song can walk into a kitchen and bring a whole year with it, including a year you would rather leave unscored. Sometimes the cue is not a love song. It is hold music, a weather-radio voice, a chorus one of you tolerated because the other loved it, or three notes hummed badly and then abandoned out of embarrassment.

The track is the cue. The story is the point. 075, Our Greatest Hits, compiles stories, and a hit may mention a song and then leave it off. 077 is the long line, including years with no soundtrack. Pressing play without the story turns the hour into a playlist. Treating a lyric as if you had written it to each other swaps your life for someone else's chorus. You can name the piece. You tell what it holds. If the sound stays off, and the title plus two sentences still carry the chapter, you are inside this hour.

## Why it might matter

Shared history sticks to kitchens, coats, and sometimes to a few bars of sound. A diner, a stairwell, a power cut: people often report that a cue puts them back in the room. That report is ordinary talk. It is not a finding this book claims. No music-and-memory paper was used here. Nothing in this chapter asserts that songs reliably retrieve autobiography or repair a bond. Mixtapes, "our song," and processionals are widespread customs. They are not a validated protocol for relationship quality.

What the hour can do is narrower and more interesting. Each person brings music tied to a chapter or a feeling, speaks before any excerpt, and lets the other person hear the story at the size it actually has. Silence, news radio, and a song one of you loved while the other endured it, all count. A commercially romantic track is allowed. It is not required. Taste is not a diagnosis.

## Before you begin

About 50 minutes, seated. Each person brings three pieces: one from early on, one from a difficult or ordinary middle stretch if you want that stretch included, and one that fits now. Six stories is the full set. If you only have two honest pieces, bring two. Padding the list until the stories shrink is how a soundtrack fails.

For each track you will want a written liner note the other person can read with the sound off: title, artist or source, and two sentences. "Artist" may be a weather service, a ceiling speaker, or "unknown, from the diner." Instrumental music counts. Lyrics are optional to mention, and they are never printed here. A short paraphrase of why a line mattered is enough. The line itself stays in the recording.

Volume is a shared decision, including silence. Headphones, one speaker, or no playback are equal options. Tinnitus, misophonia, hearing aids, and noise sensitivity set the level. If a sense the joke depends on is missing for either of you, tell the story in another channel.

Warn before a song tied to grief, illness, or an ex-partner. Either person may skip a track without explaining the association. Music that mocks a community, or that one of you finds degrading, does not arrive on a shared soundtrack by surprise.

No dancing is required. 038, The Slow Dance With No Music, is the chapter for moving together in silence. This one can stay in the chairs.

You may pass a piece, change the prompt, or stop after the early tracks if the middle ones are heavier than the evening can hold.

## The experience

**Liner notes first, about 10 minutes.** Write the six, or however many are real. For each: where you were, whose choice it was, what it carries. Whose choice includes "neither of us; it was on in the room." What it carries might be hope, irritation, competence, grief, or the plain fact of having stayed another hour.

**Speak, then play.** Take turns. The teller speaks the liner note before any sound. Then a short excerpt, at a volume both can stand, or no excerpt at all. The title is allowed to stand alone. The listener does not quiz. "Do you remember this song?" is a test, and this hour has no test. They may ask one question about the scene: "Were we in the booth or at the counter?" They name, in a sentence, what they heard the track carrying. Then the next piece.

A private association needs a warning, not a confession. "This one brushes an old relationship. I can skip it." Skipping is complete. The listener does not bargain for the backstory.

Go through the pieces you brought. After all of them, make two choices together.

One piece to keep as a private encore: a track you might actually play again, or a title you are glad to have named, at home, without an audience.

One story that should be told even when the song is unavailable. Say it once with the sound off, so you know the story can travel. If the only version of the memory is the chorus, you have not finished the telling. Add the place and the human detail until the song could be lost and the chapter would remain.

If you want a sequence, put the pieces in an order and read the titles as a track list. The order can be chronological or emotional. It can also be no order. A sequence is a pleasure, not a requirement. The track that is yours for reasons only the two of you know can be marked as such and left unexplained on the shared page.

Then stop. A long playlist after the stories is a different night.

## Three ways to play

**20 minutes.** One piece each. Liner note spoken first, excerpt optional, one question, one sentence about what it carries. Name the story that survives with the sound off. You postpone the middle stretch and the "now" track. One cue each is a finished session.

**50 minutes.** Three pieces each, or fewer if that is all you have, spoken before they are played, then a private encore and one sound-off story. This is the version that feels like a soundtrack with chapters, not a shuffle.

**75 minutes.** Trade written liner notes and read them all in silence before anything plays. Then play only the excerpts you still want, or play none and talk from the page. Choose the sequence if you want one. The extra time is for reading and for cutting a track that looked right on the list and feels wrong in the room. Cutting is part of authorship.

## Going deeper

On a later day, tell the sound-off story to each other again without naming the song until the end, and see whether the scene holds. If it collapses without the title, add the missing human detail and keep that version.

You might also add a piece that is not music in the shop sense: a kettle, a bus chime, a podcast sign-off, the particular silence after a power cut. If it cues a chapter, it belongs. Call it by a plain name. Give it the same two sentences.

Embarrassment as the joke can be retired. If a story only works by making one of you the punch line, it leaves the shared soundtrack. It may stay in a private note. It may also simply end.

## Variations and replay

Send titles in advance if you will listen on a call, or trade liner notes asynchronously and talk later. The story still comes before the file. A partner who misses hearing reads the notes. A partner who is overloaded by sound uses silence from the start.

The motif is a ribbon of musical notes running through a row of years. You do not need to draw a staff. A track list with years in the margin is the ribbon.

075, Our Greatest Hits, compiles stories. A hit may mention a song and then leave it off. 077, The Relationship Timeline, holds the long arc, including stretches with no soundtrack at all. Replay when you add a piece, or when one should fade. Date the revision. Faded tracks can remain in an old list so you remember you let them go on purpose.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan's early piece is "Everyday People," which was playing from a diner ceiling the afternoon they split a grilled cheese and Rowan decided to stay another hour. Whose choice: the room's. What it carries: the decision, and the fact that neither of them has ever agreed on the soup. Rowan says this before any sound. Vale asks, "Booth or counter?" Rowan says, "Counter. My knee against the bracket." Vale names what landed: "The staying, not the chorus." They play twenty seconds, quietly, and stop.

Vale's middle piece has no band. During a power cut they listened to a weather-radio voice because it was the only steady sound in the apartment. Vale's liner note: "Source, the weather service. It carries competence, and the dark, and your hand finding the flashlight without narrating it." They do not play a clip. The title is enough. Rowan says, "What I hear it carrying is the hour we were useful to each other in a small room." Vale says, "Yes. Leave the heroism out. We were useful."

Rowan almost queues a song from before Vale. Rowan says, "This next one belongs to an older story. I am skipping it." Vale says, "Skipped." No explanation follows. That skip is part of the soundtrack.

For now, Vale chooses an instrumental, Satie's Gymnopédie No. 1, which Rowan hums inaccurately on some Sundays. Vale loves the piece. Rowan tolerates the humming and likes the quiet underneath it. They mark that tolerance as a true chapter: one person glad, one person willing, nobody pretending the tastes match.

The private encore is the weather-radio story, which they can tell with no file at all. The sound-off test is the diner: counter, bracket, grilled cheese, the decision to stay. The song's name is a cue they may lose. The hour remains. They do not print a lyric. They do not build the list inside anyone's branded couple-playlist product. Six was the plan. Five were honest. Five is the record.

## Afterward

Look at the liner notes before you close the player.

Which story survived with the sound off?
Where did a quiz almost replace a question?
Which track did you skip, and was the skip allowed to stand?
What is the private encore, and what are you willing to let fade?

A soundtrack can include silence and a song somebody only tolerated. That is a life. It does not have to sound like a shop window.

## Our Story

Date the session, the version, and the place. Note the volume you actually used, including silence. Mood and initials are optional.

Liner notes can stay private. Sharing them is optional. The shared page is for the sequence, if you want one, and for the track that is yours for reasons only the two of you know. You may name that track without explaining it.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- A piece of music you chose, the chapter or feeling it holds, and the story attached to it.
- The piece you would keep as a private encore, and the story that should be told even when the song is unavailable.
partner_b:
- A different piece, your chapter, and your story.
- A track you skipped or a volume you set, silence included, and the chapter that piece would have carried.
shared:
- The sequence, if you want one, and the track that is yours for reasons only the two of you know.
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- What you add to the soundtrack, and what you let fade.
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
