## The hook

Draw one timeline together, and keep a disputed date as two notes. A photo album is a corridor of good light. Trips, dinners, a coat that photographed well. A timeline has a ruder job. It has to make room for the winter someone was ill, the month the money went strange, the move you remember in two different seasons, the repair after a stupid fight, and the joy that was real in the same year as the strain.

You draw that line together. Pencil, cards, or a shared screen. The beginning, written apart, is 073, The First Chapter. Here you may place both dates and tick them if they differ. When a date clashes, you tick it disputed and keep both notes. You do not average a March and a June into a plausible April so the line will look sure of itself. A scrapbook of highs drops the premise. So does one partner's chronology adopted as the official past because that partner remembers more street names.

## Why it might matter

Memory stores peaks and lets the connective tissue go thin. The tissue is often where a relationship actually changed: a role, a household, a body, a boredom, a decision that never became an anecdote. Putting those on a line you can both see makes the plot large enough for two editors.

Family chronologies are an old clinical and kitchen-table tool. This chapter borrows the line and leaves the clinic. You are making a shared drawing both people can edit. Some important changes have no event attached. A shift in trust, a season of distance, a return of ease: those can sit on the line as weather, with an approximate year and no costume of a single dramatic day.

An oral-history study of married parents found that the manner of telling was associated with later stability in that sample. See B1992 in the notes. The researchers conducted an interview for the study. They did not hand couples a pencil timeline, and they did not show that drawing one predicts a future. Your map is a record you are willing to live with, gaps included. It predicts nothing, and it sentences no one.

## Before you begin

About 60 minutes. A long sheet on a table is enough. No wall-height display is required. Index cards left to right work. So does a shared screen if you are apart. Large print, or verbal placement ("put the move after the winter"), covers low vision. If fatigue is in the room, agree on a stopping year and continue another day. A partial map can be real.

Pencils, or any mark you can revise. Dates may be approximate. Marriage, children, and a single household are common landmarks and are not required. A timeline of two people who have never shared an address is still a timeline.

Either of you may leave a period unmarked and label it "private span." Losses, pregnancies, migrations, and breakups that involve other people come onto the line only with care for who is in the room and who is not. You may include at least one challenge if you are both willing. You may also let a challenge sit on the line without working it through tonight.

This is a poor hour for a performance review. The partner with the sharper memory does not narrate the other's years. A felt turning point survives a calendar that disagrees with it. The calendar can be noted. It does not get to evict the experience.

You may pass a category, pause, or stop at the year you have energy for.

## The experience

**Lay the line.** Mark years or chapters along the sheet, not only the pretty dates. Leave space. Crowding the early years into a corner is how later life becomes a footnote.

**Add, in pencil, taking turns.** Each partner contributes, and then the other may add, move, or dispute. Aim to touch these if you can, in any order:

A beginning point. The two of you may not share one. 073, The First Chapter, is the hour for drafting those beginnings apart. Here you may place both dates and tick them if they differ.

A repair. Something that went wrong at a human scale and was met, even partly.

A change of role, home, or health.

A joy.

A loss, if you both want it on the page.

A turning point in how you understood each other.

At least one challenge, if you are both willing. Illness, money, sex, conflict, boredom, a move that did not feel like an adventure: the line can hold one true difficulty without becoming a dossier of injuries.

Write small. A slug and a year are enough. "Sink fight, then the sentence we still use. 2016, or 2017." When memories clash, write "disputed" and keep both notes beside the mark. Do not blend them into a false precision. Some changes get a bracket with no date at all: "the year wanting each other went quiet, then returned." That is a legal entry.

The person who remembers a scene may not be the person who felt it as a turn. Both can place a mark. If one of you says, "That was logistics to me, and it was a turn for you," the turn stays, with that difference written under it.

**Close.** Name one stretch that built something you still use. A way of apologizing, a Sunday habit, a rule about money, a joke that lowers the temperature. Then name one stretch that deserves a kinder retelling than the one you usually give it. You do not have to perform the kinder retelling tonight. Naming the stretch is enough. If you want one sentence of it, each of you may write one, and they do not have to match.

Optional: photograph the line and add a caption in both voices. "One of us remembers the move in March. The other remembers June. We are keeping both." Store the photo only if you both want it kept.

Challenges can remain unfinished. 060, The Repair Shop, is where a minor misstep gets acknowledgment and repair. A whole hard year is larger than one repair, and it can stay on this line as history.

## Three ways to play

**25 minutes.** Place a beginning, one joy, one change, and one challenge if you are both willing. Tick anything disputed. Skip the photograph and the kinder retelling. Say which year you are willing to reach later. A short line with gaps is a finished session. What you postpone is range, not honesty.

**60 minutes.** The fuller set of marks, disputed notes kept side by side, then the stretch you still use and the stretch that deserves a kinder retelling. This is the version that feels like a map rather than a highlight reel.

**90 minutes.** Work in two passes. First pass: place the marks without debate, each person adding while the other listens. Second pass: disputes, private spans, and the closing sentences. If energy drops, the stopping year is a real boundary. Write it on the line. The extra time is for a second pass over what you already placed, so the first speaker's memory does not become the default.

## Going deeper

On a later day, choose one bracket that has no event, only a change in weather between you, and give it three lines: what shifted, what you each thought it meant at the time, what you are willing to say now. Leave the old guesses visible.

You can also add a mark for a success that never looked like a party: a bill paid down, a diagnosis faced, a boring month you got through without contempt. Success on this map includes endurance.

If the line starts to prosecute, stop and read the disputed notes aloud as notes, not as charges. The map records. It does not sentence.

## Variations and replay

A table, a notebook turned sideways, or a shared document with a horizontal list: all of these are the line. Verbal placement works when writing is hard. Cards can be reordered by the person who did not write them, with the original order photographed first if you want the disagreement preserved.

The motif is a long string with knots for turning points. You do not need string. Pencil knots are enough.

075, Our Greatest Hits, is the repertoire of stories you like to retell. This chapter keeps the hard arc available so the hits do not have to carry it. Replay when a new year needs a mark, or when a kinder retelling has become sayable. Add to the line. Date the attempt. Old maps stay, so a later correction does not pretend the earlier memory never existed.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

They use a roll of paper and two pencils. Rowan places "bus stop, March" at the left. Vale places "hardware store, three weeks later" and ticks both "disputed start," with a note: "Both true. Different counts. See 073."

Vale adds a winter when Vale's father was ill. The mark says "hospital winter" and nothing else. They do not open it tonight. Rowan adds the year they changed cities and writes "March." Vale writes "June" beside it and ticks "disputed." Neither reaches for a lease to settle it. Rowan says, "The lease can be right and my body can still remember mud." Vale says, "June stays."

A repair: a fight about a sink that was really about being spoken to sharply. Under it, Rowan writes, "We still use 'I was sharp.' 2016 or 2017." A joy: the ferry orange, which also lives on their hits list, placed here as a date and not retold in full. A change of role: Rowan went to four days a week. A turning point: the lobby with the child's coat, which Vale marks as a change in understanding and Rowan marks as "a Tuesday I remember for the elevator."

They name what they still use: the sentence "I was sharp," which ends certain arguments before they recruit an audience. They name what deserves a kinder retelling: the move. The usual version is "we were a mess." Vale writes, "We were tired and we kept the plants alive." Rowan writes, "I was frightened and I still chose the city." Both sentences stay. They photograph nothing. The paper stays on the table, gaps included, including a bracket with no story: "the quiet months," labeled and unopened.

## Afterward

Look at the line before you roll it up.

Which mark surprised you by belonging?
Where did you want the calendar to win against a felt turning point?
What did you leave as a private span, and was that respected?
Which stretch built something you still use, and which one is waiting for a kinder sentence?

A map with gaps is a grown-up document. You can be glad for the joys and still refuse to erase the winter.

## Our Story

Date the session, the version, the place, and the years the line actually covers. Mood and initials are optional. Note whether you finished or paused, and any stopping year you agreed.

Personal additions can stay on a private card. They are optional to share. The shared page is the line you can both live with, disputes and gaps included.

## Notes

Reimagined from family chronologies and the ordinary wish to see a shared life in order. The editable line, with room for two memories of one date, is the mechanism. No study has tested this paper timeline.

See B1992. Buehlman, Gottman, and Katz conducted an Oral History Interview for their study. It concerned dating and marital history, philosophy of marriage, and change over time. The prompts fair to name are good times and bad times, philosophy, and change. The sample was selected married parents. Methods recruited 56 families; the summary and follow-up concern 52. Over about three years, the manner of telling was associated with later marital stability. Fondness, we-ness, negativity, chaos, and disappointment ran in the directions stated in the abstract. The association is in the manner of telling. It is not a test of a couple's pencil timeline and not a treatment. No divorce-prediction percentage belongs here.

See GOTTMAN. The love map is a practitioner metaphor. This chapter does not turn it into a quiz, and the map does not predict a future. A holiday-and-trip line that skips difficulty has dropped the premise. Some important changes have no event attached.
