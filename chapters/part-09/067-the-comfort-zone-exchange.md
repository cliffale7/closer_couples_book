## The hook

Everybody has a small territory where their hands already know the next move. A chorus. A stitch. A stretch. A pan. A block they could walk with their eyes on the shop windows. From the outside, that territory can look like a personality. Up close, it is usually a set of steps, and the steps can be taught at beginner size.

This chapter is a trade. One of you hosts something you can already guide. The other is new to it, and has already heard what it asks of a body, a noise budget, and a room. Then you switch. The edge of familiarity is the point. Reluctance is information. It is a useful kind.

## Why it might matter

Showing someone "my thing" is an old custom between friends and between partners. The custom gets better when the slice is small, the demands are spoken first, and both people have a turn as the guest. You learn the texture of a world the other person already lives in. You also learn what you do when you are bad at something in front of the person who is good at it.

The First-Time Challenge (065) is for an activity neither of you knows. The World Through Your Eyes (070) is for an interest that matters deeply, hosted so its meaning can be heard. The Shared Courage Experiment (072) is a manageable challenge with an encouragement role. Stay here for a hosted introduction at beginner scale. This chapter claims no boost from a study. A good visit is a good visit.

## Before you begin

Each of you will offer two activities you know well enough to shrink. The receiver picks one. Unfamiliar is not a surprise. The guest reads a plain description before anything starts, and may stop at the threshold.

"Threshold" means that offer is done. Pick the other one, or stop for today. "Pause" stops a step. Passing a debrief question is a complete answer. You may change a prompt.

The host adapts pace, grip, noise, and posture. A comfort zone for one body can be pain or overload for the other, so acceptability belongs to the guest. Put the alternatives in the offer: a seated version, a large-motor version, a written version, a quiet version. Vision and hearing decide the channel. If the guest needs the steps in text, the steps are in text. If the guest needs them spoken, they are spoken. Fine-motor trouble and balance trouble are reasons to change the offer, and the guest does not have to earn that change.

Public performance is off the table. No audience, no posting, no "just try it in front of them." Cost stays at whatever you both accept, and the default is nothing. Duration is a time box you write down.

Any hands-on help waits for a yes, and the yes can be withdrawn mid-step. A stretch is not a sexual invitation. If you want a sexual conversation, have it somewhere else, on purpose. Finishing a beginner slice is not an agreement to be touched.

If a fear is the main flavor of an offer, choose a different offer. Interesting and unfamiliar is the target. Frightening is a different chapter, and even there the ceiling belongs to the person who feels it.

Long-distance works on a camera with materials you both can get, or with activities that need none: a chorus hummed, four phrases of a language, a stretch you can see and also skip. Mail is optional. The second hosting still happens, same call or a named later time.

## The experience

**The demand card.** Each offer gets a card, a note, or a large-type screen. Two cards from each of you, so four cards are on the table.

- Name of the activity.
- What we will actually do, in a few sentences, at beginner size.
- Time box.
- Seated or standing, and the alternative.
- Noise, mess, audience (audience is none), cost, duration.
- What a beginner can skip.
- The channel: spoken, written, shown, or hands-on.

Beginner size means a slice, not a conversion. Useful shapes, if you need them: eight bars of a song you can also speak as rhythm; one round of a card game you already know, taught from the rules you can say aloud; a stretch in which every position is optional, including sitting still; one step of a dish the guest can do with a spoon; one flat block you already walk, with a turnaround; four phrases of a language, repeated and then dropped; one craft stitch, or a large two-finger version if the real stitch is too fine.

**Pick.** Trade cards. Each guest chooses one of the host's two offers. If both of you are eager and the clock is short, do the shorter time box first. If an offer fails the guest's body, noise, or nerve, it is declined at the threshold and the other offer comes up. If both offers fail, that person still hosts later, using a new pair of cards you write together at a smaller scale. Skipping the second hosting is how the exchange collapses into a tour of one world. Book the second turn before you leave the table if you cannot do it today. Write the day and the time.

**Host.** Teach for at most 3 minutes. Then do the thing together inside the time box. 10 minutes is a solid standard slice. The host demonstrates once, then the guest tries, then you do it side by side if the activity allows. The host may correct a step. The host may not collect an audience, speed up to show off, or add a harder trick in the last minute.

The guest may stop mid-slice. Stop is data. The host asks one practical question, once: "Smaller, or done?" Then the host follows the answer.

**After each slice.** Two minutes, guest first.

- What was unfamiliar?
- Where did you want to stay?
- Where did you want out?

The host listens. The host does not recruit. There is no homework unless the guest asks for a way to try again.

Then you switch roles. The person who just floundered gets to be the one who knows the next thing. That turnaround is the exchange.

## Three ways to play

**Short, 25 minutes.** Write all four cards, smaller if you must. Do one 8-minute slice. Ask the three questions. Book the second hosting before you stop, with a day and a time. The short version delays a turn. It does not cancel it.

**Standard, 60 minutes.** Four cards, two slices the same day, about 10 minutes each, plus the choosing and the two short debriefs. Leave a few minutes between them so the first guest can come back into their body.

**Extended, 100 minutes.** Keep the beginner scale and the exits. Each slice may run to 25 minutes if the guest still wants it. The extra time is for a second try at the same slice, or for a slower pace, not for a performance or a new risky add-on. Both people still host.

## Going deeper

If you want one more question after both turns, use this. What did reluctance tell us that enthusiasm would have skipped?

A guest who wants out at the doorway may be protecting hearing, pride, a bad memory of being laughed at in school, or a simple lack of interest. Those are different facts. You only learn which one if the guest feels like saying. They may pass. A host who feels a sting when the guest stops can say "I wanted you to love it" and then let the want sit there without a recruitment speech.

You can also name the pleasure, if there was some. Being taught a small thing by a person you love is a particular warmth. Being the teacher, and shrinking your expertise until it fits, is another.

## Variations and replay

Replay in a few weeks with two new offers. The old hobby can return only at a new slice, and only if the guest asks.

Low energy uses songs spoken as rhythm, four phrases, or a card round at the table. Pain uses seated offers only, written into the card before the pick. Sensory limits use the quiet, low-mess, low-smell versions, chosen by the guest. Budget stays at zero unless a supply is already in the house.

The long-distance replay uses the camera and a shared clock. If one of you cannot get the materials, switch to an offer that needs none. The guest's channel still governs. A beautiful demonstration the guest cannot perceive is a film, not a visit.

Nobody has to take up the hobby afterward. A pleasant 10 minutes can be the whole relationship between a person and a craft.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you.

Rowan's cards are a knitting cast-on and the chorus of a song from their aunt's kitchen. Vale's hands hurt on the needles, so Vale says "threshold" to the cast-on after hearing "fine motor, both hands, 10 minutes." They take the chorus. Rowan knows it well enough to slow it down and to speak the rhythm instead of singing when Vale's ears want less melody. They get through eight bars twice. Vale does not want a choir. Rowan does not offer one.

Vale's cards are a one-block walk Vale does on ordinary Tuesdays, and a stretch Vale can guide from a chair. Rowan picks the stretch because the block is icy. Every position is optional. Rowan skips the one that asks for a twist and stays for the shoulder roll, hands on Rowan's own shoulders. Vale says, "Smaller or done?" once, when Rowan goes quiet. Rowan says, "Done, and that roll was the part I liked."

They write the reluctance down as information. The needles were pain. The choir was simply unwanted. The shoulder roll can be visited again. Nobody adopts a new identity before dinner.

## Afterward

Thank the host for shrinking the thing, and thank the guest for being new in public, which in this case means in front of one person. Skip the speech about growth.

If you want a last pass, use these.

- Which card was the true beginner's size?
- Where did the host adapt, and where did the host show off?
- Is the second visit already on a real day, if it did not happen today?

Then put the cards away. They are not a contract to become hobby people.
