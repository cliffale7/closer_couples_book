## The hook

Twenty-four slips, a private cut to five, a draw, and a pass. The questions that feel slightly impolite, slightly obvious, or slightly too alive for a weeknight are still in the room. They get a job only if chance draws them, and either of you may pass. The pass is the whole turn. No reason, no substitute dare, no speech about openness.

## Why it might matter

A host with a clever list can turn curiosity into an interview. The person who did not write the list spends the evening on someone else's timing. A box you both fill, then refuse to curate in the moment, asks something neither of you selected with eye contact. That is a different feeling from being praised, and a different feeling from being quizzed.

Rarity is the point. Invasion is a different sport. A question you are rarely asked can be about the first hour of a free Saturday. It can be about which compliment you trust least. Willingness to answer every slip is a poor way to measure love. The interesting information is often which question got air, which one went back, and whether a pass could sit in the room without a trial.

## Before you begin

Sit where you can both reach the bowl. Phones face down. If you are on a call, each of you needs a private list and a way to roll a number, described under variations.

Before anyone writes, name topics that are off the table tonight if either of you wants them off: sex, family trauma, ex-partners, money, faith, or anything else you choose. A nod is enough. A question that asks someone to disclose harm, an illness, or a third person's secret is a misfit for a random draw. If one appears, pass it and tear it up.

Two writing manners. Avoid a question you already know your partner will not want. And both of you supply slips. A bowl filled by one person is an interview. Rebuild it.

A pass is a finished turn. Ending the session early is a finished session. An answer is not a promise to act on what was said, and it is not consent to touch or to sex if a question wanders near either.

## The experience

### Build the box

Use the 24 slips at the end of this section, or blank paper if you want every question to be original. Cut the printed slips apart. Shuffle them face down. Deal 12 to each person.

Take your 12 somewhere the other person is not reading over your shoulder. Set aside any slip that assumes a body, a family form, or a sexual life you do not share. You do not owe a category name. From what remains, keep five. If fewer than five remain, write originals until you have five. You may also rewrite a kept slip in your own words, or swap one for a question you write from scratch. Questions you are rarely asked belong in the box. So do questions you rarely dare to ask. Pocket or tear up the rest without showing them.

Fold each kept slip so the wording is inside. Put an initial on the outside, small, so a drawn slip can be offered back to its author. Drop the five folds into the bowl. Do not comment on what you think the other person discarded.

If you would rather skip the printed bank, each of you writes five original questions under the same privacy rules and folds them the same way. The bank is there so the evening can start without invention. Originals are welcome. They are not a higher grade of honesty.

### A turn

Flip a coin. The winner draws first. After that, you alternate. A session holds at most four draws. Passes count. An early "close the box" counts as the end, even if draws remain.

The person who draws opens the slip. They may read it silently, or ask to hear it aloud.

They then choose one of three endings.

**Answer.** Speak as long as you want. When you are done, say "that's the answer." That sentence closes the turn. The listener may ask one question only if you invite it. After the close, the listener says "thank you" or stays quiet. A follow-up that corners a finished answer is outside the game.

**Pass.** Say "pass," or set the slip down and tap it once if you want a gesture instead of a speech. The listener says "Passed." The turn is complete. Leave it alone: no groan, no bargain, no translation of what the pass "really means," no dare to replace it. Tear up the passed slip. It does not return to the bowl.

**Offer back.** If the initial on the outside is yours, this option does not apply. You answer your own slip, or you pass. If the initial is your partner's, you may say, "This one is yours. Do you want it?" The author may answer, pass, or return the slip unread to be torn up. Their choice ends the turn. Offering a slip back is an invitation, not a penalty.

Every answered, passed, or returned draw counts as one of the four. Stop at four even if the bowl is full and the mood is good. Unread slips are torn up, or handed back face down to the person whose initial is on the outside, to be destroyed without a reading. Peeking "just to know what we almost asked" reopens a door the cap exists to keep shut.

A phone timer may mark 5 minutes as a check-in during an answer. At the chime, the speaker says whether they are done. The chime is not a buzzer and not a permission to interrupt.

Alcohol is a drink, if you even want one. It is not the price of a slip.

### The slips

Print and cut on the borders. One question per slip. Wording faces inward once folded.

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** Each of you keeps three slips. Two draws, same three endings, same silence around a pass. Two draws are a whole session.

**40 minutes.** Five slips each. Four draws at most. This is the version the bowl was built for: enough chance to surprise you, a ceiling so the night cannot turn into a deposition.

**70 minutes.** Still four draws. The extra time is for writing original questions in place of the bank, for answers that want a quiet minute before they start, and for tearing up the unread slips without a recap. If four feels like too many, stop earlier. The cap is a ceiling. A second bowl belongs to another day.

## Going deeper

On a later day, the listener may ask for one follow-up on a question that was answered. The speaker may decline. A decline completes that conversation. Passed slips stay passed. You do not reopen them by calling the new talk "just a follow-up."

You can also each bring one question you wish someone had asked you years ago, fold it, and play a two-draw box under the same rules. Old wishes can be tender. The pass still works.

If a drawn question was really a complaint about your partner wearing a question mark, say so after the session, in ordinary language, and retire that move. The box is a poor costume for a grievance you already know how to name.

## Variations and replay

Two colors of paper can replace initials. Color A is one person's slips, color B the other's. Offer-back still works.

Large print is the same game. If reading a fold aloud is easier, the listener reads it when asked and then returns to listening.

Apart, number your five private slips. Use a die, rerolling six, or any shared way of picking a number from one to five. A coin decides whose list is live. The person who did not write the slip answers, passes, or asks the author to take it. Unpicked numbers stay unread. Four draws remain the ceiling.

Replay with a new set of questions. A slip someone passed last time is not owed this time. Date the session so a later box can show whether the rarely asked questions moved.

The motif is a closed box and a hand setting one slip aside, unopened. A doodle of that is optional. The aside slip is the pass, and it stays unread.

005, The Things Nobody Thinks to Ask, is a direct interview of unusual questions. 047, Truth, Dare, or Discover, is where dares live, and a declined dare over there is its own complete turn. 087, The Question Behind the Question, keeps walking down from an answer. This box stops when the speaker stops.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

They have already named money as off the table. Rowan draws Slip 02, which happens to be in Vale's handwriting: "Which compliment do you trust least?" Rowan says the easygoing one, because Rowan edits a week until it looks smooth, and the compliment praises the edit. Rowan says "that's the answer." Vale says thank you and does not ask whether Rowan is, in fact, easygoing.

Vale draws a slip about what a free Saturday's first hour should contain, feels the room tilt toward a real negotiation about chores, and says "pass." Rowan says "Passed." The slip is torn. Nobody translates it.

Rowan's next draw is Rowan's own slip, about a skill that never comes up. Rowan answers: a knack for getting a jammed window to sit square, learned from a neighbor, useless in conversation and oddly precious. Vale offers one invited question, "Do you want that known, or was the answer enough?" Rowan says the answer was enough.

They stop at three draws because Vale says "close the box." The unread folds go back by initial and are torn over the sink. The session is complete.

## Afterward

Stay at the table for a minute before you turn the evening into a verdict about who is braver.

Which question are you glad got air?
Which pass, if there was one, still feels like it belongs to the person who passed?
Did either of you want to groan, explain, or sneak a leftover slip?
Is there a topic you want named as off the table before the next box?

You can be glad you played and glad the cap arrived. Unread questions can stay unread.

## Our Story

Date the session, the version, the place, and whether you closed the box early or played to the cap. Initials and mood are optional. Passing is a complete entry. Paraphrase a question on the page if the exact wording should stay unwritten.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- A question you put in the box, and whether you hoped it would be drawn. Paraphrase it if the exact wording should stay unwritten.
- One topic you kept out of the box on purpose, named only as a topic, or left blank.
partner_b:
- A question you drew or passed, and what the pass, or the answer, protected.
- What you said or did when your partner passed, if they passed.
shared: The question you are glad got air, and the one you put back.
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit: Are the rarely asked questions different now, and can you still pass without explaining?
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```

## Notes

Reimagined from question jars and other folk games of the "ask me something you never ask" kind. Those forms are folk games, not validated protocols. The twist kept here is the three mechanics together: questions contributed in private, drawn by chance, and declined with a free pass. See L1998. In diary studies of daily interactions, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. L1998 did not test a question box. See A1997. In Study 1, a roughly 45-minute escalating self-disclosure procedure among strangers produced greater post-interaction closeness than a small-talk comparison. Studies 2 and 3 did not find closeness differences for attitude matching, expected liking, or making closeness an explicit goal. That procedure is a different activity. Its question list is not in this box, and the participants were not a study of long-term couples. A pass completes the turn.
