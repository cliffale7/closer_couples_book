## The hook

"What would you do if" is an old way couples and friends poke at a future. Left loose, it becomes a test: did you give the loving answer, the ambitious answer, the answer that matches mine? Tightened a little, it becomes something more useful. An unexpected fork, answered in your own voice, with the first pull still visible and the regret you could actually live with named out loud.

What Would You Do If…? plays three forks. One is money. One is loyalty or a moral tug of ordinary size. One is a life-changing chance. Chance picks the order. You each answer for yourself. Your partner reflects the tradeoff. The room does not score the match.

## Why it might matter

You can know someone's favorite breakfast and still not know which regret they would sign. Money, loyalty, and a door that would rearrange a year are three places that knowledge hides, because the real forks arrive already tangled in logistics. A card lets the first pull show up before the reasonable speech smooths it over.

The product is the approach: first pull, value underneath, regret you could live with, and what you would want to talk about before acting. Matching answers are a pleasant accident when they happen. Different answers are a successful round. A hypothetical spoken here stays hypothetical. It is a way of thinking aloud. It does not spend the savings, accept the job, or take a side in a family fight.

## Before you begin

Separate the cards into three piles: Money, Loyalty, Opportunity. Keep the two warm-up cards in a fourth pile, face down, out of the main draw. Shuffle each main pile. Then write the three pile names on slips, shuffle those slips, and play the piles in that order. Use a coin only to decide who speaks first on the opening fork. After that, alternate who opens. Neither of you chooses the pile order to suit a point you hope to make.

Show each fork in writing. Take 2 quiet minutes before anyone speaks. If a card mirrors a current crisis, a real debt, a real job search, a real rupture, say "retire" and draw the next card in that pile. You do not have to reveal the crisis. This chapter does not collect confessions by dressing them as hypotheticals.

If a card assumes a shared bank account, a sibling, a marriage, or a promise structure you do not have, swap the people and the money for a version that fits your actual life, or retire it. The chapter does not assume monogamy, marriage, or joint finances. Relationship forks stay inside the agreements you already have. A card is not a proposal to rewrite them.

Passing a fork is a full turn. The other person may still answer it, or you may draw a replacement for both of you. Either way, nobody owes a performance of decisiveness.

If the first real fork is too charged and the evening needs a sillier on-ramp, play one warm-up card, laugh, and then return to the premise. The warm-up is a doorway. It does not replace the three stakes.

## The experience

### One fork

Read the card aloud. Both of you can see it. Write for 2 minutes if you like. Then the opener answers all four beats, about 3 minutes.

1. **The first pull.** What you would want to do before you talk yourself into looking reasonable.
2. **The value underneath.** The thing the pull is protecting or chasing.
3. **The regret you could live with.** Name it as a regret, not as a flaw in your partner.
4. **Before acting.** What you would want to talk about with your partner, or with someone else, before you moved. "I would decide this alone" is an allowed answer. Say so, and say why that fork stays personal.

The listener reflects the tradeoff in one sentence: "You would spend ___ in order to keep ___." They do not announce the right answer. They do not translate the answer into a promise. A useful check, said lightly, is "The card did not just decide this." Then the other person answers the same fork with their own four beats, which may diverge. The first speaker reflects that tradeoff in the same sentence shape.

If you differ, say "Different is a result." If you lean the same way, say "We leaned the same way." Neither sentence scores the relationship. There is no bonus for agreement.

One foul to retire on contact: a fork built so that only one answer counts as love. If you feel the card, or your partner's follow-up, arranging that trap, name it and draw a different card. Rapid-fire dilemmas that never reach the value are the other foul. If you are three forks in and nobody has said a regret, slow down and finish the beats on the one in front of you. Drop the rest if you must.

Leave "what my partner would do" out of this sitting. That round has its own chapter. Here you answer only as yourself. Guessing your partner is a different instrument, and it turns their turn into a grade of your imagination.

### The forks

Cut the cards. Keep the pile name with the card so a retired draw can be replaced from the same pile.

```cards
Money · the object | A sudden debt in your name could be cleared by selling something the other person loves and uses. You could also keep the object and live a tighter year. Answer for yourself. If a real debt is already in the room, retire this card. The card does not sell anything.
Money · the quiet sum | You receive a sum that would loosen a daily money worry. A letter from the giver asks you not to tell the rest of the family the amount. Your partner would be living beside that secret if you accept the condition. You might tell them, tell everyone, decline the gift, or negotiate the condition. If this describes a real inheritance you have been hiding, retire the card.
Money · the cushion | A friend asks for a loan that would empty the cushion you count on. If your money is separate, use a cushion that is yours. You can say no, say yes, or offer a smaller amount that still changes the next few months. The friend is not in danger. Saying no is a complete answer.
Money · the ugly bonus | You are offered a legal bonus for work you find pointless, large enough to fund a year of something you care about. Taking it funds that thing. Leaving it keeps a story you tell about what you will not do for money. Poverty is not a virtue in this card, and ambition is not a vice. Both paths spend something.
Loyalty · the unwitnessed fight | Someone close to you, a sibling or a friend, asks you to take their side in a fight you did not witness. The other person in the fight also matters to you. You can take a side, refuse, or ask for time. You do not have new facts. Loyalty and honesty are both pulling, and so is the wish to stay out of a story you only half know.
Loyalty · the small lie | You learn that a friend lied to your partner about something small that will keep mattering if it continues. It is not a danger, and it is not an affair. You might tell your partner, ask the friend to fix it, or wait. If a real lie is what you are actually trying to announce, retire the card and speak plainly another way.
Loyalty · the favor | Someone with influence offers a legal favor that would help a person you love. You do not respect the someone. Doing the favor spends a standard you like having. Refusing spends a real convenience for the person you love. The favor is not sexual, not illegal, and not a threat.
Loyalty · the same night | Two people you love have asked for you on the same night. One asked first and is in a hard week. One is celebrating and would feel the absence. You can be in one room. You can also tell both the truth about the clash and disappoint them more evenly. Nobody is in danger. The hard week is ordinary hardship, not an emergency.
Opportunity · the other city | A one-year offer in another city would advance a craft you care about and interrupt the life you are living now. The offer, in this card, is still a hypothetical. It is not a job you have already accepted. Yes advances the craft and relocates the year. No keeps the current life and lets that particular door close.
Opportunity · your name on it | A six-month project would put work into the world with your name on it. It would also make you the person who is often gone. The work might matter. The absence would be definite. You can take it, refuse it, or try to reshape it, knowing the reshape might fail.
Opportunity · the delayed plan | A training you have wanted has one open seat. Taking it would spend savings you and your partner, or you alone, had named for a shared plan. The plan could wait. Waiting might become canceling. You can also let the seat go. If there is no shared plan, use a plan that is yours and still costly to delay.
Opportunity · the decent job | Someone you respect asks you to join a venture that fits what you are good at. It might fail within a year. Joining means leaving work that is dull, decent, and predictable. The dull job is allowed to be a real loss. The venture is allowed to be a real want. Neither is the moral winner by default.
Warm-up · breakfast or the chair | For a year you may keep either a perfect ordinary breakfast or a perfect chair. The other is gone. This card is a warm-up. Play it to loosen a heavy start, then return to a money, loyalty, or opportunity card.
Warm-up · the late train | Your train is late. You can reach only one of two low-stakes plans: a casual dinner you can reschedule, or a shop that closes in an hour where you meant to pick up a gift. Warm-up only. Then go back to the premise.
```

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** One card from any main pile. Both of you answer the four beats. Each of you reflects the other's tradeoff. Say whether you differed. Stop. One fork is a finished session. If that fork has to be retired, a replacement from the same pile still fits in the short version. A warm-up alone does not finish the chapter. It only opens the door.

**45 minutes.** Three cards, one from each pile, in the shuffled order. About 10 minutes a fork, including the quiet 2 minutes. Replace retired cards from the same pile until each pile has had one real hearing, or until you choose to stop because the evening has done enough. Stopping is allowed. A stack of untouched regrets is not a prize.

**75 minutes.** One warm-up, then the three main forks, with the text in front of you the whole time. Use the extra minutes on the regret and on the "before acting" beat, which are the ones people rush. You may replace one fork that mirrored a live crisis and still finish three. You may not add a fourth round of guessing each other's answers.

## Going deeper

Pick the fork where your regrets differed, if one did. Each of you says what you would want from the other in the hour before a real decision of that kind. Keep it concrete: a night to think, a look at the numbers together, a conversation with a friend, solitude. This is still not a promise to decide that way when a real fork arrives. It is a sketch of the hour before.

If your regrets matched, skip the victory lap and ask a smaller question: which beat was hardest to say, the first pull or the regret? The hard beat is usually the interesting one.

A real version, if it ever shows up, gets to be real. You can remind each other what you thought you would do. You also get to be different from the person on the card. The revisit page is for that surprise. The card is not a contract you broke.

## Variations and replay

On a call, read the card twice before the quiet. The reflection sentence does the work of proving you heard the tradeoff rather than the speech you expected.

Low energy: one fork, four beats spoken without paper, reflection, done. If thinking on the spot is the hard part, trade written beats and read them aloud with no extra debate.

Long distance is the same piles, photographed or copied, drawn by a shared number. Show the fork in writing even if you are tired. A fork only heard once is easy to warp into the argument you were already having.

Budget is paper. Mobility is a chair or a bed or a walk slow enough for the four beats. No physical challenge is attached. A game result here is talk. It is not consent to touch, to a move, or to a sale.

Replay when a real fork has come and gone, or when you want new cards because the old ones became too easy. Date the answers. "Then versus now" is often a change in which regret you are willing to hold, which is more interesting than a change in your slogan.

The motif is three doors under a single handwritten "if." If you draw it, leave the doors shut. The chapter opens them one at a time, on purpose.

These cards stay at recognizable adult stakes, and the voice that answers is your own.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Chance puts Money first. They draw the object. Rowan feels the first pull as "sell it and clear the debt," the value as relief from a weight that would otherwise run the next year, and the livable regret as the loss of a thing Vale loves and uses. Before acting, Rowan would want one conversation about what replaces the daily use, and a night between the talk and any listing. Rowan says the card did not just sell the object.

Vale's first pull is to keep it and take the tighter year. The value is that some objects are how a life feels inhabited. The livable regret is the stress of the tighter year, named as stress, not as Rowan being careless. Before acting, Vale would want the numbers on one page and no decision in the same sitting.

Rowan reflects, "You would spend a tighter year in order to keep the thing in use." Vale reflects, "You would spend the object in order to get the weight off the year." They say, "Different is a result." Nobody updates a budget. The card goes back in the pile. They are a little more readable to each other, and the object, which in their real kitchen is a heavy pan, stays where it is.

## Afterward

Before you turn answers into forecasts, ask:

Where did the first pull differ from the answer you think you should admire?
Which regret sounded livable in your mouth and expensive in your partner's?
Did any card try to announce a real decision? If it did, take that decision out of the game and speak it as itself.
Where would you decide together, and where did "I would decide this alone" show up?

You can be glad you know the approach and still meet the real fork differently. Leave room for that. The cards are paper.

## Our Story

Date the session, the version, the place, and which piles you actually played. A hypothetical on this page stays a hypothetical. Write the choice you argued for yourself. Leave your partner's choice on their page.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- The dilemma you took on — money, morality, a relationship, or a life-changing chance — and the choice you argued.
- The regret you could live with. This page does not bind you to act.
partner_b:
- Your choice on another horn of that dilemma, or on a different dilemma, so both lines of thought are in the record.
- What you would want to talk about before acting, and where the decision stays yours.
shared: Where you would decide together, and where the decision stays personal.
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit: Did a real version arrive, and were you who you thought you would be?
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```

## Notes

Reimagined from ordinary "what would you do" talk among friends and couples. The structure is three unexpected forks, in an order neither person controls, answered as oneself: first pull, value, livable regret, and the talk before acting. No empirical claim is made. No whitelist ID is claimed, and none is invented. A hypothetical answer is not a binding promise. The chapter does not moralize poverty or ambition, does not assume shared finances, marriage, or monogamy, and does not treat different answers as a failed match. Guessing a partner's choice belongs to 101, The Borrowed Brain Experiment, and stays out of this sitting.
