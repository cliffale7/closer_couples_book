## The hook

"What do you need?" is a kind question and a terrible interface when you are already underwater. Lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, and disconnected are not the same weather. The hug that steadies one of them can make another worse. The pep talk that helps a stressed afternoon can land on sadness like a flashlight in the eyes.

This is a menu you each write for yourselves. The rows are states, not a personality type. The point of writing them down is that your partner should not have to mind-read which night you are having. A signal, a word or a short text, can do that job when explaining is too hard.

## Why it might matter

Most people in a close relationship own one comfort strategy and apply it like a house key to every door. They talk, or they fix, or they hold, or they give space, because that is what they would want, or because it worked last March. Sometimes the key turns. Sometimes it bends in the lock while everyone stays polite.

Separating the states is the mechanism. What it looks like on you, what helps, and what makes it worse can change from row to row. A single strategy copied five times fails the premise, even if the strategy is loving. The menu also includes at least one help you can give yourself, so the page is not entirely a work order for the person who loves you.

This is a design used in kitchens and in couples' conversations, not a validated coping protocol. No attachment study and no coping-style study were opened for it, and none is claimed. It is also not a crisis plan. If you are in danger, or you need urgent mental health help, use the supports you already trust and the emergency options where you live. This page will not invent a protocol, and it will not replace one.

015, When I Feel Small, stays with the particular weather of feeling inadequate or overlooked. 028, Instructions for Loving Me, is about ordinary operating conditions. 025, What Loving Me Actually Looks Like, compares the acts that help you feel loved on a regular day. Tonight's card is wider, and it is allowed to contradict those other pages. A person can want company when they feel loved and quiet when they feel sad.

If you arrived hoping for one type of affection, set that hope down. Impett, Park, and Muise reviewed the popular love-language claims and found little empirical support for one primary love language, for exactly five, or for matching languages as what predicts satisfaction. People do seem to benefit from a range of affectionate behavior. These five rows are situations. The same person can need different help in each.

## Before you begin

A one-page grid each, on paper or in large type. Five rows, in this order if you want a shared map: lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, disconnected. Three columns: What it looks like on me. What helps. What makes it worse. You can talk the grid and let the other person write, then swap, as long as the author approves every cell.

Draft privately for about 15 minutes. Each cell is a behavior, not a vibe. "Give me space" becomes usable when you add the offer. "Say you will be in the next room and come back at nine." Include at least one help, somewhere on the page, that you can do without your partner. If a long walk is the only stress remedy and one of you cannot take a long walk, it cannot be the only stress remedy. Add the seated version, the shower, the dark room, the 10-minute nothing.

Mark which helps work across distance and which need a body in the room. A scheduled goodnight, a photo of an ordinary object, a call with no advice: those travel. A hand on a back does not. If either of you needs quiet, low light, or no touch on some rows, put those options on the menu. They are part of the design of your household, not a treatment claim.

You may leave a row blank. A blank is better than a guess you will resent. The "worse" column is not a charge sheet of past crimes, and it is not ammunition for a later fight. Store it in the shared notes only if you both want that memory. Otherwise it stays on the personal page or gets left off entirely.

A menu does not authorize touch, sexual reassurance, or a conversation someone has declined. Either of you can refuse an item in the moment even if it is printed. Menus get a review date. They are not permanent law.

## The experience

Fill every cell with a behavior. A lonely-help might be a 10-minute call with no advice, plus a text you can send a friend yourself. A stress-help might be "I will be in the next room until nine," or the dishes done without a report. If the same hug, the same talk, or the same space appears in every row, rewrite until the rows disagree in the places your life disagrees. The example later is one household. Yours can reverse it.

In the standard session you talk through all five rows, briefly. The partner reads a row back in a sentence. "When you are stressed, it looks like short sentences, it helps if I say I will be in the next room until nine, and a planning talk makes it worse." Then they mark the help with one of three phrases.

I can do this.
I can sometimes.
This conflicts with what I need.

Conflicts are named and parked. You do not score them tonight, and you do not solve a conflict by deleting the other person's weather. A later conversation can look for a third option. Tonight, the mark is enough. "I can sometimes" is a respectable adult answer.

If you are using the longer version, add a signal for each filled row. A word, or a text you can send when a speech is too much. Pick signals that are not already your ordinary chatter. "Yellow" can mean insecure. "Kettle" can mean sad, sit nearby, do not fix. Say the signals out loud once so both of you have heard them in a calm voice. A signal is a request to consult the row. It is not a command, and it can be answered with "I see kettle, and I can do the quiet. I cannot do the conversation after."

Put a review date on the corner. A month is a reasonable first check. So is a change in job, health, or housing.

## Three ways to play

**25 minutes.** The two states you hit most often. Fill all three columns for those rows, including one self-help. Read them back. Mark can, sometimes, or conflict. Leave the other rows for a day when you are not already in one of the two.

**55 minutes.** Private draft, then all five rows, briefly. Read-back, marks, and a review date. This is the version that keeps one comfort strategy from colonizing the week.

**70 minutes.** The full menu, plus a signal for each row you filled. Practice sending one signal in the room, with no emergency attached, so the word exists before you need it. Still park the conflicts. Extra time is not a courtroom.

## Going deeper

The "worse" column is where people get brave and then get unkind. Write the pattern, not the indictment. "Advice when I am sad" is a pattern. "The way you always steamroll me" is a case you may need to have, and it does not belong as a menu item you spring at 10 p.m. If the pattern is serious, say "this one needs its own conversation" and give it a time. The menu can point at the door. It should not pretend to be the whole house.

A self-help item that still needs your partner to react correctly is a partner item. Move it. If insecure or sad keeps showing up as hopelessness, panic, or a wish to disappear, this menu is too small. Use the help you have outside this book. You can keep the other rows.

## Variations and replay

Across distance, build one remote column in the margin: call, photo, scheduled goodnight, a sentence that does not require an answer. Do not ask one partner to purchase their way into the room. If fatigue or pain is in the week, every help has to be something the helper can do while tired. A menu of ideal-energy gestures is a brochure.

Replay on the review date. Retire any signal that has become a joke at someone's expense. Add a blank row, or blank one that no longer fits. Keep the page where you can find it on the night you need it. A signal you both remember is the working part.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan's lonely looks like scrolling old photos and going quiet. Help: Vale talks about an ordinary piece of their day for 10 minutes, no advice, and Rowan texts one friend. Worse: "You have me, so why are you lonely?" Stressed looks like a jaw and short sentences. Help: "I will be in the next room and back at nine," plus the dishes without a report. Rowan can also take a shower alone. Worse: opening the shared calendar. Insecure looks like jokes that shrink. Help: one specific true sentence about something Rowan did this week. Worse: a pile of "you are amazing," or a debate about whether the insecurity is logical. Sad looks like stillness. Help: sit nearby, low light, no questions for a while. Self-help: a playlist Rowan already trusts. Worse: a cheer-up montage. Disconnected looks like polite efficiency. Help: one shared ordinary task, phones in a drawer. Self-help: say "I feel far." Worse: a forced deep talk at midnight.

Vale's grid does not match, which is the point. Vale's stress wants company in the room and dislikes silence. Vale's sadness wants a walk that is allowed to become a lap of the apartment, and a report of the walk would make it worse. Vale's lonely is not photo-scrolling. It is over-scheduling. The help is one plan canceled without a mood attached. Vale's insecure wants the specific sentence, same as Rowan, and also wants no comments about the knee. Vale's disconnected looks like extra chores. The help is stopping at "good enough" and sitting down. The worse column says "a ranked list of what I got done."

They mark conflicts without scoring. Rowan's wish for next-room silence during stress conflicts with Vale's wish for company during stress. They park it. The signal words are kettle for Rowan's sadness and porch for Vale's stress, even if the porch is a hallway. They practice once. Rowan texts "kettle." Vale says, "Low light, I can do. I will skip the questions." Nobody hugs unless the row asked for a hug. Rowan's sad row did not.

## Afterward

Say the signals once more, while everyone is still steady enough to hear them.

Which state do you usually misread in yourself, calling it stress when it is loneliness, or sadness when it is disconnection?
Which help can you offer without resenting it, and which mark has to stay at "sometimes"?
What is the review date, and where will the menu actually live?

You learned a little cartography. You did not sign up for perfect weather response. A missed signal on a hard night is a human evening, and the menu will still be there tomorrow.

## Our Story

Date the session and the review date. Note which rows you filled, which you left blank, and whether the "worse" column is stored. Initials and mood are optional. Keep the signal words where you can find them.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- My signal words, and one help for each state I chose to fill in
- One help I can give myself when my partner is unavailable or at their limit
partner_b:
- Which of my partner's rows I can meet, which I can sometimes meet, and which conflict with what I need
- The state I most often misname in myself, and the signal I want used instead
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
