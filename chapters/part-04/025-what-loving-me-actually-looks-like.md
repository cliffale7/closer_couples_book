## The hook

The job is which acts land as love: two you can see or hear in Words, two in Gestures, and two in Behaviors. 028, Instructions for Loving Me, is operating notes, warning signs, and maintenance. This sheet is those acts.

"I love you" can be a true sentence in a kitchen and still leave the other person guessing which evidence counts. One of you may feel chosen when a text arrives that says only "off the train." The other may feel it when a laptop closes at the start of a story that will last a minute. Same household. Same affection. Different proof.

This sitting is a comparison. Each of you writes the words, gestures, and behaviors that actually land, at a grain an observer could see or hear. Then you look at the two lists together. You mark what already happens, what is news, and what is hard. You leave with a clearer picture of these two people. You do not leave with a type.

## Why it might matter

You have been loving someone in the dialect you trust, and you have been receiving love in the dialect you notice. Both of you can be sincere and still miss. A specific act gives the other person something they can repeat on a tired Thursday. A category gives them a mood to aim at, and moods are hard to schedule.

You may have met this question as a quiz. Gary Chapman's 1992 book popularized five labels and the idea that each person has a primary one. Impett, Park, and Muise reviewed that popular frame and found little empirical support for one primary love language, for the claim that there are exactly five, and for matching languages as what predicts satisfaction. People do seem to benefit from a range of affectionate behavior. That review is why the sheet in front of you has acts on it, and why a mismatch is information about you two, not a verdict that someone is unloving. Comparing lists is a design for this hour. The review did not test it as a treatment, and this chapter does not pretend otherwise.

What the hour can do is smaller and more useful. It can surface a kindness that has gone invisible because it already happens, and it can surface a wish the other person has been translating badly for months.

## Before you begin

You need two sheets and something to write with. One person can scribe while the other speaks, then you swap the pen. Draw three rows and label them yourselves: Words, Gestures, Behaviors. Leave any printed five-label cards in the drawer. A notes app is a fine sheet. Use the channel you already trust when a conversation matters. Large type if you want it.

Sit. If either body is in pain, keep every item inside what you can both actually do. A requested walk can become the chair by the window. If an item costs money or a free hour you do not have, write the version that exists this month, including a version that costs nothing. If you live apart, you will mark which lines survive a message and which ones need a person in the room. If surprise, crowds, or a heavy scent make a day worse, leave those out.

This chapter has no touch demonstration. A shared vocabulary for welcome touch lives in 022, The Language of Touch, and it can wait.

A line on the sheet describes what has helped. It is a request-shaped observation, and the other person may decline it. You may pass a row, pause, or revise the list next month. Feeling loved is allowed to change with the season. If what you need written down is mostly "stop doing this harm," write that in plain language. A cute paraphrase would hide the point.

## The experience

Take about 10 minutes apart. Silence helps. Each row gets two items.

The grain that works is visible or audible. "Text me when you arrive." "Sit on the same side of the table." "Say what you liked about the specific thing I cooked." The grain that collapses the hour sounds like "quality time," "acts of service," or "touch." Those are bins. Bins feel efficient, and they leave your partner sorting through them alone on a Wednesday.

Write from your life with this person, or from a pattern you have already felt in your own body. If you are early together and the sample is small, say so, and write the smallest true things. A sophisticated need invented for the sake of the page helps nobody.

Come back together. Read both sheets aloud, or trade them and read in silence if that keeps the evening kinder. Mark every item with one of three notes.

Already happens.
I did not know.
Hard for me.

"Hard for me" can mean skill, history, energy, money, or a real limit. You do not have to diagnose which one tonight. You do have to avoid turning "hard" into "you should not have wanted that."

Circle any behavior that shows up on both lists, even if the wording differs. A shared circle is pleasant. It is a poor compatibility score. Where the lists diverge, read the divergence as a map. One of you may want words that name a particular effort. The other may want a gesture that requires no speech. Both can be true here. Two different lists are what two particular people look like on paper.

If a line asks the other person to become less anxious, more confident, or generally improved, rewrite it as a behavior or cross it out. "Be less sharp when you are tired" is a personality repair. "Tell me you are fried before we open the bills" is a behavior. Keep the second kind.

If you live in different places, add a small mark to each item before you finish: message, or presence. A goodnight call can be loving and still miss someone who wanted a hand on their back at the sink. Naming the channel keeps a text from being treated as a failed visit.

Close by each naming one mark that surprised you. Stop before you draft a household constitution. One surprise is a full result.

## Three ways to play

**15 minutes.** One item per row. Read them. Mark each one. A short sheet is a finished session.

**35 minutes.** Two items per row, the three marks, and the circles. Leave time to say which "I did not know" item is interesting and which "hard for me" item is a real no. This is the version that lets the comparison work.

**55 minutes.** Use the standard sheet. Then each of you picks one "I did not know" item to try once in the coming week. The try is a single pass. Either of you can decline before it starts and again in the moment. Put the day somewhere you will see it if you like. Leave the scorecard out of the week. If the item stops being welcome, it stops.

## Going deeper

The useful friction is the item one of you loves and the other finds costly. Ask what a smaller version would look like, at the scale you named at the start. A daily letter can become one specific sentence. A standing evening out can become 20 undistracted minutes on the couch you already own. If the smaller version is still a no, the no stands. You have learned a limit, which is better data than a promise pulled out at the table.

The "already happens" column deserves a second look. Care that already works can disappear into the week. Hearing "you already do this" can be as moving as hearing a new request, and it gives you something to keep doing on purpose.

Lists age. A behavior that mattered during a move may go quiet once the boxes are gone. A behavior that mattered during a hard season of health may need to stay. Date the sheets. When you replay, bring the old pages and see which lines you still recognize. Revision belongs to the practice.

Once in a while a line is not a preference. It is a request that something harmful stop. If that is what the page is doing, say so and give it a different conversation. A loving-gesture list is the wrong folder. This chapter will not smuggle a repair into a row labeled Gestures.

## Variations and replay

Speech and writing are equal. The author of each line is the person the line is about, even when the other hand holds the pen. Read a dictated line back and let the author change the wording before it counts. If hearing is the easier channel, speak the lists and let the marks be verbal. If print is easier, trade pages and skip the performance.

Replay when work, pain, money, or a new living arrangement changes what is actually available. Keep the same three rows. The point of a replay is the new grain.

028, Instructions for Loving Me, is a portrait of how you run: operating notes, warning signs, and maintenance, including maintenance you do for yourself. 029, The Reassurance Menu, sorts help by lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, and disconnected. 032, The Everyday Devotion Experiment, takes a few of these concrete preferences into several ordinary days and asks you to notice the effects, including the flat ones. Tonight is only the comparison.

## Examples

Fictional. Rowan and Vale are an illustration, not a portrait of you.

Rowan's words: "Text me when you get off the train, even if the text is only 'off.'" Gestures: "Sit on the same side of the booth when we eat out." Behaviors: "Tell me what you liked about the specific thing I cooked. 'Dinner was great' slides off."

Vale's words: "Use my name when you thank me." Gestures: "Hand me the chipped blue mug before I ask." Behaviors: "Close the laptop when I start a story, including a short one."

Vale marks the train text as already happens, the booth as news, and the cooking sentence as hard, because Vale grew up in a kitchen where praise of food was treated as fuss. Rowan marks the laptop as news, and marks the mug as a habit that already exists on good mornings. They look for a circle and almost miss it. Then Rowan says the train text and the closed laptop are the same shape: a signal that someone has arrived. Vale agrees. They circle that shape in the margin. They do not name a type.

Vale offers the booth as a one-time try next Saturday, and says the specific food sentence may stay hard. Rowan says, "Then tell me one ingredient you noticed. That would count." Vale says, "I can try the ingredient. I will not promise a speech." That is a complete negotiation. The sheets go in a drawer with the date on the corner. Nobody totals a column.

## Afterward

Before you leave the table, each of you says three behaviors you want remembered, in your own words, and one thing you learned about the other. Skip the type. Skip the score. If you chose a single try for the week, name the day and the exit. Either of you can cancel it without building a case.

Ask each other one plain question. Which mark was a relief, and which one do you want left alone for now? Then put the pens down. The lists can be revised. They do not have to be obeyed before dessert.

## Our Story

Date the session. Note the version, the place, and whether you finished or paused. Initials and mood are optional. Write the behaviors in the wording you want to reread, not in a category name.

The two personal pages are yours. Share them, keep them, or leave them blank. Unread is a complete way to use them. The shared notes are the part you write together.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused

- One item I learned about my partner, and whether I can try it once or need to decline it

- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything

- Then versus now
```
