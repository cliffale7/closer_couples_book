# Dossier 005 — The Things Nobody Thinks to Ask

- Catalog ID: 005
- Part: 1 — The Art of Really Knowing Someone
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

Ask unusual, specific questions about beliefs, habits, memories, preferences, and private thoughts.

## Must preserve

The questions have to be unusual and specific, and they have to cover more than one of the premise's kinds: beliefs, habits, memories, preferences, private thoughts. "What's your favorite movie?" fails. A famous published list pasted into the chapter also fails, because the mechanism is fresh curiosity, not a ritual the couple could find elsewhere.

## Evidence

L1998: intimacy in daily interactions tracked disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness; emotional disclosure predicted more strongly than factual disclosure in Study 2. The study did not test unusual question prompts, and it does not rank any particular question as intimacy-producing.

A1997's Study 1 used escalating disclosure tasks of about 45 minutes against small talk, among people who were not long-term couples in a falling-in-love trial. Studies 2 and 3 did not find closeness effects for attitude matching, expected liking, or an explicit closeness goal. That procedure is a different activity. Its question list is not a source text for this chapter.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Question games are a folk pastime, from conversation cards to late-night "tell me something true." Classification stays reimagined. The categories and the ban on obvious prompts are the design. There is no validated question bank behind them.

## Design notes

Materials: blank cards. The writer should supply original sample questions in all five kinds, clearly marked as examples the couple may discard.

Setup: together, name any zones already off the table. Then each person writes three questions they have never asked this partner. A question qualifies if it is hard to answer with one word and easy to decline.

Standard round, about 30 minutes: draw and answer six questions in total, alternating who asks. The asker gets one follow-up. After each answer, the asker reflects one specific detail they heard before the next card. That reflection is the responsiveness piece; skip it and the chapter becomes trivia. Short: two questions each. Extended: a second pass in which each person rewrites their dullest question into something more particular.

What makes the instructions fail: generic favorites; trick questions; both people asking only about sex or only about childhood because that seems "deep"; interviewing without ever answering; treating a pass as a clue and circling back.

Accessibility. Speech or writing both work. Vision: read questions aloud. Hearing: trade cards and write answers if speech-reading is tiring. Mobility and pain: seated. Long-distance: a shared note or a call. Sensory overload: cap the session at the short set. Private thoughts can be answered in a sentence rather than a confession.

Journal cue: the question that surprised them, and the answer they are willing to keep. Unasked leftovers can sit in a personal note, not the shared page.

## Consent and harm

A pass needs no reason. Questions about other people's secrets, current harm, or a body the partner has marked as private are out even if they sound specific. Unusual is not the same as invasive.

## Red flags

Do not reproduce the A1997 question list, in whole or in reconstructed paraphrase. Do not claim a research basis for any sample question in the chapter. Do not sell the stack as a closeness dose.
