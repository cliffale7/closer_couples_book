# Dossier 006 — The Museum of Me

- Catalog ID: 006
- Part: 1 — The Art of Really Knowing Someone
- Access date: 2026-10-14

## Source premise

Curate an imaginary museum of objects, people, songs, and events representing your life; give your partner a tour.

## Must preserve

One partner curates and the other is a visitor on a tour. The collection has to include objects, people, songs, and events, each standing for a life rather than for a mood board. If they only tell stories with no museum frame, or if the visitor never gets to stop in front of a piece, the premise drops.

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Memory palaces, house tours, and "soundtrack of my life" games are folk forms. Museums also suggest labels, order, and a visitor who may ask. Classification stays reimagined. This tour's four galleries are a book design, not a tested method.

## Design notes

Materials: four cards or a single sheet divided into Object, Person, Song, Event. Optional: one real object in the room. The museum can stay entirely spoken.

Standard, about 40 minutes. Each curator prepares four exhibits. Every label needs three lines: what it is, which stretch of life it holds, why it is in the collection. The tour is in the curator's order. The visitor may ask one question per exhibit and may not add a rival story until the tour is over. Then they swap. Short: one exhibit from each gallery, told in a single pass of about 15 minutes total. Extended: a "closed stack" exhibit that is named but not fully opened.

Songs: name the piece and the scene it marks. Do not print lyrics. People: the living and the dead can be included with the curator's judgment about what is theirs to tell. Events: one scene, not a decade.

What makes the instructions fail: a craft project so elaborate they never tour; a speech with no visitor role; four exhibits that are all achievements; the visitor correcting the label. Imaginary exhibits are enough. A shoebox diorama is an option for people who like making things, not the activity itself.

Accessibility. Vision: the tour is spoken; tactile objects are optional. Hearing: written labels, read in silence, then questions in writing. Mobility and pain: the "walk" can be from chair to chair or entirely in place. Long-distance: camera on one object, or a shared page of labels. Sensory overload: four exhibits is the ceiling for a first tour. Crowded rooms and background music are unnecessary.

Journal cue: the exhibit title, one line from the label they want remembered, and a sketch or photo only if the curator agrees.

## Consent and harm

Entry 102, The Museum of Unsaid Things, is a different exhibit: labels for what was left unsaid, pairing what happened with what the person wished the other understood. This chapter is a tour of objects, people, songs, and events that represent a life.

Some "people" exhibits will involve family harm or an ex-partner. The curator chooses the plaque's detail. The visitor does not demand the story behind a closed stack.

## Red flags

Do not turn the tour into a search for hidden objects. Do not quote song lyrics. Do not claim that metaphor or memory palaces have been shown to improve this relationship.
