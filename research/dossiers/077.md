# Dossier 077 — The Relationship Timeline

## Source premise

> Map key moments, challenges, successes, changes, and turning points together.

## Must preserve

A joint map of key moments, challenges, successes, changes, and turning points, with room for two memories of the same date. A scrapbook of highs, or one partner’s chronology adopted as official, loses the map.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Family chronologies and relationship timelines are common clinical and folk tools. Buehlman, Gottman, and Katz (1992) used a semistructured oral history of dating, hardship, philosophy, and change, then coded the telling. In that selected sample of married parents, the way the history was told correlated with later stability over about three years. The study did not test a couples’ paper timeline, did not assign this exercise as an intervention, and must not be quoted for a prediction percentage. The chapter’s timeline is a shared drawing both people can edit. It is not that interview and not a validated protocol. Gottman’s love-map metaphor is a practitioner frame only.

## Design notes

Materials: a long sheet, a digital line, or index cards arranged left to right. Time: 45–70 minutes. Mark years or chapters, not only pretty dates.

Each partner adds, in pencil: a beginning point (they may differ; see 073), a repair, a change of role or home or health, a joy, a loss, a turning point in how they understood each other. Include at least one challenge if both are willing. Dates can be approximate. When memories clash, tick “disputed” and keep both notes. Do not average them into a false precision.

Close by naming one stretch that built something you still use, and one stretch that deserves a kinder retelling. Optional journal artifact: photograph the line and add a caption in both voices.

Failure points: holiday-and-trip timelines that skip illness, money, sex, conflict, or boredom; using the line as a performance review; the partner with the better memory narrating the other’s years.

Accessibility: a table works; no wall-height display is required. Large print and verbal placement (“put the move after the winter”) cover low vision. Long-distance: a shared screen. Fatigue: agree a stopping year and continue another day. The map does not need to be exhaustive to be real.

## Consent and harm

Either person may leave a period unmarked and label it “private span.” Losses, pregnancies, migrations, and breakups involving other people are included only with care for who is in the room and who is not. The timeline is not 075’s set list of hits and not 060’s repair of one misstep. Challenges can sit on the line without being worked through tonight.

## Red flags

Do not claim the map predicts the relationship’s future. Do not require legal marriage, children, or a single household. Do not correct a partner’s felt turning point with a calendar as if the calendar were the experience. Some important changes have no event attached.
