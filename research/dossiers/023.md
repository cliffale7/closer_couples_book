# Dossier 023 — The Sixty-Second Embrace

- Catalog ID: 023
- Part: 3 — The Science and Experience of Closeness
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: mixed
- Confidence: high

## Source premise

Share an unhurried, mutually comfortable embrace and notice how deliberate affection differs from routine.

## Must preserve

One embrace, long enough to be unhurried, comfortable for both, and noticed against whatever their routine hug is. Sixty seconds is the book's proposed duration, not a researched dose. If they hug for a minute while chatting about errands, they miss the noticing. If they endure a squeeze they want to leave, they miss "comfortable."

## Evidence

J2017 reviews and models wanted affectionate touch and its possible links to relational, psychological, and physical well-being. It is not a test of a timed embrace, and it does not establish a 20-second hug, a hormone change, or an oxytocin effect. Do not cite any such claim. Unwanted touch is outside the review's claim. The difference between a deliberate embrace and a routine one is this chapter's question, and it has not been measured here.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

The doorway hug, the greeting hug, and workshop "long hugs" are all common. Classification stays mixed: affectionate touch has a serious review behind it, and this sixty-second comparison is still an untested design.

## Design notes

Materials: a timer with a soft alarm, a place they can stand or sit without an audience.

Optional baseline: if they have a habitual hug, do that one first, about as long as they usually would, and step apart. Then the deliberate embrace: they agree the form (full, side, or seated lean), who starts, and the release phrase ("I'm going to step back"). Sixty seconds is the standard. Either person may end at ten. Ending early still counts. Short: about fifteen seconds of the same rules, for pain or overload, with the chapter still asking what differed from routine. Extended: a second minute only if both ask, after a breath apart.

During the minute, no errands talk. After, each names one difference from the routine hug: weight, breath, time, warmth, or awkwardness. Awkwardness is a valid finding.

What makes the instructions fail: a stamina contest; rocking or squeezing harder as the timer runs; pinning someone's arms; saying the minute "didn't work" if it felt ordinary; importing a hormone fact.

Accessibility. Height difference, pregnancy, pain, sensory overload, and limited arm mobility: side-by-side or seated contact can still be an embrace if both call it one. Chest-to-chest is not required. A partner who cannot hug can do a sustained hand-hold and label it a substitute, not as the embrace J2017 was wrongly imagined to have tested. Long-distance: they cannot share one embrace over video. Do not replace it with a self-hug and cite the review. They can postpone until they are in the same place. Vision and hearing: the alarm should be perceptible to both; a silent visual timer is fine. Heat and illness: skip.

Journal cue: the form they used, whether they finished the minute, and one noticed difference. No body score.

## Consent and harm

Mutually comfortable is checked before the timer and may be revoked inside it. The release phrase is required so nobody has to yank away. This chapter stays nonsexual. Entry 022 maps many kinds of touch; this one compares a routine hug with one deliberate embrace.

Do not use the embrace to end an unresolved argument by force of closeness.

## Red flags

No 20-second rule, no oxytocin, no cortisol promise. J2017 did not trial this embrace. Sixty seconds is a design choice and must be described that way.
