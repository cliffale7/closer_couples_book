# Dossier 032 — The Everyday Devotion Experiment

- Catalog ID: 032
- Part: 4 — Love, Affection, and Feeling Chosen
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: mixed
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

Spend several days performing small, personally meaningful acts of affection and notice their effects.

## Must preserve

The span is several days, the acts are small and personally meaningful to the receiver, and both people notice effects, including weak or awkward ones. A single grand gesture, a week of generic romance, or a scoreboard of who did more all drop the premise. Noticing is part of the mechanism, not a follow-up survey the writer may skip.

## Evidence

G2012 tied feeling appreciated and feeling appreciative to responsiveness, commitment, and maintenance, and it notes that appreciation can show up in behavior. It did not test a multi-day devotion protocol, and it did not measure a dose of kind acts. Do not claim a happiness lift, a hormone change, or a permanent maintenance effect for this experiment.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

"Do small things often" is household wisdom and couples-education advice. Classification stays mixed. The neighboring research is about appreciation as a relationship process. The several-day experiment, the null-result log, and the ban on secret tests are design.

## Design notes

Materials: a shared window of three days (five if they want the extended version), a one-line daily note, and a prior check on what "personally meaningful" means.

Day 0, ten minutes. If they already know concrete preferences from life or from a chapter like 025, each person names two small acts that would actually land this week, and one act to avoid. If they do not know, the first move is the question, not a guessed gift. Set a money ceiling, including zero. Agree that acts are ordinary: a specific message, a chore the other has said matters, a drink made the way they like, a touch they have already welcomed. Surprise is allowed inside that map. Surprise outside it is how the experiment fails.

Each day, each person does one act if they can. The evening note has four lines: what I offered, what I noticed from the other, what I felt, and "nothing in particular" as an allowed answer. No points. On the last night, compare notes for twenty minutes. The question is what they noticed, not who won.

What makes the instructions fail: expensive gestures; public performances; acts that violate a known limit; keeping a deficit tally; "I did it secretly and you failed to notice." Effects include nothing. The log has to make a null result easy to write or the chapter pressures people into fake gratitude.

Accessibility. Fatigue, pain, and caregiving: an act can be a sentence, and a missed day is recorded as a miss, not a moral event. Long-distance: choose acts that travel (a letter, a timed call, a photo of an ordinary thing) and do not require one partner to "make up for" distance with purchases. Vision and hearing: acts in the channel they actually use. Sensory overload: no scented surprises, no sudden touch. Mobility: do not set errands the person's body cannot do.

Journal cue: the three-day window, two acts worth remembering, and one effect, including a null. No rankings.

## Consent and harm

Personally meaningful includes the right to say an act is unwanted. Affection that is sexual or public needs a prior yes. Do not run the experiment as a repair for harm or as a test of whether the partner deserves love. Either person may stop the window early.

## Red flags

Do not cite G2012 as a trial of kindness challenges. Do not promise that several days of effort will be felt as devotion. Do not turn the notes into a ledger they use in a later argument.
