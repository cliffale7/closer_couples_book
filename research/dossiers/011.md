# Dossier 011 — What I Don’t Show the World

- Catalog ID: 011
- Part: 2 — Vulnerability and the Courage to Be Seen
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

Describe parts of yourself you hide from others and what would make them safe to reveal.

## Must preserve

Both halves are the activity: a hidden part, and the conditions that would make showing it safer. A reveal with no safety conditions becomes a dare. A list of conditions with nothing hidden becomes a policy memo. "The world" may include the partner, but the speaker gets to say whether this part is hidden from everyone or only outside the relationship.

## Evidence

L1998: intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. The diaries did not map hiding, and they did not test a safety-conditions conversation. Do not replace perceived partner responsiveness with "understanding." Perceived safety in this chapter is the speaker's report, not a measured state. Corrected after `review/evidence/batch-001-036.md`.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Talk of masks, private selves, and "I'm fine in public" is everyday language. R1988 is only the framework of disclosure, response, and the feeling of being understood. It does not say that people disclose when a response seems likely to be decent. Classification stays reimagined. The paired prompt, hidden part plus safety conditions, is a design.

## Design notes

Materials: two columns on a page, Hide and What would help. Timer.

Private writing, about 8 minutes. Two items in the first column, each a specific part (a tenderness, an ambition, a shame, a taste), not a global "the real me." Beside each, two conditions that another person could actually offer: who is in the room, what they do with the information, what they do not joke about, how soon they give advice. At least one condition should be behavioral and observable.

Standard sharing: each person reads one pair. The partner's only reply is a restatement of the condition, plus whether they can offer it, cannot, or are unsure. They do not promise "you are safe with me" as a blanket. Swap, equal time. Short: one pair each. Extended: the second written item, still optional to read.

What makes the instructions fail: treasure-hunting for the biggest secret; the partner negotiating the conditions down; confusing "safe to reveal" with "I will fix the feeling"; a performance of mystery. If the hidden part is already known to the partner, the useful half is where it is still hidden from others, and what the partner does that helps or complicates that.

Accessibility. Writing, typing, or dictation into a private note before anything is shared. Vision and hearing: trade paper or talk, whichever is easier. Mobility and pain: seated, extra draft time. Long-distance: draft offline, then read. Sensory overload: one item. No group, no audience, no recording unless both ask for it.

Journal cue: the condition they want remembered. The hidden content is optional on the page.

## Consent and harm

Hiding can be protective. The chapter does not treat privacy as a flaw to be cured. A part that involves harm, illegal risk, or another person's safety is not a reveal-for-intimacy item. Either person may leave an item in the unread column.

## Red flags

Do not claim that naming a safety condition creates safety. Do not instruct them to reveal the thing they are most afraid to say.
