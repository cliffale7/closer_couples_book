## Source premise

> Create exhibit labels for thoughts, appreciation, fears, or needs left unsaid; pair 'what happened' with 'what I wish you understood.'

## Must preserve

Exhibit labels for what went unsaid, each with two lines. The signature twist, from the project’s expanded brief: “Each exhibit has two labels—‘What happened’ and ‘What I wish you’d understood.’” One label, or a tour of a whole life, is a different museum. Formulated for this project. Not a claim of worldwide novelty.

## Evidence

L1998 linked intimacy in daily interactions to disclosure plus perceived responsiveness. An exhibit that finally says the emotional point is adjacent to that finding. L1998 did not test museum labels, and a label is not guaranteed to land as responsiveness. The visitor still has to receive it.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Museums use tombstone labels: title, date, a short interpretation. That graphic convention is the metaphor. Chapter 006, The Museum of Me, is a tour of objects, people, songs, and events that represent a life. This museum exhibits only what was left unsaid, and the second label is the wished-for understanding. Do not merge them.

## Design notes

About 45–90 minutes. Each person builds two to four small exhibits. Suggested titles from the project brief, which the writer may voice freshly: “The Compliment I Never Gave,” “The Fear I Disguised as a Joke,” and “The Moment I Needed a Hug but Didn’t Ask.” Extra doors if needed: “The Thanks I Swallowed,” “The Worry I Turned into a Chore.” Template: title; date or rough time; “What happened,” in concrete sentences; “What I wish you’d understood,” in one or two sentences. Read as a curator, not as a prosecutor. The visitor asks one question and does not defend themselves until the curator invites it. After all exhibits, each visitor names one label they can carry without promising a personality change.

Sensitive alternatives when a direct exhibit would ambush: write it in the hypothetical (“there have been evenings when…”); postpone it to a planned conversation; or show the title only and say the gallery is closed. An ambush is a label that reveals a relationship-ending fact, a sexual grievance, or a humiliation the visitor has no context to hold.

Failure points: a museum that is entirely the partner’s failures; witty labels that hide the second line; forcing every closed gallery open.

Accessibility: large-print cards, audio labels, or a shared doc. Long-distance: exchange PDFs and tour by call. Seated. No craft skill required. A short path is one exhibit each.

Journal: keep cards the visitor has agreed to hold. Return or destroy the rest.

## Consent and harm

The curator warns before an exhibit about sex, family, or shame. The visitor may skip a room. Nothing in the gallery immunizes cruelty. Needs can be named without becoming orders. Distinguish 006’s life tour and 013’s single chosen confession. This chapter can hold several small unsaid things, each with the two labels, and it can also hold less.

## Red flags

Do not reproduce a real museum’s label text. Do not claim the format is art therapy with outcome data. Do not require symmetrical vulnerability. A closed gallery is part of a real museum.
