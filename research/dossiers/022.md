# Dossier 022 — The Language of Touch

- Catalog ID: 022
- Part: 3 — The Science and Experience of Closeness
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: mixed
- Confidence: high

## Source premise

Explore welcome nonsexual forms of touch and discover which create comfort and affection.

## Must preserve

The touch is welcome, it stays nonsexual for this chapter, and the outcome is discovery: which forms create comfort and affection for these two people. A preset sequence of strokes, or a drift into sex, replaces discovery with a script.

## Evidence

J2017 is a theoretical model and review of wanted affectionate touch in adulthood, linking that literature to relational, psychological, and physical well-being, including stress-related pathways. It is not a trial of this chapter's menu. Touch that is unwanted sits outside the claim. Do not cite a 20-second hug, a hormone spike, or any timed dose. J2017 does not establish one.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Couples massage, "love language" touch talk, and sensate-focus exercises in sex therapy all use touch with very different rules. Classification stays mixed. A review of wanted affectionate touch is real background. The nonsexual menu, the overlap rule, and the comfort check are design. They are not a clinical protocol, and the chapter should not borrow sensate-focus steps from a manual.

## Design notes

Materials: a private card each, ordinary clothes, a place to sit. No oils required.

The writer invents a short original menu, for example: hand held, hand on shoulder, hand on upper back, arms linked, knees touching while seated, hair touched, face touched. Leave a blank line for their own addition.

Each person privately marks every item: welcome tonight, not tonight, or leave out. Compare, and keep only the overlap of "welcome tonight." Try one item at a time. The receiver sets pressure and duration. A few seconds is enough; they may stop sooner. There is no researched duration. After each item the receiver says one word: comfort, affection, neutral, or stop. Stop retires that item for the session. Then swap roles. Short: one item. Standard: up to four overlaps. Extended: revisit the item that ranked highest and talk about when it would be welcome in ordinary weeks.

What makes the instructions fail: trying items outside the overlap; the giver lobbying; treating "not tonight" as a project; adding genital or explicitly sexual touch under a nonsexual label; copying a therapy hierarchy the couple did not ask for.

Accessibility. Pain, injury, sensory aversion, and trauma: the leave-out column is part of the activity, not a preamble. Offer a no-touch discovery path in which they describe, without demonstrating, which forms would be welcome on another day. Gloves, fabric barriers, and very light pressure are valid forms if both mark them welcome. Long-distance: compare the cards and wait to try items in person; a video substitute is optional description, not touch. Vision: spoken check-ins. Hearing: thumbs up, down, or sideways agreed in advance. Mobility: the seated subset of the menu. Sensory overload: one item, quiet room, no scent.

Journal cue: the items that were welcome, the one word that followed each, and anything retired. Do not record a partner's body as a score.

## Consent and harm

Welcome is specific to the item and the night. It expires when the session ends. Nonsexual is a boundary of this chapter even for couples who are sexual together; sex can be chosen another time, outside this data. Either person may stop mid-contact. Do not ask why for a "leave out."

Entry 023 is one sustained embrace. Entry 036 includes touch as one station among senses. This chapter is a vocabulary of forms.

## Red flags

No hormone claim, no 20-second prescription, no "oxytocin hug." Do not present J2017 as a test of the menu. Do not assume who touches whom first based on gender.
