# Dossier 021 — The Trust Walk

- Catalog ID: 021
- Part: 3 — The Science and Experience of Closeness
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: high

## Source premise

Guide a partner with closed or covered eyes through a safe space; practice clear direction and trust.

## Must preserve

Someone guides, someone cannot see the route, the space is safe, and the practice is clear direction plus trust. Risk, pranks, and obstacle courses contradict "safe space." Trust here is reliability of instruction, not a willingness to be endangered.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Blindfold guides are a camp and team-course staple. Classification stays reimagined. The couple version in this book keeps the folk shape and adds a safety sequence, a no-prank rule, and a debrief about instruction rather than about loyalty. It is not a validated measure of relationship trust.

## Design notes

Materials: a clear indoor route on one level, a sleep mask the walker controls (or simply closed eyes), a stop word, a slow word, and a freeze word. Optional light touch at the elbow. Voice-only guiding must be available, because the premise does not require touch.

Sequence. (1) Both walk the route with eyes open and remove trip hazards. No stairs on the first pass. (2) Agree the words and who is guiding. (3) The walker covers their eyes only when they say they are ready, and they may uncover at any time. (4) Directions are concrete: "two steps, then the rug, then a chair on your left." "Over there" is a failed instruction. (5) The guide warns before any touch and before letting go. (6) Switch roles only if the second person wants a turn. (7) Debrief: which instruction was early enough, which was late, what the walker needed and did not ask for.

Short: a single room, one minute. Standard: a short loop, under five minutes of walking, plus debrief. Extended: a second loop with voice only, or with touch only, comparing clarity.

What makes the instructions fail: spinning, fake stairs, surprises, public streets, kitchens with knives and heat, pools, darkness that the guide also cannot see, holding the walker's hand down if they reach for the mask. Funny is allowed only before the mask goes on, in the planning, never as a sudden stimulus.

Accessibility. Vestibular disorders, pain, mobility limits: use a seated version in which the guide directs the walker's hands to three objects on a table, eyes closed, with the same stop word. A partner who is blind is not a prop for this game. Skip the blindfold and practice describing a route they both need, or do the table version with the sighted partner as walker. Vision of the guide has to be good enough to keep the walker safe; if not, do not run the walking version. Hearing: agree tactile signals in advance for stop, slow, and turn. Long-distance: a phone guide through the walker's own room changes who holds the risk. Label it as a remote adaptation. The walker still needs a clear floor. Sensory overload and claustrophobia: eyes-closed for ten seconds counts as a full first session.

Journal cue: one instruction that worked, one that arrived too late, and whether they want a second role-switch another day.

## Consent and harm

Covering someone's eyes without warning is out. Trust is optional and revocable mid-route. Do not interpret a refusal as a relationship defect. People with histories of being restrained or startled need the table version or a pass, offered before any mask appears. No alcohol.

## Red flags

Do not claim the walk builds trust in a measured way. Do not cite a ropes-course study. None was opened. Do not send them outdoors near traffic.
