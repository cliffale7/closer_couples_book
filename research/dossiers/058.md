# Dossier 058 — What You Heard vs. What I Meant

- Catalog ID: 058
- Part: 8 — Trust, Communication, and Emotional Safety
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: mixed
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

> Compare intended meaning with perceived meaning and practice checking interpretations.

## Must preserve

A side-by-side comparison of what the speaker meant and what the listener heard, including the story the listener added, followed by a check: match, partial, or missed. Then one try at a response that would have felt understanding. Intent does not erase impact. The exercise compares the two accounts; it does not crown the speaker’s intention as the real event.

## Evidence

L1998 found that, in diary studies of daily interactions, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. That supports taking seriously whether someone feels understood. It does not test this clarification exercise, does not show that the exercise reduces conflict, and did not use couples’ repaired misunderstandings as the method. R1988 is the interpersonal-process model — disclosure, response, and the feeling of being understood — that L1998 tested. It is a framework, not a result for this chapter.

GOTTMAN’s clinical writing popularized repair attempts and bids for connection. Checking an interpretation sits near that practitioner language. No Gottman study is cited here, and no divorce statistic exists to import. The seed classification mixed is kept: a designed comparison that neighbors both a diary finding and a practitioner frame, without being either one.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

“That isn’t what I meant” is an everyday repair. Communication courses often teach a playback of what was heard. This chapter is a short, bounded version for a mild moment. It is not a validated couples treatment and not a license to relitigate who is right about the facts.

## Design notes

Choose a recent low-stakes moment, not the worst fight. Two columns: “what I meant,” in two sentences, and “what I heard,” including the added story. Mark match, partial, or missed. The speaker names a response that would have felt understanding. The listener offers that response once, as practice.

Failure points: picking a loaded injury; arguing the facts until the meanings vanish; treating mind-reading as a test the listener should have passed.

Accessibility: written columns if people talk over each other. Long-distance can use a shared note. Text serves hearing access and also gives processing time between columns. Partners who use other communication methods can complete the whole exercise on the page. No eye contact is required.

## Consent and harm

Either person may decline a moment that is still too hot. Practice is not a verdict on a real injury. The listener’s added story is not a confession of bad faith unless they say so.

Distinguish 060, which revisits a minor misstep for acknowledgment, accountability, and repair. A meaning gap is not always a misstep. Distinguish 087, which follows an answer with further questions. Entry 058 is specifically intent beside perception. Do not merge them.

## Red flags

Do not let “I didn’t mean it that way” cancel the effect. Do not claim this is clinical communication training or that it produces the intimacy L1998 measured. Do not invent a Gottman success rate.
