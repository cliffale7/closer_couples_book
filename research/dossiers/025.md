# Dossier 025 — What Loving Me Actually Looks Like

- Catalog ID: 025
- Part: 4 — Love, Affection, and Feeling Chosen
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: mixed
- Confidence: high

## Source premise

Compare the exact behaviors, words, and gestures that make each person feel loved.

## Must preserve

The comparison is of exact behaviors, words, and gestures. Each person supplies their own, and they look at the two lists together. Category labels, a quiz score, or a single "primary language" replace the premise with a sorting system.

## Evidence

I2024 reviews love-languages claims and finds little empirical support for the ideas that each person has one primary love language, that there are exactly five, and that matching languages is what predicts satisfaction. People do seem to benefit from a range of affectionate behavior. That review is why this chapter stays with specific acts. It is not evidence that comparing lists changes a relationship.

CHAPMAN names Gary Chapman's 1992 popular book, the source of the five-language frame. It is not established science. If the chapter mentions it, pair it with I2024 and keep the mention short.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

The five love languages are a popular typology. Classification stays mixed because that popular frame will crowd the chapter unless the writer actively prefers concrete examples. The activity itself is a comparison of behaviors. It is not a quiz and not a replication of I2024.

## Design notes

Materials: two sheets, three rows labeled Words, Gestures, Behaviors. No preprinted language categories.

Private writing, about 10 minutes. Each row gets two items an observer could see or hear. Good grain: "text me when you arrive," "sit on the same side of the table," "say what you liked about the specific thing I cooked." Poor grain: "quality time," "acts of service," "touch." If an item costs money or time, they note a scale that is actually available.

Compare. Mark each item: already happens, I did not know, or hard for me. Where both lists share a behavior, circle it. Where they differ, read the difference as information about these two people. Standard session about 35 minutes. Short: one item per row. Extended: each person picks one "I did not know" item to try once in the next week, with an explicit chance to decline.

What makes the instructions fail: handing them the five labels and asking for a primary; declaring incompatibility because the lists differ; translating every want into a gift or a chore because those are easy to count; a list of personality repairs ("be less anxious") instead of a behavior.

Accessibility. Speech or writing. Vision and hearing: use the channel they use for serious talks. Mobility and pain: items must be things both bodies can actually do; replace a walk with a seated equivalent if needed. Long-distance: mark which words and gestures survive a message and which require presence. Sensory overload: skip any item that depends on surprise, crowds, or heavy scent. No touch demonstration in this chapter; 022 is the touch vocabulary.

Journal cue: three behaviors they want remembered, in their own words, plus one they learned about the partner. No type, no score.

## Consent and harm

A listed behavior is a description of what has helped, not an order. The partner may say an item is something they will not do. Feeling loved is allowed to differ across weeks; the lists can be revised. Do not use a mismatch as evidence that someone is unloving. If one person's items are mostly "stop doing harm," write that plainly and do not translate it into a cute preference.

## Red flags

Do not treat Chapman's five love languages as science. I2024 reviews that evidence as weak. Do not sell matching types as the path to satisfaction. Do not invent a quiz.
