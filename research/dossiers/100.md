## Source premise

> Describe your inner state as a weather forecast, with an optional forecast of what may help tomorrow.

## Must preserve

A short forecast of inner weather in place of a bare “how was your day,” plus the listener’s freedom not to solve it. The signature twist, from the project’s expanded brief: “The weatherperson optionally forecasts what could help tomorrow, without making the partner responsible for changing the weather.” Formulated for this project. Not a claim of worldwide novelty, and not a mood treatment.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Mood metaphors and “emotional check-ins” are common in classrooms and therapy. They are not a validated protocol for couples. No affect-labeling study was opened, so the chapter must not claim that naming a feeling changes the brain or the weather.

## Design notes

Five to fifteen minutes, repeatable. The weatherperson picks a region: work, body, the relationship, or the private interior. They give a condition, a direction of change, and, if they want, tomorrow’s help. Playful example: “Late fog, a small break of sun after lunch, chance of existential drizzle tonight. Tomorrow would go better if I stepped outside once. I can do that myself.” Serious example: “Steady rain since the appointment. I don’t want ideas. Company in the kitchen would help, and I will ask if I want it.” The listener may ask where it is heaviest or whether it is passing. They do not hand over an umbrella in the form of advice unless asked.

Log sheet columns: date, region, forecast, help I will arrange for myself, anything I am explicitly asking (often blank).

Plain-language path for people who dislike metaphor: “Right now I am ___. It may last ___. A help I can arrange is ___. A help I am actually requesting is ___ / none.” Both paths are the same ritual.

Failure points: a forecast that is a coded complaint about the partner; the listener rushing to fix; tomorrow’s help assigned to the partner by default; performing cheer.

Accessibility: text, speech, or a picture of a sky. Long-distance: a two-line message. Sensory sensitivity: skip thunderclap theater. The ritual fits fatigue because it is short. No one must forecast every day.

## Consent and harm

Either person may answer “no forecast tonight” and talk ordinarily. A forecast of despair, panic, or danger is a real signal, not a poem; respond with care and outside help when safety is in question. The partner is not the climate. Distinguish 020, where one person speaks at length and the other reflects. Here the report stays brief, and solving is off the job description.

## Red flags

Do not diagnose from the metaphor. Do not require wit. Do not track the log as a compliance chart. Irreverent weather is allowed; so is a plain sentence. The optional tomorrow line stays in the weatherperson’s hands.
