## Source premise

> Invent versions of yourselves in another world and imagine your meeting, attraction, and misadventures.

## Must preserve

Invented versions, not a coded retelling of a real injury. The story needs three beats: how these versions meet, what attracts them, and a misadventure they get through together. The misadventure should be funny or tender. The last question returns to the real pair: one trait the alternate shares with the person in the room. Without that return, the chapter is only collaborative fiction. Without the three beats, it is only a costume.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Collaborative make-believe — spies, bakers, castaways — is a folk form of play. It is not narrative therapy and not a compatibility test. The writer can nod at the pleasure of plotting without citing a clinical school.

## Design notes

Constrain the world to one sentence: an undersea library, a failing bakery during a festival, a small research outpost. Keep the couple’s real first names and add a role. Spend about ten minutes on the three beats, trading sentences. End by naming one shared trait worth remembering in ordinary life.

The writer should include one fictional mini-story so the tone is obvious. Give the misadventure a repair inside the fiction: a lost ticket, a collapsed cake, a map read upside down, solved by a trait the real partner actually has. That keeps the caper affectionate. If the story stalls, a prompted handoff works: “you say how we meet; I say what I notice; you break something small; I get us out.”

Failure points: a misadventure that accuses the real partner; a world whose joke depends on oppression; a plot so long that attraction and mishap never both occur; turning the alternate into a “better” spouse.

Accessibility: oral telling or alternating written paragraphs. Long-distance is a strength. No props. The story can be told in low light or with cameras off. Fatigue: hit the three beats in five minutes. Language can be plain; no mythic diction is required.

## Consent and harm

Romance in the story is fictional and is not a request to reenact it. Either person may reject a world. Do not cast a real third person as a villain.

Distinguish 039, which keeps the real selves and asks what would still attract them in another meeting. Entry 046 changes the versions and wants a plot, including misadventure. Distinguish 104, which invents a civilization’s principles under pressure. Entry 046 is a couple’s caper, not a constitution. Do not merge these.

## Red flags

Do not present the story as evidence of compatibility, destiny, or unresolved conflict. Do not require action, combat, or sexual content in the misadventure. Label examples fictional.
