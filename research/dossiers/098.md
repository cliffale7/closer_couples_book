## Source premise

> Interview each other as imagined centenarians and turn three invented memories into real-life aspirations.

## Must preserve

Both people play centenarian versions of themselves and interview one another about decades they have not lived, contradictions included. The signature twist, from the project’s expanded brief: “Each person identifies three invented memories they'd genuinely like to make real someday.” The interview without that naming, or a single future scene with no centenarian frame, is a different chapter. Formulated for this project; not a claim of worldwide novelty or of research validation.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Future-self letters and “interview at ninety” prompts circulate in coaching. They are not this chapter and were not opened as sources. Schacter, Addis, and Buckner (2007) review evidence that imagining the future uses much of the same neural machinery as remembering and that simulation recombines past details. That review does not test a couple’s centenarian game, does not make imagined events more likely, and does not say people confuse fiction with memory. The theatrical frame should stay obviously fictional.

## Design notes

About 30–60 minutes. Theatrical setup: sit as if on a porch, use a scarf or nothing, and speak a little more slowly. State the year as one hundred years from the older partner’s birth, or simply “we are both about one hundred,” if ages differ or the number is unwelcome. Interviewer questions, original here: what adventure still makes you laugh; what mistake taught you a skill; what ordinary Tuesday you would repeat; what did you stop pretending; what did we get wrong about each other and later understand. Contradictory versions of the same decade stay in play. Neither corrects the other’s fiction during the interview.

Then each person names three invented memories they would genuinely like to make real. For each, one line on the optional future-memory page: the scene, a step that is available without heroics, and a piece that must remain fantasy if the other person does not want it. The page is practical and optional. It is not 095’s promise sheet.

Failure points: using the future to indict the present; a centenarian voice that mocks old age; one partner narrating both lives.

Accessibility: skip the age frame and use “a long life from now” if disability, illness, or grief makes one hundred feel cruel. Seated. Writing optional. Long-distance: one reads questions, both answer. No makeup or props required.

## Consent and harm

Invented memories that spend a partner’s body, fertility, faith, or home need a check before they land on the “make real” page. Either person may leave the interview without naming three. Illness and death jokes at a partner’s expense are out. Distinguish 090: one happy future memory in the past tense, then a how-to. Here the span is decades, contradiction is welcome, and the twist is three chosen inventions. Do not merge them.

## Red flags

Do not claim the game extends life or repairs regret. Do not quote clinical cases from the 2007 review. Do not require the couple to share one canonical future. Two incompatible centuries can both be affectionate.
