## Source premise

Write a revealing biography of your contradictions, motivations, vulnerabilities, and less visible qualities.

## Must preserve

It is written, it is a biography of the writer, and it has to contain contradictions, motivations, vulnerabilities, and less visible qualities. A spoken self-summary, a highlight reel, or a biography of the partner all miss the premise. "Secret" means less visible, not a forced disclosure of the most dangerous fact they know.

## Evidence

L1998: in diary studies of daily interactions, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. Writing a biography was not tested. A preference for motive and feeling over a list of preferences is this book's writing advice, not an L1998 result. Do not call one page "thinner." The partner's perceived responsiveness still matters after the page is read; that is the whitelist phrase, not a biography outcome. Corrected after `review/evidence/batch-001-036.md`.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Third-person self-portraits are a writing-class exercise. Therapeutic journals sometimes ask for the same material in the first person. Classification stays reimagined. The four required ingredients and the choice to write before speaking are this chapter's design, not a published protocol.

## Design notes

Materials: paper or a private note, a pen, a timer. About one page, roughly 250 to 400 words, so it will actually be read. Third person is a useful default ("they are both...") because it makes contradiction easier to write. First person is allowed if third person turns into a joke they cannot exit.

Required sentences, which the writer of the chapter can phrase more elegantly: two traits that pull against each other; a motive that does not match the public story; one vulnerability stated without a rescue clause; one quality friends underuse. Twenty minutes to draft, in silence. Then each author either reads aloud or hands the page over. The partner asks two questions of clarification and does not edit the portrait. Swap. Short: four sentences, one per ingredient, read in the room. Extended: a second paragraph on where the contradiction shows up in the relationship, only with the author's consent.

What makes the instructions fail: writing the partner's character; a comedy sketch that never risks a real line; a clinical self-diagnosis; a page so long it is summarized instead of heard; the listener arguing with the self-portrait.

Accessibility. Dictation counts as writing if the author can review it before it is shared. Vision: dictate, or type at a comfortable size. Hearing: exchange pages silently. Mobility and pain: no posture demands; extra time if writing hurts. Long-distance: exchange after both drafts are finished, so nobody writes to an audience that is already reacting. Sensory overload: the four-sentence version.

Journal cue: store only the sentences the author chooses to archive. The full page can stay in a private file. The shared note can be the contradiction they are willing to have remembered.

## Consent and harm

The author decides which sentences are read. Writing a line does not obligate sharing it. Vulnerabilities that involve ongoing harm, or another person's safety, are outside a "secret biography" game; see the boundary language in 013 if the writer is tempted to promise secrecy. The partner does not psychoanalyze the page.

## Red flags

Do not paste a published questionnaire as the biography outline. Do not claim L1998 validated written self-portrait exercises.
