## Source premise

> Answer a scenario as you think your partner would and compare the reasoning, not just the final decision.

## Must preserve

You answer as you believe your partner would, then they explain their actual choice and their reasons. The signature twist, from the project’s expanded brief: “Reward understanding the partner's logic more than matching a yes/no answer.” A matched yes with a wrong reason scores below a missed yes with a grasped reason. Differences are not failures. Formulated for this project, without a worldwide-novelty claim.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity. No perspective-taking paper was opened. The chapter must not cite a fictional empathy study.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

The Newlywed Game and 043 ask you to guess a partner’s answer to trivia about them. That tests stored knowledge of facts and preferences. This experiment tests whether you can inhabit the reasoning. Party games that buzz wrong answers teach the wrong lesson. None of those games is a validated protocol here.

## Design notes

About 30–45 minutes. Play three scenarios, one realistic, one funny, one morally gray, drawn from a larger bank so the night can be replayed. Original bank:

Realistic: overtime on the night a friend is in crisis; a parent criticizes their housekeeping in front of them, and the question is what they do in the next ten minutes; a neighbor asks to borrow the car for a week; they find a wallet with cash and an ID.
Funny: a solemn host serves a dessert they hate; they are offered a free tattoo of your face; their most disliked song comes on and everyone else starts dancing.
Gray: a sibling asks them to keep a non-dangerous secret from you; a refund is available only if they give a reason that is not quite true; a friend wants them to skip your event to help someone else.

Turn structure: read the scenario. Silently write the partner’s choice and three beats of their reasoning (value, fear or hope, practical constraint). Reveal. The partner gives their actual choice and reasoning. Scoring, optional and light: two points for naming a real value of theirs, one point for a constraint you understood, zero for the yes or no itself. A wrong value cannot be rescued by a lucky yes. Subtract nothing for a warm miss; ask “what value did I miss?” and write it down. Cap the night at three scenarios so the debrief stays kind.

Failure points: scoring the person rather than the guess; scenarios that are traps about sex or fidelity; using a miss as a grievance the next day.

Accessibility: written answers, extra time, scenarios read aloud. Long-distance works. Replace any scenario that assumes a job, a sibling, money, or a household the couple does not have. The funny scenario must not humiliate a body or a culture.

## Consent and harm

Either person may reject a scenario before play. The partner is the authority on their own mind. A guessed betrayal or a guessed sexual refusal is a poor joke and should not be dealt. This is not 043’s trivia match, and it is not 085’s “what would you yourself do.” Keep the borrowed brain on reasoning. Do not merge the chapters.

## Red flags

Do not claim the score measures love or empathic accuracy. Do not require a winner. Optional scoring exists to glorify reasons. If the points start to sting, drop them and keep the conversation.
