## Source premise

> Use imaginary locked doors for inner-life themes; the person sharing controls which keys are offered and how deeply to explore.

## Must preserve

Imaginary doors, and the sharer’s control of the keys. The partner may ask. The sharer decides which key, if any, is offered, and how deep the tour goes. The signature twist, from the project’s expanded brief: “A door may be offered that the other person didn't know existed, but no one can demand access.” A demanded door ends the round. Formulated for this project, without a worldwide-novelty claim.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity. L1998’s link between intimacy and perceived responsiveness supports a listener who can tolerate a closed door. It does not test key rituals, and disclosure is not owed.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

“Rooms of the self” appear in guided imagery and some therapies. No such script was opened, and none is the protocol. The original rule is revocable depth: the sharer can offer a key, refuse a key, or end a sentence and keep the rest. Chapter 013 offers one chosen embarrassment, regret, insecurity, or harmless private truth for curious listening, and it is explicit that the frame is not immunity for harmful behavior. 108 is a tour of several possible rooms with pass controls. Do not merge them.

## Design notes

About 30–75 minutes. Optional door cards, blank on the back. Each person prepares up to four doors. Themes may include a cherished memory, a fear, an unrealized ambition, a private desire, a paradox, or something hard to explain. The front shows a title only. Titles can be new to the partner. That is the twist, and it is an offer, not a tease that must be satisfied.

Keys the sharer may offer or refuse: Brass, the facts of what happened. Glass, how it felt. Iron, what it costs to say more. Ribbon, what I hope you do with this, which can be “nothing.” Blank, the door exists and the tour stops. The partner may request a key by name. Silence and “not that key” are complete answers. A key already offered can be put down mid-sentence. The partner does not finish the sentence.

Debrief, short: which door surprised you by existing; which closed door you can respect without a story; anything that needs a conversation outside the game. The debrief is not a second attempt on a refused key.

A short path is two doors each and one key. Journal titles only, unless the sharer wants a sentence kept. The partner does not keep notes on a closed door.

Failure points: wheedling after a refusal; treating a blank key as a puzzle to solve later; the listener interpreting a room in clinical language; stacking heavy doors with no lighter one available.

Accessibility: cards can be spoken titles. Long-distance: each person holds their own cards on camera, backs unseen. No dark room and no locked physical space. The metaphor is verbal. People who dislike symbolic play can use the same rules with the word “topic” instead of “door” and “level” instead of “key.” The control rule does not change. Seated. Stop on fatigue.

## Consent and harm

The sharer controls the keys for the whole round, including doors they themselves opened. No one earns access by being a good partner. Ongoing danger, abuse, or a crisis is not game content; those need a direct conversation and outside help when someone is unsafe. Desires and fears can be named at Brass and stopped there. A Ribbon key is a hope, not a contract. Sexual doors require prior willingness to have that kind of conversation at all. Distinguish 013’s single volunteered truth from this multi-door pass system.

## Red flags

Do not describe the exercise as therapy, shadow work, or a trauma method. Do not imply that love means every door eventually opens. A door offered in one sitting may be declined in the next. Privacy is a successful outcome.
