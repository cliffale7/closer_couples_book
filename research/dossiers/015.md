# Dossier 015 — When I Feel Small

- Catalog ID: 015
- Part: 2 — Vulnerability and the Courage to Be Seen
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

Describe what makes you feel inadequate or overlooked and which reassurances genuinely help.

## Must preserve

Two products from each person: the situations that make them feel small (inadequate or overlooked), and the reassurances that actually help, as distinct from the ones that bounce. A pep talk the partner would like to give, untested against the speaker's experience, misses the second half.

## Evidence

L1998: intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. Do not say responsiveness explained the intimacy that followed. The study did not catalog reassurance lines, and it does not show that praise repairs inadequacy. A reassurance "helps" in this chapter when the speaker says it helps, not when it sounds loving. Corrected after `review/evidence/batch-001-036.md`.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Reassurance is ordinary couple behavior and a common clinical suggestion. Classification stays reimagined. Pairing a specific smallness with a specific, speaker-tested response is the design. It is not a validated reassurance protocol.

## Design notes

Materials: two columns, Situations and What helps. A third faint column, What bounces, because people often know the failures better than the successes.

Private notes, about 8 minutes. Two situations, concrete enough to recognize ("when I talk for a while and no one asks a question back"). Beside each, one response that has helped, even once, and one that makes the feeling worse. Responses should be behaviors: sit down, ask one more question, skip the joke, say the particular sentence. "Love me more" is too vague to use.

Standard: each person shares one situation and both response columns. The partner repeats the helping response in their own words and says whether they can do it, sometimes, or not really. They do not demonstrate a speech on the spot unless asked. Swap. Short: one situation each. Extended: the second situation, plus a signal the speaker can use later when they feel small and do not want to explain.

What makes the instructions fail: global inadequacy ("I am not enough") with no situation; compliments that the speaker has already said bounce; the partner defending themselves instead of learning the overlooked moment; collecting reassurances as a script the partner must perform forever.

Accessibility. Writing optional. Hearing and vision: talk or trade notes. Mobility and pain: seated. Long-distance: note which helping responses work by message and which need a body in the room. Sensory overload: one situation. No mood lighting, no forced closeness.

Journal cue: the helping response in the speaker's words, and the bouncing response they want the partner to remember. Skip clinical labels.

## Consent and harm

Feeling small can sit next to shame, discrimination, or a partner who does overlook them. If the situation is "you mock me in front of friends," this chapter can name it, and the partner still does not get to cross-examine the feeling. They may need a separate conversation about the behavior. Reassurance is not a substitute for stopping a harmful pattern. Either person may pass. Entry 029 builds a wider menu across lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, and disconnected. This chapter stays with inadequacy and being overlooked.
