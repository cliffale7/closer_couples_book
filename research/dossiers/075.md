# Dossier 075 — Our Greatest Hits

- Catalog ID: 075
- Part: 10 — The Past, the Present, and the Story of Us
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

> Compile the funniest, most romantic, unexpected, and meaningful shared moments.

## Must preserve

Four different kinds of hit, compiled as shared repertoire: funniest, most romantic, unexpected, and meaningful. A single “best of” montage, or a private ranking of whose memories are better, drops the premise.

## Evidence

G2004 associated sharing a positive event, and a partner’s active-constructive response, with affect and relationship quality beyond the event alone. Passive or destructive responses were not the helpful pattern. Compiling greatest hits was not the study. The listener’s interested follow-up is a design idea drawn from that pattern, not evidence this chapter has been tested.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Couples and families already retell favorite stories at tables and reunions. That folk habit is not a validated protocol. Buehlman, Gottman, and Katz (1992), opened for the memory chapters, coded fondness during oral-history interviews in a selected sample of married parents and followed them for three years. Fond telling was a correlate in that study, not a treatment, and the chapter must not quote any divorce-prediction percentage from it. See dossier 073 for the sample limits. Gottman’s practitioner writing is not a journal article for this compilation.

## Design notes

Materials: four labeled cards or columns. Time: 40–60 minutes.

Each person nominates at least one story per column when they can. A column may stay empty; inventing a fake “most romantic” story to fill a blank breaks the record. Tell each story in a few minutes, with place, the turn, and why it still plays. The listener asks for one extra detail and names the part that landed. They do not top the story with a better one in the same breath.

Then mark three stories to keep telling, and one detail a future retelling should stop sanding down. Disagreement about what was funny is itself a useful beat; write both reactions.

Failure points: a highlight reel that erases hard years; in-jokes that humiliate one partner or a friend who is not present; turning “unexpected” into a grievance.

Accessibility: stories can be written for a partner who misses hearing, or told without playback of any related song. Skip sensory punchlines that depend on a sense either person lacks. Long-distance: shared doc plus a call. The activity stays seated.

Journal: a one-page set list with the four headings and a note of who remembers a detail differently.

## Consent and harm

Stories about sex, bodies, family, or someone else’s embarrassment need a check before they enter the shared set list. Either person can move a nomination to a private margin. This is not 074’s reveal of underestimated gestures, and it is not 077’s map of challenges and turning points. Keep the hard arc available elsewhere so this chapter can stay a repertoire of hits.

## Red flags

Do not claim that repeating fond stories predicts staying together. Do not reproduce song lyrics or film dialogue; name the piece and paraphrase. Do not score the relationship by how many hits it has. New couples have short lists. That is a real set list.
