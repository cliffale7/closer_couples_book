# Dossier 073 — The First Chapter

## Source premise

> Reconstruct your early relationship separately, then compare impressions, uncertainties, and memories.

## Must preserve

The separate draft. Each person reconstructs the beginning alone, including what was uncertain, before any joint telling. Comparison treats mismatches as information about memory and perspective. A single smooth meet-cute told together from the first minute loses the mechanism.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Couples already tell “how we met.” Buehlman, Gottman, and Katz (1992), opened for this dossier, coded an Oral History Interview in which married couples described dating, marriage, their philosophy of marriage, and change over time. The paper presents that interview as building on Satir’s family life chronology and Terkel’s interviewing style. Those books were not opened here. In the 1992 report, fondness and we-ness were among the coded qualities associated with later stability, while negativity, chaos, and disappointment ran the other way. The article’s summary describes 52 couples; the methods describe newspaper recruitment of families with a young child, a Locke-Wallace distribution skewed toward higher satisfaction, and a three-year follow-up. Researchers coded an existing telling. They did not assign a private-draft-then-compare game, and they did not show that reminiscing changes a future. The chapter must not quote the paper’s discriminant-function accuracy figure. Husband and wife codes were not interchangeable; that pattern is not gender advice. The interview is not a validated protocol for this chapter. Gottman’s “love map” is a practitioner metaphor only. This chapter is not a love-map quiz.

## Design notes

Materials: two sheets or two private pages, then one shared page. Time: 35–50 minutes.

Round 1, about ten minutes, no talking. Each person notes where the relationship begins for them (the dates may differ); what they were unsure of; one detail later retellings may have polished; what they thought the other felt; one fact they still do not know.

Round 2: tell or read without interruption. The listener marks “same memory,” “news to me,” and “I remember it differently.” No mid-story correction.

Round 3: compare starting points, surprises, and details worth keeping even if they cannot be proved. A joint paragraph is optional. Contradictions may stay side by side in the journal.

Failure points: cross-examination; treating a forgotten detail as lack of love; the faster writer’s draft becoming the official story.

Accessibility: voice memos recorded alone, then played; large print; written exchange if hearing makes live telling hard. Long-distance: private notes, then one call for rounds 2 and 3. No darkness, walking, or required eye contact. A beginning that includes grief, family pressure, or a former partner may be narrowed; either person may start the draft at a later scene.

## Consent and harm

Either person may leave out sexual firsts, family conflict, or any stretch they do not want replayed. No reason is required. This is a poor container for a live fidelity fight. Keep 076 as one later revision of perception, 077 as the long arc mapped together, and 080 as a single day. Those are neighbors, not substitutes.

## Red flags

Fond origin stories are not a divorce-prevention method. The more romantic draft is not automatically the true one. Do not require marriage, a heterosexual script, or one cinematic “moment we knew.” Confidence in a memory is not accuracy.
