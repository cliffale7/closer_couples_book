# Dossier 024 — The Presence Challenge

## Source premise

Remove phones, chores, and other distractions for a dedicated stretch of undivided attention.

## Must preserve

Three removals (phones, chores, and other distractions) plus a dedicated stretch whose point is undivided attention. A phone stacked face-down on the table, a half-finished chore tugging at the conversation, or a stretch with no shape all fail. The chapter is about attention, not about winning a ban.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Device-free meals and sabbath-like breaks are common household practices. Classification stays reimagined. The combination of phones, chores, and a pre-chosen way to spend the stretch is a design. It is not a validated digital-detox protocol.

## Design notes

Materials: a basket or a room where phones can actually leave, a notepad titled Later, a timer, and one chosen activity light enough to share.

Before the stretch, write the undone chores on the notepad so they have a place to sit. Medical alerts, caregiving, and genuine on-call duties stay available; name them as exceptions rather than pretending every household can vanish. Agree the length. Short: 20 minutes. Standard: a meal or about 45 minutes. Extended: an evening, with a planned pause so nobody is trapped.

Phones go in another room. Face-down on the table still trains the buzz. Choose the container before starting: cook one simple thing, sit outside, play one low-stakes game, or talk about the day. The container can be quiet. It cannot be "see what happens" with no plan, or the stretch becomes itch and withdrawal.

What makes the instructions fail: using the time to open a postponed fight; scoring who broke first; a secret second device; banning music or reading they had agreed to share; calling caregiving a distraction. At the end, two questions: when did attention leave, and what helped it come back? No winner.

Accessibility. Hearing and vision: the activity they choose has to be one both can do; a silent shared task is a valid container. Mobility and pain: the stretch comes to the couch if needed. Long-distance: a call with no second screen, length agreed, and chores written down on each side. Sensory overload: a familiar low-input activity, not a new intense one. Cognitive load: the Later list is the accommodation, not a moral extra.

Journal cue: the length, the container, one distraction that pulled, and whether they would repeat that length. Skip purity language.

## Consent and harm

One partner's job, disability, or parenting load may make a total ban unfair. Design the exception in advance. Undivided attention is not a demand for constant eye contact or constant talk. Either person may call a pause. Do not take the other person's phone as a punishment.

## Red flags

Do not cite a phubbing or screen-time study. None was opened. Do not promise that a phone-free hour improves the relationship by a stated amount. This is not a hunt for notifications or a contest.
