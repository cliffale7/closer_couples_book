# Dossier 081 — The Forbidden Question Box

- Catalog ID: 081
- Part: 11 — Radical Curiosity and the Surprising Stuff
- Access date: 2026-10-14
- Classification: reimagined
- Confidence: medium

## Source premise

> Privately contribute rarely asked questions, draw randomly, and freely pass when desired.

## Must preserve

Three mechanics together: questions contributed in private, drawn by chance, and declined with a free pass. A host who reads a clever list aloud, or a pass that must be justified, turns the box into an interrogation.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity. L1998 connected daily intimacy with disclosure and perceived responsiveness. It did not test a question box. A1997 used an escalating question procedure with strangers and found a closeness difference versus small talk in Study 1 only. That procedure is a different activity. Its question list must not be copied into this box.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Question jars, confession games, and “never have I ever” variants are folk forms. They are not validated protocols. The reimagining is the combination of rarely asked questions with an explicit, no-reason pass. Truth-or-dare belongs to 047 and stays there.

## Design notes

Materials: envelopes, folded slips, or a bowl. No special box is required. Time: 30–45 minutes, with a cap of four draws.

Before play, each person privately writes five questions they are rarely asked or rarely dare to ask. Original examples of the right grain, not a canon: “What do you edit out when you tell the story of your week?” “Which compliment do you trust least?” “What pleasure do you postpone until you feel you have earned it?” “What have you changed your mind about and not announced?” Writers should avoid questions they already know their partner will not want.

Draw one slip at a time. The person who draws may answer, trade the slip for a pass, or offer the slip back to its author to answer. A pass needs no explanation and no substitute dare. Unread slips can be destroyed. Stop while both are still willing.

Failure points: groaning at a pass; follow-up questions that corner someone who has finished; alcohol as the price of honesty; one partner supplying every slip.

Accessibility: the other person can read a drawn slip aloud if asked. Large print. Long-distance: a shared randomizer and private lists. The game is seated and quiet. Skip any question that assumes a body, a family form, or a sexual life the couple does not share.

## Consent and harm

Passing is success, not a deficit. Topics that are off the table should be named before writing if either person wants a boundary: sex, family trauma, ex-partners, money, or faith. A question that asks someone to disclose harm, illness, or a third party’s secret is a misfit for a game draw. Either person may end the round.

## Red flags

Do not reproduce the Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone, and Bator (1997) question set or any other copyrighted card deck. Do not call the box therapy. Do not imply that willingness to answer anything is a measure of love. Rarely asked is not the same as most invasive.
