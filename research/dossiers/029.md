# Dossier 029 — The Reassurance Menu

- Catalog ID: 029

- Access date: 2026-10-14

- Confidence: high

## Source premise

Create individualized menus of what helps when lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, or disconnected.

## Must preserve

Each person gets their own menu, and the menu distinguishes at least the five states in the premise: lonely, stressed, insecure, sad, disconnected. A single comfort strategy copied into every row fails, because the premise says the help is state-specific. The partner is not asked to mind-read which row is active.

## Evidence

No empirical citation is claimed for this activity.

## Tradition or practitioner frame

Comfort menus and "what I need when" cards circulate in couples work and in peer support. Classification stays reimagined. Five named states plus a personal menu is a design, not a validated coping protocol. Entry 015 stays with feeling inadequate or overlooked and which reassurances help. This chapter is the wider card.

## Design notes

Materials: a one-page grid per person, five rows, three columns: What it looks like on me, What helps, What makes it worse.

Private drafting, about 15 minutes. Each cell is a behavior. "Give me space" is usable only if they add how the space is offered ("say you'll be in the next room and come back at nine"). Include at least one help the person can give themselves, so the menu is not entirely a work order. Standard: talk through all five rows, briefly, about 40 minutes. Short: the two states they hit most often. Extended: add a signal for each row, a word or a text they can send when explaining is too hard.

The partner reads a row back and marks: I can do this, I can sometimes, this conflicts with what I need. Conflicts are negotiated later, not scored tonight. Menus get a review date. They are not permanent law.

What makes the instructions fail: the same hug prescribed for every row; items the partner cannot do (change careers, stop being tired); a worse-column that is used as a list of past crimes to litigate; no signal, so the menu exists and nobody knows which night they are on.

Accessibility. Templates in large type, or a conversation the other person writes down. Hearing and vision: paper or talk. Mobility and pain: help items must match real bodies and energy; "take a long walk" cannot be the only stress row. Long-distance: mark remote-capable helps (a call, a photo, a scheduled goodnight) separately from in-person ones. Sensory overload: quiet, low-light, and no-touch options appear somewhere on the menu if either person needs them. Those options are design, not a claim about sensory treatment.

Journal cue: the signal words and one help per state. Store the "worse" column only if both want that memory.

## Consent and harm

A menu does not authorize touching, sexual reassurance, or a conversation the person has declined. Insecure and sad can sit next to mental health problems; the menu is not a crisis plan. If they need one, say so and point them to their own supports and local emergency options without inventing a protocol in the chapter. Either person may leave a row blank. Do not use the worse-column as ammunition later.

## Red flags

Do not cite a coping-style or attachment study. None was opened. Do not collapse the five states into one "emotional support" language. I2024's caution against a single primary love language belongs if the writer is tempted to turn the menu into a type.
