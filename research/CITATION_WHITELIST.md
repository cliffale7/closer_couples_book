# Citation whitelist

Access date for every item below: **14 October 2026**. Chapter notes may use these IDs. Do not add a DOI that is not listed here unless `review/evidence/` records the page you actually opened.

## Empirical

### A1997

Aron, A., Melinat, E., Aron, E. N., Vallone, R. D., & Bator, R. J. (1997). The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness: A procedure and some preliminary findings. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 23*(4), 363–377. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297234003

What it supports: a roughly 45-minute pair of escalating self-disclosure and relationship-building tasks produced greater post-interaction closeness than a small-talk comparison in Study 1. Studies 2 and 3 did not find closeness differences for attitude matching, expected liking, or making closeness an explicit goal. Participants were not a study of long-term couples falling in love.

What it does not support: “four minutes of eye contact makes people fall in love.” Do not reproduce the task’s question list verbatim.

Checked against the SAGE abstract and a publicly posted PDF of the article on 14 October 2026.

### K1989

Kellerman, J., Lewis, J., & Laird, J. D. (1989). Looking and loving: The effects of mutual gaze on feelings of romantic love. *Journal of Research in Personality, 23*(2), 145–161. https://doi.org/10.1016/0092-6566(89)90020-2

What it supports: in two studies, mutual unbroken gaze for two minutes with an opposite-sex stranger was associated with higher self-reported affection, liking, and (in Study 2) passionate love. Study 1 compared eye gaze, hand gaze, and blink-counting. The mutual-gaze increase was not the same as one person staring while the other looked away. Study 2’s effect was limited to people more inclined to read their own behavior as information about themselves.

What it does not support: a universal romantic result for established couples, a four-minute prescription, or a guarantee of love.

Checked against the ScienceDirect abstract on 14 October 2026.

### G2004

Gable, S. L., Reis, H. T., Impett, E. A., & Asher, E. R. (2004). What do you do when things go right? The intrapersonal and interpersonal benefits of sharing positive events. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 87*(2), 228–245. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228

What it supports: telling someone about a good event (capitalization) was associated with greater positive affect and well-being beyond the event itself. Perceived active-constructive responses were associated with higher relationship quality; passive or destructive responses were not the helpful pattern.

Checked against the author-posted PDF on 14 October 2026.

### A2000

Aron, A., Norman, C. C., Aron, E. N., McKenna, C., & Heyman, R. E. (2000). Couples’ shared participation in novel and arousing activities and experienced relationship quality. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78*(2), 273–284. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.273

What it supports: correlational work linked shared exciting activities with relationship satisfaction, with boredom as a mediator. In laboratory experiments, about seven minutes of a novel and arousing joint task increased experienced relationship quality more than a mundane task. This is short-term experienced quality, not a permanent change.

Checked against the Europe PMC abstract on 14 October 2026.

### L1998

Laurenceau, J.-P., Barrett, L. F., & Pietromonaco, P. R. (1998). Intimacy as an interpersonal process: The importance of self-disclosure, partner disclosure, and perceived partner responsiveness in interpersonal exchanges. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 74*(5), 1238–1251. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1238

What it supports: in diary studies of daily interactions, intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator. In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure.

Checked against the journal abstract and an author-posted PDF on 14 October 2026.

### G2012

Gordon, A. M., Impett, E. A., Kogan, A., Oveis, C., & Keltner, D. (2012). To have and to hold: Gratitude promotes relationship maintenance in intimate bonds. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 103*(2), 257–274. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028723

What it supports: feeling appreciated and feeling appreciative of a partner were tied, across methods, to responsiveness, commitment, and relationship maintenance. Appreciation can be visible in behavior. It does not mean a scripted compliment exercise has been tested as a treatment.

Checked against the Europe PMC abstract and a publicly posted PDF on 14 October 2026.

### J2017

Jakubiak, B. K., & Feeney, B. C. (2017). Affectionate touch to promote relational, psychological, and physical well-being in adulthood: A theoretical model and review of the research. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 21*(3), 228–252. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868316650307

What it supports: a theoretical model and review arguing that wanted affectionate touch can relate to relational, psychological, and physical well-being, including stress-related pathways. It is a review and model, not a trial of this book’s exercises. Touch that is unwanted is outside the claim.

Checked against the SAGE abstract on 14 October 2026.

### I2024

Impett, E. A., Park, H. G., & Muise, A. (2024). Popular psychology through a scientific lens: Evaluating love languages from a relationship science perspective. *Current Directions in Psychological Science, 33*(2), 87–92. https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214231217663

What it supports: a review finding little empirical support for the claims that each person has one primary love language, that there are exactly five, and that matching languages is what predicts satisfaction. People do seem to benefit from a range of affectionate behavior. Use this to keep “What Loving Me Actually Looks Like” from becoming a love-languages quiz.

Checked against the SAGE page and the University of Toronto summary of the review on 14 October 2026.

## Frameworks, not controlled tests of these chapters

### R1988

Reis, H. T., & Shaver, P. (1988). Intimacy as an interpersonal process. In S. Duck (Ed.), *Handbook of personal relationships* (pp. 367–389). Wiley.

Use as the model that L1998 tested: disclosure, response, and the feeling of being understood. Page numbers are from the standard citation of that chapter; this audit did not re-typeset the 1988 book.

### GOTTMAN

John Gottman’s clinical writing popularized love maps, bids for connection, repair attempts, and the “emotional bank account.” Treat these as practitioner frameworks. Do not invent a journal article for them. Do not claim a percentage of conflicts or a divorce-prediction statistic.

### CHAPMAN

Gary Chapman’s *The 5 Love Languages* (1992) is a popular book. Cite I2024 if the chapter mentions it, and do not present the five languages as established science.

### B1992

Buehlman, K. T., Gottman, J. M., & Katz, L. F. (1992). How a couple views their past predicts their future: Predicting divorce from an oral history interview. *Journal of Family Psychology, 5*(3–4), 295–318. https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.5.3-4.295

What it supports: a coded Oral History Interview about dating and marital history, philosophy of marriage, and change over time, in a selected sample of married parents. Methods recruited 56 families; the summary and follow-up concern 52. The interview was conducted for the study. Over about three years, the manner of telling was associated with later marital stability. Fondness, we-ness, negativity, chaos, and disappointment ran in the directions stated in the abstract. This is a correlate in that sample, not a reminiscence treatment, and not this book’s exercise.

What it does not support: any discriminant-function accuracy or divorce-prediction percentage. Do not quote one. Do not write that either spouse’s fondness score predicted stability. Wife fondness was below the paper’s predictor cutoff and was not in the nine-variable predictor set. Do not turn separate husband and wife codes into gender advice. Do not describe an interview section the paper does not have. The prompts that are fair to name are good times and bad times, philosophy, and change.

PDF checked: `https://www.johngottman.net/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/How-a-couple-views-their-past-predicts-their-future-predicting-divorce-from-an-oral-history-interview.pdf`

### S2007

Schacter, D. L., Addis, D. R., & Buckner, R. L. (2007). Remembering the past to imagine the future: The prospective brain. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 8*(9), 657–661. https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2213

What it supports: the authors argue that imagining the future depends on much of the same neural machinery as remembering. Their constructive episodic simulation hypothesis says future simulation recombines details from the past, and the review says that hypothesis has support. Use “argue.” The same review says further work still has to test whether future simulations recombine elements from different episodes or retrieve fragments.

What it does not support: that imagining makes an event happen, that people cannot tell memory from imagination, that neuroscience validates any chapter in this book, or that a centenarian game or a future-memory exercise was tested. Do not quote the review’s clinical cases. Do not say the review proved recombination.

PDF checked: `https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/neuroscience/2007-schacter.pdf`

### G1999

Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. *American Psychologist, 54*(7), 493–503. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493

What it supports: a distinction between goal intentions (“I intend to reach x”) and implementation intentions (“When situation x arises, I will perform response y”). The review describes a research program in which those if-then plans supported goal attainment in the domains studied. In that review, goal intentions had modest effects. They were not useless.

What it does not support: a study of promises between partners, proof that a vow in this book will be kept, or the phrase “bare sincerity is weak” as Gollwitzer’s conclusion. The if-then line is not a tested couple treatment.

PDF checked: `https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/99_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions.pdf`
