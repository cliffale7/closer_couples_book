## The hook

The jam jar doesn't match the diner tumbler, and the tumbler doesn't match the good glass you're slightly afraid to break. Line them up anyway. Each one stands for a year, or for an era you'd rather name than number. Seven is as long as the row gets. One glass is raised at a time, for a year you want to honor. At the far end, past the last base, a blank card waits for a year that hasn't arrived.

Most toasts float. To us. To the next one. To whatever the room seems to need. This one has to land. You tell one scene you can still see. You name one thing the other person did that you actually remember. Then you stop. If the year has gone fuzzy, the glass holds water and a sentence that says so. The speaker doesn't invent a memory to fill a glass.

## Why it might matter

Years collapse in the retelling. A long stretch becomes "the busy time," and the busy time becomes a shrug you both recognize. The shrug can be fond and still leave the year unnamed. A scene is harder to counterfeit. A particular act, such as quarters in a palm, a bowl left on a counter, or a person who stayed in the car, gives the other something they can recognize as theirs.

Feeling appreciated and feeling appreciative were tied to responsiveness and maintenance in that research, and this toast wasn't the procedure studied (see G2012 in the notes). A precise memory isn't a treatment, and a row of glasses won't maintain a life by itself. What the shape can do is keep a compliment from hovering at "you are wonderful," which may be true and still too large to hold.

The card at the far end does a different job. Each of you may write one hope there. A hope isn't a promise the other person must keep. Hearing it isn't agreeing to carry it. That gap is what makes the sentence writable. A year you leave unturned is still an honest year. In this ritual, a skip is a finished turn.

## Before you begin

Stay indoors, at a table where a row of glasses can live through the evening. The two of you are the guest list. You need unmatched glasses, up to seven, blank cards, and a pen. Water is enough. Alcohol is optional and not required. If either of you wants water, pour water and let the drink question end. A mug counts. A jam jar counts. If the glasses already match, borrow one odd cup so the years don't look like a set.

Set them side by side, each on its own base. Earlier years go left and later years go right, unless you pick another order before anyone lifts a glass. The blank card stays at the far end, past the latest year. These cards name years and lean on the glasses. Place cards belong to a different session. One glass is raised at a time.

You can stop well before seven. Three eras inside one calendar year can be the whole row: the first month, the move, last month. A long life together still stops at seven. Park an eighth year on a scrap, off the table, for another night. Don't fatten a thin memory so the line looks finished.

Each of you may propose a year or an era. It joins the row only when you're both willing to see it there. "Not that one" is a complete answer. A year on the table isn't a promise to toast it. Either person may skip when the turn arrives, and a skip gets no substitute story. The other person doesn't supply one, doesn't ask for a harder try, and doesn't stretch their own toast across the quiet.

A year from before you were a pair belongs only if the speaker remembers a scene they saw, or a telling they can repeat without decorating it. A mood about the other person's past isn't a scene. Call it fuzzy, or skip it.

Before the first raise, leave out a third person's secret, any subject you've already closed, and anyone's body used as a joke. If the picture would embarrass them, choose another or leave the glass down. A joke you'd both tell is welcome.

You may say you love them in the toast, if you mean it, and then stop. The ritual asks for no touch. A toast isn't consent to touch or sex. Either of you may end the evening after any year.

Pour the water. Leave the far card blank until the row is underway.

## The experience

In the standard version, give the choosing about 15 minutes. Write each year on a small card and lean the card against its glass. Read the names once, in order, before any story attaches itself. If a name is only a mood, "the good year," sharpen it until you could point at a stretch of time, or take that glass off the table.

The lines below are the printable set. Copy them by hand if you aren't printing. Seven year cards can be small, about the size of a business card, so they lean. The future card should take two handwritten lines, about 4 by 6 inches, and it lies flat at the far end. The numbers mark the order you move down the row. A later number isn't a harder year. Any year you chose may sit on any line. The fuzzy rule, the skip, and the stop apply to every glass. The cards repeat them in different words so a glance can catch a speech before it grows a second act.

```cards
Year ________ | Glass 1. Fill in a year or an era. One scene you can still see, one thing the other person did, then stop.
Year ________ | Glass 2. A different year. One room and one remembered act. Skip if you can't see it, with no substitute story.
Year ________ | Glass 3. If this year is fuzzy, let the glass hold water and say so in a sentence. Don't invent the rest.
Year ________ | Glass 4. Ordinary time counts. Name one thing they did. Stop before a speech about the whole year.
Year ________ | Glass 5. Name an action you remember, not a trait. Put the glass back when you finish.
Year ________ | Glass 6. If the scene would embarrass them, leave this glass unraised. A skip needs nothing in its place.
Year ________ | Glass 7. Last year in your row. Same shape. Don't add another glass to keep the evening going.
Year that has not arrived | Each of you may write one hope. It isn't a promise the other person must keep. Read it aloud only if you both agree.
```

Toss a coin for who leads the first glass. Each glass offers a turn to each of you before anyone moves on. The leader goes first. The other person may then raise that same glass for a different scene, skip, or call the year fuzzy. On the next glass, the lead swaps. A skip spends the turn. It doesn't add time, and it doesn't ask the other person to speak in the gap.

On a turn, raise the glass and toast, leave it down and skip, or leave it down and say the year is fuzzy. Each choice ends the turn. The glass can keep its water either way.

A toast uses this shape, and then it ends.

One concrete scene. A room, a sidewalk, a kitchen at a known hour, something you could point at. "We were happy" is a caption, and a caption isn't a scene. If you can't see a place, use the fuzzy rule.

One thing the other person did that you can actually remember. A verb you could show with your hands: they passed you quarters, they left a bowl, they sorted screws, they waited in the car. "You were supportive" doesn't qualify. Name the act. If you aren't sure it happened, don't promote the guess. If two winters have fused, say so, and keep the detail you can stand behind. The speaker doesn't invent a memory to fill a glass.

A stop. Set the glass back on its spot, or say "That's the toast," and stop. No lesson for them to absorb, and no second story that starts "and another time."

If a year is fuzzy, the glass holds water and one sentence. "This year is fuzzy for me." That sentence is the turn. Don't follow it with the version that seems likely. Your partner doesn't fill in the room unless you ask.

The listener keeps still while the glass is up. A correction, or a competing story from the same stretch of time, stays unsaid. After the stop, "I heard you" is enough, and so is quiet. If you remember the day differently, keep your version unless the speaker wants one sentence afterward.

Stop when you've both been offered every glass, or when 90 minutes have passed. An unraised glass isn't homework. Don't slide a scrap-paper year into the row because the talk feels generous.

Then the far card. Each of you may write one hope for the year that hasn't arrived, in a single sentence. If it gives the other person a job, rewrite it until it asks nothing, or leave the line blank. "I hope we get a week with no errands" can stay. "You'll arrange the week" can't. "I hope I ask first" is about your own conduct, and it's still a hope, not a voucher. In the standard sitting, turn the card face down. Don't read it aloud.

You're done when every glass has been offered, skipped, called fuzzy, or left because the clock ended it, and each of you has had a chance to write or to leave the line empty. Nothing is tallied. A longer memory isn't a lead. A skip isn't a loss. There's no winner.

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 35 minutes and uses three glasses. Choose them in about 5 minutes. Each glass gets about 8 minutes total. If you both speak, split that time: one scene, one act, a stop. A fuzzy sentence or a skip still closes the turn. Spend the last 6 minutes writing the hopes, and leave them unread. Spare quiet is part of this version. A fourth glass is a different night.

The standard version takes about 90 minutes, with up to seven glasses, under the procedure above. You may both speak to one year when the scenes differ. 8 minutes a glass is a fair guardrail. If you're both toasting every year, stop at 90 minutes even if a glass remains. That remainder is a finished standard evening. Write the far card and don't read it.

The extended version takes about three hours. Keep the cap at seven, and give both turns room, including the quiet after a stop. Eat first. A pause in the middle belongs here. Make tea, and don't spend the break ranking what you've heard. After the row, read the far card aloud only if you both agree. If either of you wants the lines left on the paper, they stay, and the evening still counts. Each person reads their own hope. The listener doesn't improve it. Glad, unsure, and too heavy to carry are all whole answers. A plan, if you ever make one, waits for a later day when you both still want it. If the row ends early, you're done. Stay at the table if you like. Don't pad a thin year to use up the clock.

## Going deeper

If a toast starts to annex the night, write the scene and the act on the year card first. Read that, then stop. A second sentence can wait for the journal or be crossed out.

After the stop, the listener may repeat the deed in one sentence and let the speaker edit it. "The bowl was in the evening." Keep it that short. It isn't another toast, and either of you may skip the check.

A heavy year can stand in the row when you both want it there. The shape stays: one scene, one thing they did, a stop. You don't owe an explanation, a repair, or proof that you got through it. If what shows up is a grievance you can't tell without making them small, skip. The glass is for a thing you remember, at a size they can hear.

A hope may include fear. "I hope next year is quieter than this one." That still assigns nothing. If writing it would sour the night, leave the line blank. A private copy of your own hope can live on a separate slip in your own notes. The far card holds the lines you're willing to leave where the other person might see them.

## Variations and replay

An anniversary, New Year's, or an unmarked Tuesday will all hold the row. When you play again, choose a new line of years. Repeat a year only when the scene is new. If all you have is last time's story, skip that glass.

On a call, each of you sets a row at home and counts aloud so the year matches. Lift toward the screen, or leave the glass down and let the words carry it. Write the hope off camera. Read it only in the extended version, and only if you both agree.

If lifting hurts, point at the glass and speak. If standing is the hard part, stay seated. A tray on a couch is a fine sideboard. Juice, tea, or water in a mug marks a year as honestly as anything else. If a stronger pour is making the stories longer and less exact, switch that glass to water.

When seven would feel like a performance, use three glasses. When you both want a single year, one glass is a fair short cousin. Say so in the session line. Don't borrow extra years for the look of a longer life. With thin walls or someone asleep nearby, use a kitchen voice or read a written toast softly. Either of you may pause after a glass and finish another night. A paused row is a real session.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They aren't you. They're here so the rules have somewhere to stand.

They could overfill seven glasses and still have years left over. They set five. Vale keeps two proposals off the table with "Not that one." Rowan keeps off the year of a move that still snarls. Nobody smuggles those years back in as color for a safer toast. The row is the year they met, a winter of night shifts, a hardware-store spring, one heavier year they're both willing to see, and a recent quiet year.

Rowan raises the jam jar. "The laundromat on Pine, the broken change machine. You had quarters. You handed me two and didn't make a thing of it. That's the toast." Vale almost adds that the dryer ate a sock, then lets the turn end.

Vale lifts the diner tumbler. "The kitchen around 6 a.m., under that miserable light. You left a bowl on the counter and a note that said to eat before I tried to be a person. You were already asleep." Vale stops there, without asking the bowls to come back.

The hardware-store spring falls apart in Rowan's hands. "Drawers of screws, and then I'm in a different year. This one is fuzzy for me." The green tumbler goes back full of water. Vale knows the likely aisle and leaves it unnamed.

The heavier year reaches Vale. "I'm leaving this one." Rowan had a Wednesday and a vending-machine coffee ready, and keeps both. There's no substitute story. The glass stays, and they move on.

For the quiet year Rowan raises a chipped cup. "Last October, the Sunday we fixed the lamp and then forgot to be impressive. You sat on the floor and held the shade so it wouldn't roll under the couch. That's the toast." They laugh. The laugh isn't a score.

On the far card Vale writes, "I hope you stop starting projects," then crosses it out. The line that remains is, "I hope we get one week next year with no house projects." The first was a job. The second is a hope, and Rowan doesn't have to deliver the week. Rowan writes, "I hope I ask before I assume you want a plan." That binds Rowan alone, and it still isn't a promise Vale can collect later. They turn the card face down, drink the water, and return the glasses to the wrong shelves, where those glasses already lived.

## Afterward

Leave the year cards up while you clear the glasses, if you like the row you chose. Keep what you want and recycle the rest. The far card can go into a book you'll both open, into a private note, or into the trash once you've each seen your own line. Pick one, and skip the second ceremony.

If you talk, talk about which toast was easy to stop and which skip you're glad you used. There's no best glass. The next day, a single message can repeat the act you heard, when that would feel like attention. A plan born from a hope waits for an ordinary day when you both still want it. If the evening touched a closed subject, name it in a sentence and set it down. Washing the glasses can be the whole close.

## Our Story

Write the date, the time, the place, which version you played, a mood if you want one, a one-line intention, and your initials if you want them. Mark the session done or paused. Private pages are optional to share. Keep a line in your own note if you'd rather the shared pages hold only what you both choose to put there. A later sitting gets its own lines. Two revisits are a pattern, and you can keep going after that.

```journal
session: date, time, place, version played, mood if wanted, intention, initials if wanted, done or paused
partner_a:
- The scene I raised a glass for, and the thing you did that I could actually remember
- A year I skipped or called fuzzy, and what I refused to invent
partner_b:
- A toast of yours I can still picture, or the silence I managed not to fill with a better story
- The hope I wrote for the year that has not arrived, and whether I wanted it read aloud
shared:
- What actually happened
- The line or moment worth keeping
- What we discovered
- What we might do next, if anything
revisit:
- Attempt, date, and what changed
- Then versus now
```
