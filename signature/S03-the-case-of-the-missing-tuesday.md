## The hook

A Tuesday can vanish without anyone taking it. You both got to Wednesday. What's left is an empty square, a coat already on a chair, a receipt that never became a story, and the belief that nothing happened. Something usually did. It was too ordinary to file.

This is a parlor mystery for that kind of absence. Stay in one room, in two seats. You're both the detectives and the witnesses. There is no culprit, and there is no crime. The missing thing is the texture of a day. You solve it by talking. Each of you will point to one sentence the other wrote that you recognize as true, and one sentence you want corrected. The corrections are the solution. This book has no hidden answer for your Tuesday, and it won't invent your memories.

## Why it might matter

You start from four objects already in the room and ask a thin Tuesday in both directions. One of the five sentences has to include "I don't know." Then each of you names one sentence you recognize and one you want corrected. Those corrections are the solution. A thin day never had to explain itself. Asking for it with the objects still in the room is a way to be specific about each other.

Praise says a memory was welcome. A correction says which sentence can live in both accounts. Diary studies found that intimacy tracked self-disclosure and partner disclosure, with perceived partner responsiveness as a partial mediator (see L1998). In Study 2, emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than factual disclosure. Those diaries didn't test this hour. They're a reason to hear the mood in an answer.

A review argues that imagining a future uses much of the same machinery as remembering. The authors' hypothesis, which the review says has support, is that a future scene recombines details from the past, and the same review says further work still has to test whether future simulations recombine elements from different episodes or retrieve fragments (see S2007). "That part is wrong" is this chapter's correction, not a result the review proved. This chapter hasn't been clinically tested.

Gottman's clinical writing calls a working picture of a partner's inner world a love map. An unmarked Tuesday is the kind of page that metaphor cares about. It isn't a score. If a small good thing turns up, say you're glad, and stop. The research limit is in the notes.

## Before you begin

Play the standard version first. It takes about 70 minutes. Stay seated. Either of you may pause, pass, or stop. A pass ends that question. It doesn't end the case, and it doesn't demand a substitute confession.

Choose a Tuesday inside the last twelve months on which both of you were living, and which neither of you has asked to leave closed. You don't have to have shared the city. "I wasn't there" is a complete answer. Each of you may veto a day without explaining. Three vetoes, or a run of closed Tuesdays, means an older Tuesday or the fictional sample only.

Anything already off the table stays there: a third person, a body, an illness, a loss, a retired fight. "Pass" or "smaller" is the whole reply. "Smaller" narrows the ask to one visible detail, such as the cuff.

Every exhibit is already in this room. Name a substitute out loud.

- **Calendar.** One you can see from your seat, a phone calendar if that phone is already here, or a week drawn on paper you have. If Tuesday already has writing, copy the week, leave that square blank, cover the original, and say once what you covered. The blank copy is the exhibit.
- **Teapot.** The pot already here, or two cups, mugs, or glasses. "These two mugs are the teapot" is a legal naming. If the kettle is elsewhere, the exhibit stays cold.
- **Coat.** A coat or jacket already on a chair or a hook you can reach, or a layer one of you is wearing. Private pockets stay private. The coat is still an exhibit.
- **Receipt.** A receipt or ticket already in a wallet, bag, or pocket here. Turn medical totals and surprises face down. If you'd rather show nothing, label a blank card "receipt." "I don't know" still counts as the round.
- **Also.** Two seats, two sheets, something to write with, and a timer you can both see.

Photos stay out until the case is solved. Anything a camera tells you later is labeled later. A solved case isn't an agreement to touch or to continue into sex. That needs its own yes, separate from the game.

## The experience

Finish the sentence you're in when the bell rings, then stop.

**Choose the day. About 4 minutes.** Name the Tuesday by date or by a marker you both accept. If either of you calls it closed, it's closed. If you're playing only the sample, go to Examples.

**Name the exhibits before any theory. About 4 minutes.** Lay out the blank Tuesday, the coat, the receipt or its card, and the teapot or its substitute. Say: "This is the calendar. Tuesday is unmarked. This is the coat. This is the receipt. This is the teapot." A substitute gets its real name and its role. An early theory goes on your paper, face down. It may feed the reconstruction. It stays unspoken during the rounds.

**Four rounds, about 11 minutes each.** Order: calendar, coat, receipt, teapot. One asks, the other answers, then you swap, so both of you answer every exhibit. Whoever has the more restless curiosity asks first on the calendar and on the receipt. The other person asks first on the coat and on the teapot. A coin already in the room can decide who asks first, if you'd rather not negotiate it.

Use one card from that exhibit, or a question of your own that the exhibit and memory could answer. One follow-up, and it stays on the answer. "Say more about the empty square" is a follow-up. The asker's own anecdote ends the question. The asker doesn't answer inside the turn. Silence belongs to the person asked.

A pass ends that question, not the night. The asker may offer one different card from the same exhibit. A second pass ends that answerer's turn. Swap anyway if the other direction hasn't happened. "Smaller" keeps the turn and shrinks the question. A blank receipt still gets the receipt cards, and "I don't know" is a full answer.

The cards are the questions. Cut them if you want a pile. Large print is welcome. Reading them from the page is enough.

```cards
Calendar | What else is written in that week, and which of those marks would have changed Tuesday if it had landed there instead?
Calendar | Who usually writes on this calendar, and what kind of event earns a mark rather than living in one person's head?
Calendar | This Tuesday square is empty. What would have had to happen before noon for one of you to write in it?
Coat | Where did this coat spend the middle of that Tuesday, as far as you actually know, and which part of the day are you tempted to fill in?
Coat | What on the cloth, or in the pockets you have agreed to empty here, belongs to that Tuesday, and what is left over from another day?
Coat | Who wears this when the weather turns, and do you know whether that same person had it on during the Tuesday you chose?
Receipt | Read every printed mark aloud, including the time, the items, and anything voided or crossed out. Which of those belongs to a stop on the way somewhere?
Receipt | If there is handwriting, whose hand is it, and does it record something that already happened or something that was still waiting?
Receipt | How many people does this paper feed, carry, or charge, and what does a single line fail to prove about who else was there?
Teapot | Was this pot used on that Tuesday, and if you think it was, what time of day can you actually support?
Teapot | How many people drank, and what would a clean cup mean if you are wrong about who was home?
Teapot | What, if anything, did you talk about while it steeped, and what subject was in the room and never said?
```

**Write, then read. About 8 minutes silent, then about 10 together.** Number a page from 1 to 5. Don't show it. Each sentence does one job, in this order.

1. Name the Tuesday by date or by the marker you both accepted.
2. Say where at least one exhibit was during part of that day.
3. Say one thing you believe the other person did that you didn't see.
4. Include the words "I don't know." This is the sentence that refuses an invention.
5. End on a sentence you'll let the other person correct.

The person who answered the first calendar question reads all five. If that turn was a pass, the other reads first. No interrupting. Then the four closing moves. The first reader names one sentence they recognize as true and reads it back. The writer confirms it or edits a word. A wrong recognition gets rejected, and the reader chooses again. If none is true, say so. The first reader then names one sentence to correct, in a sentence or two. The writer accepts, amends, or says "still open." The second reader does those same two moves. Stop, unless you both want one more correction.

The case is solved when those four moves are done. Corrections are the solution. "I wouldn't correct any of these" counts when the pages agree. There's no score. A score would pay you to invent. You're done when every agreed exhibit has been asked both ways, or passed, and the closing is finished. If you finish early, stop. Don't open a second Tuesday to fill the clock.

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 25 minutes. Choose the day or the sample. Name the calendar and one other exhibit honestly in the room. A blank card counts. Spend about 3 minutes choosing, 2 naming, 7 on each exhibit with both directions included, 3 writing, and 3 closing. Write three sentences: the day's name, what one exhibit can prove, and one line you'll let the other correct. Keep the four closing moves.

The standard version takes about 70 minutes. Play this first. All four exhibits, both directions, five sentences, the times named above.

The extended version takes about 120 minutes. Solve the standard case, then add the round called "what we still cannot know" (about 50 minutes). You may decline it. A solved standard case is a finished night. Spend about 10 minutes underlining any clause with neither an exhibit nor a witness. Spend about 20 minutes writing up to three lines each that the exhibits can't support. A line stays blank unless a real memory is offered and labeled "witness." Spend about 10 minutes on one optional witness memory each, closed with one recognition and one correction if a memory was offered. Spend the last 10 minutes on four joint lines: two sentences you both accept, one correction you both accept, and one blank you both keep. The blank belongs in the solution.

## Going deeper

If the day is sore, take a two-hour window and leave the rest unnamed. If it's so ordinary you freeze, keep the hour you can see. The person who remembers more reports what they saw and handled. "You always vanish on weekdays" is a theory. "I didn't see you between the door and the tea" is a report.

Repeat a correction once, and let the writer edit the repetition. If the pages match too neatly, each of you may add, "What I may have smoothed over is." Skip the line if you'd have to invent a quarrel. If speech sands an answer down, write it and read it. Another night, play the whole case on paper, same timer, same ban on theories during the rounds.

## Variations and replay

On a call, name the exhibits you can see. No coat in reach means the blank-card rule. Cameras stay optional. A Tuesday from before you knew each other is legal if you're both curious, even when one page is mostly "I don't know."

Replay on a later thin day. Write one new question per exhibit, in the same shape as the cards. A question that worked by surprise will work less well twice. If the word Tuesday is the trouble, name another weekday and keep the rules. Stay seated. A garment in another room isn't an exhibit tonight. If reading the page is hard, one of you can read a card aloud and the other can answer on paper. There's no score and no forfeit.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They aren't you. Another pair can play this file before trusting the game with a real week.

### The fictional case file

One room, evening. The exhibits are already on the table and the chair. The coat pocket was emptied at the start. Nobody got up again.

**Calendar.** Monday: "Vale, dentist, 9:40." Tuesday: empty. Wednesday: "Rowan, library hold." Thursday: "late supper." Friday: "train, both." Saturday: empty. Sunday: "Jun, call." In the margin, outside every square: "rain?"

**Coat.** Charcoal, on a chair. The left cuff is dry, with a pale tide line. From the pocket: a transit slip printed "Tue 10 Mar" and no passenger name, a peppermint wrapper, and a library slip for *Kiln Street Atlas*, due Wednesday 11 March.

**Receipt.** Already on the table. Printed 10 Mar, 07:42: black tea, oat milk, ginger cake, an extra shot voided, cash, one order. On the back, Vale's hand: "ask Rowan about the blue folder."

**Teapot.** Empty, cool, copper on the lid, tag reading smoked black. The window cup has a tea ring. The other cup is clean. Under the pot, Rowan's hand: "9:10, not the morning."

Warm-up players use only this file and the cards. When the file is silent, say so. Don't give these two your own life. Ask both ways, write five sentences, and stop. Leave the key covered. The fictional close is past the notes, and your own Tuesday has no key.

## Afterward

Stay with the corrected sentence before you promote it into a theory of character. A thin Tuesday is a finding: the day never had to perform. If a closed subject got brushed, one sentence, then leave it. A later message that remembers the correction is plenty. A grand portrait will be less accurate than the line they already edited.

Solved isn't a yes to anything physical. If the evening turns that way, it needs its own yes. You can also stop, and let the day stay partly missing.

### Cover this until the sample case is finished

Rowan and Vale, who are fictional, closed the sample this way.

Vale asks what earns a calendar mark. Rowan says they write only what the other person must remember, so Tuesday stayed empty, and "rain?" is a Sunday question, not a weather report. They swap. Rowan asks what would have forced a note before noon. Vale starts a closed work week, says "pass," and answers the neighboring-marks question instead: dentist, library hold, train, Jun. Then they play coat, receipt, and teapot from the cards.

Rowan's five sentences:

1. The Tuesday is 10 March, the empty square after Vale's dentist.
2. The coat was on Vale in the morning. The cuff met wet weather, and the transit slip is printed that day.
3. I didn't see the counter, and I believe Vale bought the tea and the ginger cake alone at 7:42.
4. I don't know whether the library slip means that anyone went for a book that day.
5. Correct this if it's wrong: we talked about the blue folder while the tea was pouring at 9:10, and both cups were used.

Vale's five sentences:

1. Tuesday 10 March stayed blank because we only write reminders the other person needs.
2. I wore the coat in the rain and kept the transit slip. The peppermint wrapper was already in the pocket from you.
3. I wrote the folder note because I hadn't asked yet, and the voided shot was me changing my mind at the counter.
4. I don't know what you did between the door and the evening, except that you were home when I left and home when the pot was on.
5. Correct this if I stole it: I made the smoked tea at 9:10, and you drank both cups.

Rowan reads first and recognizes Vale's third sentence, the note written because the question hadn't been asked. Rowan corrects the fifth: Rowan made the tea, wrote the scrap because Vale had asked whether the pot was left from breakfast, and drank the one cup. Vale's cup stayed clean. Vale accepts. Vale recognizes the solo purchase at 7:42, and corrects the folder talk and the two used cups. Rowan accepts. The library line stays "I don't know." The case is solved.
