## The hook

You can know someone well and still let a whole season flatten into "that year we were busy." Tonight you interrupt that flattening while the week still has a texture. You sit at a table, put a few pieces of this season into an envelope or a box, and write down a day when the two of you will open it together.

The date lives on paper. Both of you write it, and you can change it later by writing a new line together. The capsule stays in the house, on a shelf or in a drawer you can both name. The key stays with you. It means either of you may ask to open the capsule before the date. Opening early is allowed. It isn't a failure.

If one of you is away when the date arrives, the capsule waits. You open it alone only when the slip you wrote at sealing says that you may. You're making a meeting, and you're writing down the ways the meeting can move.

## Why it might matter

Seal a few pieces of this season, and clip the opening date outside the seal so either of you can rewrite it later. The kitchen joke, the hour the light hits the table, who is tired this month: later retelling keeps less of that. A page written tonight can hold a detail the smoother story would drop. The capsule is that page, plus a day you've agreed to read it beside the life you're actually having.

The date slip is an if-then plan. Gollwitzer calls that form an implementation intention: when situation x arises, perform response y (see G1999). "When this date arrives, we open the capsule together" is that plan. "We should open this sometime" is a goal intention. In the research program that review describes, if-then plans supported goal attainment in the domains studied. Goal intentions had modest effects. They weren't useless. A wish with no date can still be sincere. G1999 didn't study couples, and it didn't test this capsule. The slip names a day you can meet, move, or meet early. It doesn't make the opening go well.

You'll likely picture that day while you seal the envelope. Schacter, Addis, and Buckner argue that imagining a future event uses memory's constructive machinery (see S2007). Their constructive episodic simulation hypothesis says future simulation recombines details from the past, and the review says that hypothesis has support. The same review says further work was still needed, to test whether future simulations recombine elements from different episodes or retrieve fragments. Use the picture to choose a date you can both actually meet. Imagining the evening doesn't make it happen, and the review doesn't show that this capsule works.

If a hard sentence needs an answer, say it tonight. A sealed page is a poor place for a line the other person hasn't heard. 031, A Love Letter From the Future, writes from a year you haven't lived. 090, The Future Memory, tells one happy scene as if it already happened. 096, The Next Chapter, chooses a small way to go on from what you did. This session stores pieces of the present and names a day to read them. A letter, if you add one, is from tonight.

## Before you begin

An envelope or a box is a complete capsule. A flap, a string, or a clasp will close it. Use a photograph you have the right to keep, or draw a stand-in. A key, if you want one, already lives in the house and goes back to its hook.

Sit where you can both see the date slip. A table works, and so does a call if you each write the same date while you're on the line. Stay seated if you need to. If a pen is tiring, dictate, and have the writer read the line back.

Say what stays out of the capsule. A pass needs no substitute confession. Other people's private lives, bodies, money, and any subject either of you has closed can stay out. Skip a sentence that would ambush the other person on the opening day.

The picture for this session shows a sealed capsule, a photograph, a folded letter, and a key, with the date drawn as marks on the lid. Those marks are a picture. Your date is a line on paper. Leave the lid, the envelope, and the box unmarked, so a later pen can change the day.

Choose one date you can both picture showing up for. Three weeks out, or a weekend you already know you'll share a city. Both of you write it on the slip, with the month clear enough for a stranger to read.

## The experience

Give the standard sealing about 90 minutes, and stop sooner once the capsule is closed. The opening is the later day on the slip, or the day you rewrite together. Blank is a complete choice for every item except the date.

```cards
Container | An envelope or a box. Anything you can close is a complete capsule.
Photograph or drawing | One picture from this season, or a drawing that stands in for a picture, or leave blank.
Caption line | What it is, the date, and who may see it, or leave blank.
Letter from one of you | A folded page, sealed if you want it sealed, or leave blank.
Letter from the other | A folded page, sealed if you want it sealed, or leave blank.
Shared note | A few sentences in both of your words about this season, or leave blank.
Opening date | The day you both write on a slip kept outside the seal, so you can change it later.
Key | A house key that stays in the house, a drawn key, or the sentence that either of you may ask to open early. Leave the object out if the sentence is enough.
Place | The shelf or drawer in the house you both can name.
If one of us is away | The capsule waits, unless a sentence you both write tonight says one of you may open it alone.
```

**Seal it.** Skip any optional piece.

1. Set the container down. "This is the capsule" is a fine start.
2. Both of you write the date, then the rules. "When this date arrives, we open the capsule together. Either of us may ask to open it earlier. Opening early is allowed. If one of us is away, the capsule waits." One blank line is for a different agreement you both want tonight, such as "A phone call counts as together." A rule invented the day someone is missing doesn't count. Clip the slip outside the seal so a later pen can change the date.
3. The shared note, if you want it: a scene, a fact that won't be obvious later, or a line you'd like to hear again. Each of you may add a sentence. Neither rewrites the other's words. Two paragraphs may sit side by side.
4. A photograph or a drawing, if you want one, and one caption line: what it is, today's date, and who may see it. `journal/templates/memento-caption.md` holds the longer version: where the object lives, a local filename, and the lines that you made it, that you have the right to keep a copy, and that you aren't granting anyone else the right to publish it. This book doesn't upload pictures. A drawing is a full stand-in.
5. A key, if you use one, returns to its hook in the house. It means either of you may ask to open early, and it stays out of the seal. The sentence on the slip is the same permission.
6. Name the shelf or drawer. Both of you can walk to it. The capsule stays indoors.
7. Seal what you're keeping. Leave the slip editable. You're done when you both know the date and the place.

On a standard evening, say aloud that there are no letters.

**Open it.** Sit together at a table, with the capsule, the slip, and the key if you use one. Read in this order.

1. The date slip. If you're early, say, "We're opening early, and that's allowed," then keep the same order.
2. The shared note. Either of you may read it aloud. The other may pass, read in silence, or leave it folded.
3. The picture, then the caption. Either of you may pass on talking about it.
4. The letters, writer first. They name keep private, read aloud, or share later. Private stays with the writer. Read-aloud can be withdrawn, and the listener may pass. After a pass, the person the letter is for may read it in silence, or it stays folded. Nobody owes a summary.

Then use `journal/templates/revisit-then-now.md`. Then is the sealing: what you played, what you put in, how it seemed that night. Now is this opening, before you measure it. After that, what changed, a new memory, and what you'd like next. "Almost nothing" counts. Finish another day, or log the date and leave the page.

If one of you is absent and the slip doesn't allow opening alone, put the capsule back. Waiting is the ritual. "We were here and we waited," written on the outside, records the wait. It isn't an opening. Together later, keep the date or write a new one beneath it and leave the old date readable.

You're done when each piece you're willing to open has been read or passed, and the comparison page is started or left for later.

## Three ways to play

**Short, about 20 minutes.** One shared note and a date. Both of you write the date. Write that you open it together, that either of you may ask to open it earlier, and that if one of you is away the capsule waits. Opening early is allowed. Slide the note into an envelope, clip the slip to the outside, and name the drawer. That's a complete capsule. Picture, key, and letters can stay out.

**Standard, about 90 minutes.** The sealing evening above. Date and rules, shared note, a picture and caption if you want them, the key back on its hook, the place said aloud, the seal. Say what is inside so the opening isn't a surprise inventory.

**Extended.** The standard evening, plus a letter from each of you. A paragraph counts, and so does a full page. Use `journal/templates/letter-page.md` if you want the ruled surface: sixteen lines, another sixteen if you continue, and a mark for keep private, read aloud, or share later. Fold the letter. Seal it if you want it sealed. If only one of you writes, the capsule holds one letter. A blank is a complete choice.

All three versions open in the same order.

## Going deeper

Address a letter to the other person, to yourself on the opening day, or to both of you, and put that in the first line. Mark private, read aloud, or share later, even on scrap paper. Leave unwritten any confidence you'd rather say aloud and then let go.

The shared note can be plain: who cooked, what you're in the middle of, a sentence you might want to hear later. On a heavy week, one honest line is enough, and so is something small. A complaint the other person will hear for the first time at the opening stays out.

Close the old note before you write Now, and leave a crossed-out date visible if you moved it. What you'd like next might be another capsule, one revised sentence, or nothing.

To change the date, sit down together and both write the new day. The person home alone leaves the paper alone. Wanting it sooner is a reason to ask for the key and open together. Wanting it later is a reason to move the paper.

If the opening feels thin, let it be thin. If a letter stings, say so in a sentence and leave the rest folded. You can come back, or the writer can take the page back.

## Variations and replay

Three weeks will still surprise you. Eighteen months asks more of the slip, because lives move. One date per capsule. Two horizons means two capsules in the same drawer, each with its own paper.

On a call, each of you writes the date while you're still on the line and reads it back. Seal only what both of you have heard. The capsule lives in one house. Opening on a call counts as together only when the slip says so. Otherwise you wait for a shared room, and you rewrite the date when you're both there.

The first capsule stays until its date, a rewritten date, or an early opening you both accept. Sealing another the same night is a new attempt, and it counts when you both mean to do it.

Large paper helps if reading is hard. If a read-aloud is costly, trade pages and stay quiet. You can seal the short version away from home, then bring the capsule back to your own drawer. A later year gets a fresh then-versus-now sheet. Keep the old pages, and put any correction on a new sheet, dated.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They aren't you. The two sentences in quotation marks were written for this example.

On a Thursday in October the capsule is an envelope from the bill drawer. They both write 14 March, the wait, and the early-open rule, and they leave the alone-opening line blank. The back-door key goes back on its hook. Vale draws the orange kettle and captions it for both of them. Rowan adds one line to the note about late dinners, invented for this example: "The radiator knocks twice before it gives heat, and we wait for the second knock before we start the dishes." Vale adds the drafty window and leaves that sentence alone. No letters. The envelope goes in the battery drawer, slip clipped to the front.

In December Vale picks the key up and puts it back. The almost-opening stays an almost. On 14 March, Vale is still away. Rowan leaves the envelope where it is. Home again, they write 4 April under the old date and both initial the change. March stays legible.

On 4 April they read the slip first, then the note. Vale passes on the weeks away and doesn't owe a substitute story. They laugh at the kettle's spout. Vale's sentence, also invented for this example, follows the radiator line: "It was replaced in January, and I hadn't noticed I missed the knock until this page." Then is the bill drawer and the wait. Now is a quieter kitchen and a moved date. They make tea. The empty envelope stays with the slip.

## Afterward

Stay with one line before you make a theory of the year. The knock, the moved date, a letter someone passed on, an early opening that stayed ordinary. Talk about that if you want to. You can also wash the cups.

The note and the slip can live in Our Story or stay in the envelope. A private letter stays with the writer. A digital picture stays on your machine under the filename on the caption. If the day was flat, write "flat" when that word is accurate. If you waited because one of you was away, record the wait when you're together again. The closed capsule is that session. The opening gets its own lines.

## Our Story

Use one session log for the stretch, from sealing through opening, with the date range in the date field. Mark short, standard, or extended, a mood if you want one, a one-line intention, and initials if you want them. Mark the sealing done or paused. When you open, add a short dated note in the shared pages. Blank days in between stay blank.

Private pages are optional to share. Keep a letter, a caption, or a partner page in your own stack if the shared book should hold only what you both choose. Letters use `journal/templates/letter-page.md`. Captions use `journal/templates/memento-caption.md`. The long comparison uses `journal/templates/revisit-then-now.md`. A repeat gets its own pages. The old ones stay.
