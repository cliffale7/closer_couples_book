## The hook

A long table wants a banquet and an audience. Disappoint it. Two chairs sit at the near end, turned a little toward each other, and the cards run the other way. The near questions are light enough to answer while a kettle boils. They grow more personal with distance. The last card lies face down.

Twelve questions sit in three stretches: near, middle, and far. You play them in order. Each person gets half the time on a card, and a follow-up waits for a spoken yes. Far 4 stays face down until both of you say yes. Either of you may leave the table at any card. Leaving ends the escalation, and the night still counts as finished. If either person says no to the last card, it stays down, and the evening is already complete.

## Why it might matter

A shared dialect is intimate, and it retires questions. The retired one is often the question that would show a region praise does not reach. Being accurately imagined feels different from being told you are easy to know.

John Gottman's clinical writing uses love maps for a partner's inner world and bids for the reaches people make toward each other. A card is a bid in that picture (see GOTTMAN). Neither metaphor grades a pass or predicts your future.

See L1998 in the notes for diary studies of disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness. Those diaries did not test this table. In Study 1, a roughly 45-minute pair of escalating self-disclosure and relationship-building tasks produced greater post-interaction closeness than a small-talk comparison among people who had just met (see A1997). This table is a different procedure, and it does not use that question list. The study was not a recipe for couples falling in love. Arriving at the far end earns nothing.

## Before you begin

Any table works. Distance is the order of the cards. Sit together at the near end with place cards or blank cards, a pen, and a timer you can both see. A phone may keep time if notifications are quiet. If you have lamps, set the brighter one near you and let another go lower toward the far end. Ordinary room light is complete. Darkness is not required. Raise the lamp if the ink is hard to read.

No guests. Use a room with a door when the house is busy. Do not send one person to sit at the far end alone. Eat first if you are hungry. A drink may stay. A served meal makes one of you staff, and that is a different evening.

Name closed subjects before you copy a card. They stay closed, clever rewording included. You may mention an absent person with care. You may not build a case they cannot answer. End the card if an answer turns into that case. A pass needs no substitute confession: no smaller story, no reason, and no replacement question.

Touch is not part of this session. A question answered is not consent to touch or sex, and neither is a laugh, a tear, or a long look. A request for a hand belongs to another night. No is a complete answer there.

Say which version you are playing. If either of you is too altered for a clean no, end before the first card. That night still counts. Writing is a full answer. Stay seated if movement hurts, and let the cards come to you. On a call, cameras stay optional. Each of you keeps the same order.

## The experience

Copy the cards before you sit, or the short version disappears into handwriting. Use index cards, about 3 by 5 inches, or quarter-sheets, with the zone and number in the upper left and about half an inch of margin. They mark questions, not seats. Near cards sit closest to you, middle cards farther off, and Far 1 through Far 3 farther still, face up. Far 4 lies face down at the far place: the number on the back, the question on the front. Three stacks suit a short table. A desk counts.

These questions are printed, so Far 4 may already be known. Knowing the words is not a yes. For an unseen wording, use the substitute in Variations, and do not read that last question until both of you have said yes. There is no score. Share each card, answer or pass, then move. A tame card stays where it is. The ritual is that order, the question "Do we turn it?" and a spoken end.

**Near.** A detail is enough.

Near 1. What trivial rule of your own household, real or imagined, would you enforce with a straight face?

Near 2. What skill did you pick up because the alternative was waiting, and what do you use it for now?

Near 3. Which pleasure in your week is so ordinary you have stopped calling it a pleasure?

Near 4. When something small goes right, what do you do with the pleasure if you are unlikely to make a speech about it?

**Middle.** A live fact, still ordinary enough for a kitchen.

Middle 1. What are you getting better at when nobody is grading it, and do you want that skill to stay unofficial?

Middle 2. Which of your good habits has begun to boss the rest of your life around?

Middle 3. What do you shorten when you tell the story of a hard year, and what does the shorter story protect?

Middle 4. Where do you want me near you, and where does nearness start to crowd the thing you are doing?

**Far.** Ask these because either of you can stop. Far 1 is an impression you allowed, not a list of flaws. End it if it becomes a bill of complaints. Far 2 is not a standard for the listener to repeal tonight. Far 3 asks to be heard. Calming it was not offered. Far 4 is the face-down card.

Far 1. What have you let me misunderstand because the correction felt like more work than the misunderstanding?

Far 2. What standard are you using on yourself this season that you would argue me out of if I used it on you?

Far 3. What do you want me to know about a fear without also taking the job of calming it?

Far 4, placed face down. What do you want this bond to make room for that your competence does not need, and what do you want it to leave alone?

Copy in that order, and leave the last line face down.

The person who did not copy answers first. If you both copied, or neither did, use a coin, then the earlier birthday in the calendar year, then whoever woke earlier. Alternate after every card. A pass does not change the rotation. The person who answers second reads the card once, and does not pick up Far 4 until both of you have said yes.

Each half of the clock belongs to one speaker. Times are below. Silence in a half belongs to the speaker. The listener does not advise, diagnose, or take the half over with a matching story. Four sentences can finish a near card.

One follow-up fits inside the speaker's half, and only after the word yes to "May I ask one follow-up?" It has to use a detail they actually said. Advice, or a story of your own, ends the privilege. When the half ends, finish the sentence. Spare time stays spare. You may add 5 minutes once, split evenly, if both of you agree.

"Pass" ends your half and needs no substitute confession. The other half still belongs to the other person. They do not answer for you. "That's enough of this one" closes the card for both of you, even mid-sentence. The fragment is not an opening.

"I'm leaving the table" ends the escalation before the first card, during a card, or between cards, even while the other person is speaking. They stop and do not owe the rest. The night is finished. Leftover cards, including Far 4, stay unread. Nobody plays them alone or charges a summary for the exit. If one of you says leave and the other hoped for a pause, the leave stands. "Pause" only freezes the row. Starting again needs a fresh yes from both of you at the next unplayed card, and it carries no yes for the far end.

You reach Far 4 only after each earlier card in your version has been answered, passed, or closed, in order. Leaving does not deliver you there. Ask, "Do we turn it?" Both of you have to say the word yes. Silence, a shrug, and "if you want" do not count. If either person says no, the card stays down, and the evening is already complete. Do not swap in another question, read the first line, or turn it later that night because the mood improved. Another day needs a new yes. The person who said no does not owe it.

If you disagree about keeping an unturned card, put a printed question away without discussing an answer. Tear up an unread substitute. If you both want to keep it, use an envelope, write the date on the outside, and write no hint.

Two yeses mean you turn the card and play it under the same rules. Hearing it counts, even if you then pass. You may not turn it and pretend the turn did not happen. There is no card after it. An answer at this end of the table is still not consent to touch or sex.

You are done when the cards in your version are finished and Far 4, if it was set, has been played or left down; or when either of you has left; or when either of you has refused Far 4; or when the clock ends. Finish the sentence you are in. Do not sprint the far cards. If you reach Far 4 with less than 10 minutes left, it stays down. Say, "The night is complete."

## Three ways to play

The short version takes about 45 minutes. Set Near 1 through Near 4 only, and say, "The far place is not set." Use about 5 minutes to lay the cards, 8 minutes for each card (4 minutes each), and about 8 minutes to sit before you get up. If the clock ends mid-sentence, finish the sentence. Do not promote a middle card into a leftover minute. Ending at the near end is a finished night.

The standard version takes about two hours. Play the four near cards, the four middle cards, and Far 1 through Far 3, then ask the question at Far 4. Setup is 10 minutes. Each near card is 6 minutes, 3 minutes each. Each middle card is 10 minutes, 5 minutes each. Each of Far 1 through Far 3 is 12 minutes, 6 minutes each. Far 4 gets 10 minutes, the decision included. If those 10 minutes are too small after you turn it, pass, or both agree in words to finish on the extended clock. One person's wish does not lengthen the night.

The extended version takes about three hours, with the same twelve cards and the same stops. Near cards stay at 6 minutes each. On every middle card, and on Far 1 through Far 3, write together for 4 minutes, then speak for 10 minutes on a middle card or 12 minutes on a far card, split evenly. Unread lines stay yours. Take a break of about 15 minutes between middle and far: tea or quiet, with the cards and touch left out of it. Leaving on the break finishes the night. If you turn Far 4, give it up to 20 minutes. Setup and landing are about 10 minutes each. If you skip the writing, end in the quiet instead of inventing a card.

## Going deeper

After a middle or far half, the listener may say, "The sentence I heard was," if the speaker wants the check. The speaker edits it, or says, "Leave it." Accuracy is the courtesy. The speaker may also mark a line "remember" or "tonight." A tonight-line stays off the shared page, and it does not come back later as ammunition. A private note can record a pass without the question.

A week later, replay one named card if both of you still want that card. Far 4 needs a new yes even if it stayed down last time. An old no is neither a ban nor a debt. Going deeper does not reopen a pass or a closed subject. If the speaker wants to replace a polite answer, they do it inside their own half. The listener does not dig.

## Variations and replay

Three stacks on a short table are a complete long table. So is this page, if you cover every line you are not playing, and keep the last far line covered until both of you say yes.

For an unseen Far 4, write one substitute before you sit: personal, answerable in a few minutes, with no request for touch or sex, and a question the writer will also answer. The other person may refuse it and keep the printed card face down. If you both write one, put them in an envelope, draw one unseen, and return the other unread. Tear up any card someone sees. If no unseen substitute is left, use the printed Far 4. Before you sit, also tear up a substitute that circles a closed subject or asks for a sexual disclosure.

On a call, one person holds Far 4 and reads it only after two spoken yeses. A bad connection is a pause. Do not finish a cut-off sentence for the other person.

Near and middle can be one night, and the far cards another. The first night is already complete. The second starts with a check, and either of you may cancel. Keep a heavy week on the short version. Morning is a fair hour. Do not save the far cards for a tired night. A tired yes is a poor yes.

New cards stay in register. Near can be answered beside a kettle. Middle asks a live fact. Far includes a limit on the listener's job, because stopping is what makes it askable. Tear up a trap. Retire a card that worked only by startling. Keep the order, the halves, the pass, the leaving, and the face-down rule. If you think on paper, write inside the shorter clock and speak two sentences. Stretching is not leaving. Say it when you mean it.

## Examples

Rowan and Vale are fictional. They are not you. They are here so the rules have a shape.

They use a kitchen table and leave the ceiling light on. Rowan copies the cards, sets Far 4 face down, and admits the printed question is already known. Vale says, "Then we know the words. We still both have to say yes."

On Near 1, Vale's straight-faced rule is that leftover noodles belong to the cook, even when the cook is full. Rowan waits out an urge to spend that half on a counter-rule about forks, then offers a half-joke: shoes live by the door. They let the last minute finish.

On Middle 3, Vale says "Pass" when a shortened story about a hard year reaches a colleague who is already off the table. Rowan does not ask for another story. In Rowan's half, the shortened story of changing cities left out a good year of work and a bad habit of coming home already arguing. Vale asks, "May I ask one follow-up?" Rowan says yes. Vale asks what the arguing sounded like, then says, "The sentence I heard was: you liked the work, and the temper belonged to the trip home." Rowan edits it. "The temper belonged to being talked out. The trip was where it showed."

Rowan nearly leaves before Far 1. Vale says leaving would end the night and still count. Rowan stays for one correction: the bright question "Are you fine?" lands on days when quiet would be better. Vale does not defend it. The face-up far cards still get played. At Far 4, Vale asks, "Do we turn it?" Rowan says, "No." The card goes into a dated envelope with no hint. They wash up. They do not touch.

On another fictional night, Vale says, "I'm leaving the table," at Middle 2. The remaining cards stay unread. They make toast. That night is finished too.

## Afterward

If you want words, take one sentence each: what you want kept. It is not another question. You can also wash the cups and say nothing.

If a card produced a practical decision, name it and give it a daylight hour, unless waiting would leave someone unsafe. If the night stirred you, choose quiet, a walk alone, or the same room, without touch. If Far 4 stayed down, leave it down. One accurate detail is enough to carry. The sentence they edited will outlast a portrait of who they really are.

## Our Story

Write the date, the time, the place, which version you played, a mood if you want one, a one-line intention, and your initials if you want them. Mark the session done or paused. Partner A and Partner B are pages, not roles. Trade them if you like. Sharing either private page is optional. Keep yours aside if the shared book should hold only what you both choose. A pass can be noted without the question. A repeated sitting gets its own lines.
